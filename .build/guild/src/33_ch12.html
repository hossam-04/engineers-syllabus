Chapter 12

Mentoring

{{RT}} min read{{EX}} exercises

Mentoring is the most leveraged work a senior engineer does, and it's a skill, not a personality trait. Start from what the other person actually knows, which is often less solid than what they've used. Teach at the edge of that knowledge, in an order where each idea rests on ones already secure. Give feedback that's specific enough to act on. And sponsor as well as advise: use your standing to give people the opportunities that make them grow.

Why mentor deliberately

An engineer who makes three colleagues 20% more effective has added more than most engineers add by their own work, and the effect compounds: those colleagues mentor others. That's the leverage the syllabus means by "the ability to move other engineers". There's a second reason, which the syllabus also names: "teaching is the fastest way to find the holes in your own model". Explaining something to a sharp junior engineer, who asks why at each step, exposes every place where your understanding is a memorised rule rather than a reason. Richard Feynman is often credited with the test: if you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough.

Most mentoring happens by accident: a junior engineer absorbs what they can from code reviews and overheard conversations. Deliberate mentoring does better, because it starts from a diagnosis, follows a plan, and checks whether the plan is working.

Mentor, coach, sponsor

Lara Hogan, writing about engineering management, distinguishes three roles that are often blurred. A mentor gives advice from experience: "here's how I'd approach this". A coach asks questions that help the other person find their own answer: "what have you tried, and what would you try next?" A sponsor uses their standing to create opportunities: recommending someone for a stretch project, naming them in a meeting they're not in, putting their work in front of people who decide promotions. Each has its place. Advice is fastest for facts and conventions; coaching builds judgement, which can't be handed over; and sponsorship is what changes careers, especially for people whose work tends to be overlooked. A good mentor moves between all three and knows which one the moment calls for.

Start from what they really know

People describe what they know by what they've used. A mentee who says they know query planning may mean they've read EXPLAIN output and added indexes when told to. That's real, useful knowledge, and it may rest on nothing: they may not know what a B-tree is, how a page is read from disk, or where the planner's row estimates come from. Knowledge like that is fragile. It works for the cases they've seen and fails, confusingly, on the first case that depends on the missing foundation.

So start with a diagnosis, done kindly: ask them to explain, not to recall. "Walk me through what happens when this query runs" reveals far more than "do you know indexes?". Then map what you learn against what each idea builds on. The example does this for a mentee learning database internals: a small graph of concepts and what each needs, the four the mentee says they know, and three questions. What is fragile, what are they ready to learn now, and in what order should they learn their way to crash recovery?

What the example shows

Three of the four things they know are fragile. Indexes rest on B-trees, pages and the buffer pool, and files and fsync, none of which they know; query plans rest on those and on statistics; replication rests on the write-ahead log. Only transactions stand on their own. That's typical of engineers who learned on the job: competent at the top of the stack, with gaps underneath. It isn't a criticism; it's a map of where their next bug will confuse them.

The frontier is at the bottom. The concepts they're ready to learn now, everything they need already known, are files and fsync, and statistics. Teaching anything else first would mean explaining an idea in terms of others they don't have, which is how explanations lose people. The path to their goal, crash recovery, is four steps, and starts with files and fsync. The example's last line is the practical advice: before teaching anything on the path, ask them to explain its first step. If they can, you're further along than the map says; if they can't, you've found the place to begin.

Teaching at the edge

Lev Vygotsky called it the zone of proximal development: what a learner can't yet do alone but can do with help. Teaching works there and nowhere else. Below it, they're bored; above it, they're lost. The example's frontier is that zone, computed. In practice, three techniques work well. Worked examples, then faded guidance: show a complete solution, then give a similar problem with part of the solution, then one with none. Pairing, then reversing: you drive while they watch and ask; then they drive while you watch and ask. Questions before answers: when they bring a problem, ask what they've tried and what they think is happening before saying what you think. The answer they reach themselves is the one they'll remember, and the question shows them how you think, which is what they most need to learn.

Code review is the mentoring most engineers already do, and it's easy to do it badly. Explain why, not just what: "this query runs once per order, so a page of 100 orders makes 101 queries" teaches; "use select_related" doesn't. Separate what must change from suggestions and nitpicks, and say which is which. And comment on what's good, specifically, because people repeat what's noticed.

Feedback that helps

Feedback is useful when it's specific, timely, about behaviour rather than personality, and private if it's critical. The situation–behaviour–impact model, from the Center for Creative Leadership, gives it a shape: describe the situation, the specific behaviour, and its impact. "In yesterday's incident, you posted updates in the channel every ten minutes, and support could answer customers without asking us" is feedback someone can repeat. "Great job in the incident" is a pleasantry. The same shape works for criticism, and keeps it about something the person can change.

Sponsorship

Advice helps people grow; opportunities make them grow faster. Give your mentee the stretch work: the design document for the next project, the lead on a small incident review, the talk at the team meeting. Back them while they do it, and let them get the credit, in public. Recommend them for things when they're not in the room, by name. And give away the work you'd enjoy doing yourself, because the interesting work is exactly what someone growing needs. The sign that mentoring has worked isn't that they ask you fewer questions; it's that they start answering other people's.

Common traps
  • Taking "I know it" at face value, instead of asking for an explanation.
  • Teaching above the frontier, in terms of ideas the learner doesn't have yet.
  • Answering every question at once, so the learner never builds their own judgement.
  • Feedback too vague to act on, praise or criticism without a specific behaviour.
  • Mentoring without sponsoring, advice in private and no opportunities in public.
Exercises
Exercise 12.1A diagnostic question

Write one question that would reveal whether someone's knowledge of indexes is solid or fragile, and say what a fragile answer sounds like.

Show solution

"We have an index on (customer_id, created_at). Which of these two queries can use it well, and why: one filtering on customer_id, one filtering on created_at?" A solid answer explains that the index is a B-tree sorted by customer_id first, so the first query can descend straight to one customer's entries, while the second has no efficient way in, because rows for a given date are scattered across every customer. A fragile answer gets the right result by rule, "the leftmost column must be in the filter", but can't explain it, or gets it wrong, "both, because both columns are indexed". The question works because it can't be answered from a remembered rule alone; it needs the structure underneath, which is exactly what the map said might be missing.

Exercise 12.2Feedback with a shape

Rewrite these two pieces of feedback using situation, behaviour and impact: "Your design doc was really good" and "You need to communicate better".

Show solution

"In your search design document last week, you priced the managed service and tested trigram indexes before proposing the new index. When the director asked why not the cheaper option, the answer was already in the document, and the review took twenty minutes instead of a second meeting." And: "In the refunds project, the bank's sandbox was blocked for six days before the team heard about it, at the stand-up on Thursday. We lost a week we could have used for the fallback. Next time something blocks you for more than a day, could you post it in the channel that day?" Both name a situation and a behaviour the person can repeat or change, and say why it mattered. The second also ends with a specific request, which turns criticism into something the person can act on tomorrow.

Exercise 12.3Six weeks to crash recovery

Using the example's path, plan six weeks of mentoring, an hour a week, to get the mentee to explain crash recovery.

Show solution

Week 1: files and fsync. Have them write a small program that writes a file, kills itself before and after fsync, and checks what survived; ask them to explain the results. Week 2: pages and the buffer pool. Read the relevant chapter of Database Internals together, then ask them to explain why a database reads 8 KB when you ask for one row. Week 3: the write-ahead log. Give them the rule, log before data, and ask them to argue why it's necessary, before showing the answer. Week 4: crash recovery. Walk through a crash with a half-written page and a log, and have them replay the log by hand. Week 5: reverse roles. They explain crash recovery to you, or better, to another junior engineer, while you only ask questions. Week 6: revisit the fragile concepts that now have foundations, replication on top of the log, and ask them to explain why a replica that applies the log in order converges. Each week starts with a question, ends with them explaining, and moves only when the step is secure.

Checklist
  • Do you diagnose what someone knows by asking them to explain, not to recall?
  • Can you find their frontier, and teach in an order that never skips a foundation?
  • Do you ask questions before giving answers?
  • Is your feedback specific: a situation, a behaviour and its impact?
  • Do you sponsor as well as mentor: stretch work, public credit, recommendations?
