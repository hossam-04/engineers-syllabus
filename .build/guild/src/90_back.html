The deliverable

The stage asks for "a body of work rather than a project": a maintained open-source contribution, a written archive others cite, and at least one system in production that other engineers depend on and that you didn't build alone. None of the three can be finished in a month, and they aren't meant to be. They're what years of the practices in this volume leave behind, and the lists below describe each when it's done. Start all three now, small, and review them every six months against these lists.

1. A maintained open-source contribution

Not a single patch, but a sustained part of a project that other people use. Chapter 5 is the model.

  1. One project you depend on, where you've contributed over at least a year: fixes, features, documentation and, above all, reviews of other people's work.
  2. A part of it you know well enough that maintainers ask for your review there, or give you the permission to approve changes.
  3. Issues triaged, questions answered and newcomers helped, visibly and kindly, in the project's own channels.
  4. Your contributions in your own words: a short page listing what you did, linked from your archive or CV, with the pull requests and reviews that show it.

2. A written archive others cite

Years of steady writing in your area, until other people link to it when they explain the subject. Chapter 8 is the model.

  1. A site you own, with at least two dozen posts in your area, published on a regular rhythm.
  2. A mix of hard lessons, explanations, reading notes and small tools, each with a specific title and its point in the first sentence.
  3. At least one talk, at a meetup, a conference or your company's engineering forum, made from posts that were well received.
  4. Evidence that it's useful to others: links from other people's writing, questions from readers, a post that keeps being found by search.

3. A system others depend on

Something in production, built with other people, that other engineers rely on and that kept working. Parts III and IV are the model.

  1. A design document that survived review, with alternatives, risks and a rollout, and the decision records that followed it.
  2. A project led end to end: scoped, sequenced risk first, forecast honestly, communicated to its stakeholders, and landed, with the old path removed.
  3. Engineers outside your team who depend on it, and documentation, runbooks and alerts good enough that they rarely need you.
  4. Its delivery and reliability measured, and at least one incident learned from in a blameless postmortem whose actions were done.
  5. Other people who could run it without you, because you taught them, which is the last test of having built something that lasts.

Keep the work log from chapter 16 throughout. In five years it's the record of how the body of work was built, and the evidence, for yourself as much as anyone, that it was.

The sixteen rules

One per chapter, each linked back to it. Read them when choosing what to work on, and again when a project, a review or a conversation goes badly.

  1. Choose an area to go deep in, write down why, and check the judgement your choice depends on.
  2. Read papers in passes, with a question, and build the central idea small enough to run.
  3. Find the assumption each design depends on; that's where it fails.
  4. Map a codebase before reading it, trace what actually runs, then fix something.
  5. Send patches that cost little to accept: small, tested, explained, in the project's style.
  6. Propose in the first paragraph, put numbers on goals, and show the alternatives you rejected.
  7. Write uncertain inputs as ranges, and argue about the assumption that decides it.
  8. Lead with the point, replace adjectives with facts, and publish where you own the archive.
  9. Estimate in ranges along the real dependencies, and commit at P85.
  10. Sequence work so the questions that could change the plan are answered first.
  11. Bottom line first, colour from the forecast, and every update ends with an ask and a date.
  12. Diagnose before teaching, teach at the frontier, and sponsor as well as advise.
  13. Price work in the business's units, with each assumption beside its number.
  14. Measure speed and stability together, for teams, to learn rather than to judge.
  15. Limit work in progress, finish before starting, and renegotiate commitments early.
  16. Write down what you did every week, work on important problems, and keep a pace that lasts.

Reading list

The books, papers and essays behind this volume, and the chapters each one deepens. If you read only two books, make them Tanya Reilly's The Staff Engineer's Path, the most useful book about senior technical work, and Fred Brooks's The Mythical Man-Month, fifty years old and still describing your project. For papers, start with the seven in chapter 3, in order.

Compiled October 2026 as the companion to stage 15, Mastery, of The Engineer's Syllabus, alongside The Engineer's Whetstone, Primer, Workbench, Compass, Mill, Loom, Exchange, Press, Ledger, Craft, Shipyard, Fleet, Watch, Citadel and Foundry, which completes the series: every stage now has a volume. It follows the practices the stage lists, and draws on the works in the reading list and on the original sources for its named results. These are Little (1961) on queues; Brooks (1975) on adding people to late projects; Kahneman and Tversky (1979) on the planning fallacy; Hamming (1986) on important problems; the Google File System (2003), MapReduce (2004), Bigtable and Chubby (2006), Dynamo (2007), Spanner (2012) and Raft (2014); Keshav (2007) on reading papers; Klein (2007) on pre-mortems; and the DORA research summarised in Accelerate (2018). The examples' numbers are invented, as each chapter says, so they show the shape of each argument rather than measure any real team.

Every example was executed before publication with Python 3.14.7 on macOS 26, using only the standard library, and the output printed under each one is what it printed. They're written for Python 3.12 or later and to print the same output on Linux: random choices use fixed seeds, Git runs with fixed names and dates and ignores your own settings, and anything else that varies between machines is kept out of the output. Chapter 4's map and trace describe the json package as shipped with Python 3.14, and change slightly between versions. Each example was also run a second time in a deliberately hostile environment, with a different time zone, temporary folder and home folder with its own Git settings, and printed exactly the same. Tested here: a decision matrix and its fragility; MapReduce with a combiner, re-execution and backup tasks; Spanner's commit wait against skewed clocks; a standard-library package mapped and traced with its accelerator on and off; two patches reviewed with git am; a design-document reviewer; a three-year cost comparison over twenty thousand futures; a prose checker; a task graph simulated for its percentiles and critical path; three sequencings of a risky project; a status update computed from a forecast; a prerequisite graph for mentoring; unit economics and a ranking by cost of delay; the four DORA keys from a Git history; Little's law under two policies; and a work log summarised.
