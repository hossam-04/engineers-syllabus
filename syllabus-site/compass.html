The one habit

Every result in this volume is met twice: once as a proof, which says why it holds in every case, and once as a program, which checks it in the cases you can run.

Neither replaces the other. A program can check a million cases and miss the next one, as chapter 2's counterexamples show, and a proof can contain a mistake that one quick test would expose. So prove it, then run it, and when the two disagree, find out which one is wrong. That habit is most of what mathematics does for a programmer.

Chapter 01

Logic: saying exactly what you mean

10 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

Logic is the grammar of precise statements. You already use it in every if; this chapter makes it exact. It covers what "implies" really means, including why a false premise makes an implication true, which rewritings of a statement are safe and which only look safe, and how to negate a statement full of "for all" and "there exists". For a handful of propositions a truth table settles any question mechanically. The hard part is translating English into logic correctly.

Propositions and connectives

A proposition is a statement that is either true or false: "7 is prime", "the build passed", "x > 3" once x has a value. Propositions combine with a few connectives, each defined by a truth table: not (¬p), and (p∧q), or (p∨q), exclusive or (p⊕q), implies (p→q) and if and only if (p↔q). Most map straight onto code: not, and, or, and != on booleans for exclusive or. Mathematical "or" is always inclusive. "p or q" is true when both are true, unlike the "or" of a restaurant menu.

Two formulas are equivalent if they have the same truth value in every row of the truth table, that is, for every assignment of true and false to their variables. A formula that is true in every row is valid, or a tautology. One that is true in at least one row is satisfiable. With n variables there are 2n rows, so a truth table settles any question about a few variables and becomes useless at a few dozen. Deciding satisfiability in general is the SAT problem, the first problem proved NP-complete, which The Engineer's Workbench covers in its last chapter.

Implication, the connective everyone gets wrong

p→q, read "if p then q" or "p implies q", is false in exactly one case: p is true and q is false. In every other row it's true, including both rows where p is false. That surprises people, but it's the only definition that works. "If a number is divisible by 4, it's even" should be true as a statement about all numbers, so it has to be true for 3 (false premise, false conclusion) and for 6 (false premise, true conclusion) as well as for 8. An implication with a false premise is vacuously true. Python follows the same rule: all([]) is True, because a claim about every item of an empty list has no item that could break it.

English hides implications in several phrasings. "p only if q" means p→q: q is necessary for p. "p if q" means q→p: q is sufficient for p. "p unless q" means ¬q→p. Every implication has three relatives, made by swapping its sides, negating both, or both at once.

The contrapositive is the most useful rewriting in mathematics, because proving ¬q→¬p is often easier than proving p→q directly (chapter 2). The converse is the most common error in reasoning: from "if the server is overloaded, requests time out" you can't conclude "requests are timing out, so the server is overloaded".

Checking equivalences by machine

With a few variables you never need to argue about an equivalence. Enumerate every row and compare. The example does that for the four conditionals, De Morgan's laws, and two rewritings of nested implications, then evaluates some quantified statements over a small domain, where "for all" is a loop with all and "there exists" is a loop with any.

The contrapositive matches in all four rows. The converse and the inverse each fail at p = 0, q = 1, where the implication is vacuously true and they're false, but they match each other, as the figure says. The negation of an implication, ¬(p→q)≡p∧¬q, is worth memorising: an implication fails only when its premise holds and its conclusion doesn't, which is exactly what a counterexample is. Implication isn't associative. (p→q)→r and p→(q→r) differ when everything is false. The second form equals (p∧q)→r, which is why a chain of conditions in code can be written either as nested ifs or as one if with and.

Predicates and quantifiers

A predicate is a statement with variables, such as P(x, y): "x + y = 9". It becomes a proposition when you fix the variables or quantify them: ∀x, "for all x", or ∃y, "there exists a y". Quantifiers always range over a domain, and the domain changes the truth value. "For every x there's a larger y" is true over the integers and false over 0..9, where nothing is larger than 9, as the example's third line shows.

The order of mixed quantifiers matters. ∀x∃y:x+y=9 says every x has its own partner, which is true. ∃y∀x:x+y=9 says one y works for every x at once, which is false. Swapping ∀ and ∃ changes the claim. Specifications of systems are full of this distinction: "every request is served by some replica" is not "some replica serves every request".

To negate a quantified statement, push the negation inward and flip each quantifier as it passes:

¬∀x∃yP(x,y)≡∃x∀y¬P(x,y)

This is De Morgan's law for quantifiers, since "for all" is a big "and" and "there exists" is a big "or". The example checks one instance: it's false that every x in 0..9 has a y with xy mod 7 = 1, and correspondingly there's an x, namely 0 (or 7), for which every y fails. Negation is how you find out what would disprove a claim. The negation of the service-level objective "every request gets a response within one second" is "some request doesn't get a response within one second", and that single request is what your monitoring has to catch.

Common traps
  • Affirming the consequent: concluding p from p→q and q. That's the converse error.
  • Negating "all" as "none". The negation of "all tests pass" is "some test fails", not "no test passes".
  • Swapping quantifiers and turning "everyone has someone" into "someone serves everyone".
  • Forgetting vacuous truth in code: all() of an empty list is True, so a check over zero items always passes.
  • Reading "or" as exclusive when the mathematics, and the programming language, mean inclusive.
  • Ignoring the domain: a quantified claim that is true over the integers can be false over 32-bit integers.
Exercises
Exercise 1.1Refunds and receipts

A shop's policy says: "You can't get a refund unless you have a receipt." Let r mean "you get a refund" and c mean "you have a receipt". Write the policy as an implication, then state its contrapositive and its converse in English. Which of them does the policy promise?

Show solution

"Not r unless c" means ¬c→¬r: without a receipt, no refund. Its contrapositive is r→c: "if you got a refund, you had a receipt", which says the same thing, so the receipt is necessary. The converse of r→c is c→r: "if you have a receipt, you get a refund". The policy doesn't promise that. Having a receipt is necessary, not sufficient, and a shop could still refuse a refund to someone holding one.

Exercise 1.2Negating a limit

A sequence an converges to L if ∀ε>0∃N∀n>N:|an−L|<ε. Write the negation, "an does not converge to L", with no ¬ left in front of a quantifier, and say in words what it means.

Show solution

Push the negation in, flipping each quantifier and negating the inequality at the end:

∃ε>0∀N∃n>N:|an−L|≥ε

In words: there's some fixed distance ε such that, however far along the sequence you go, there's always a later term at least ε away from L. For example, an=(−1)n doesn't converge to 1: take ε=1, and every odd n beyond any N gives |an−1|=2. Note that the condition ε>0 is not negated. It's part of the domain the quantifier ranges over, not part of the statement.

Exercise 1.3Everything from NAND

NAND is defined by p↑q=¬(p∧q). Express ¬p, p∧q and p∨q using only NAND, and check each with a truth table. Why does this matter to hardware designers?

Show solution

¬p=p↑p, since ¬(p∧p)=¬p. Then p∧q=¬(p↑q)=(p↑q)↑(p↑q). By De Morgan, p∨q=¬(¬p∧¬q)=(p↑p)↑(q↑q). Each can be checked in four rows, as in the example. Since not, and and or can express every truth table (write one "and" term for each true row and "or" them together), NAND alone can express every boolean function: it's functionally complete. That's why chips can be built from one kind of gate. NOR is functionally complete too; and, or and not on their own each are not.

Checklist
  • Can you write the truth table of p→q and explain the two vacuous rows?
  • Can you tell an implication's converse, inverse and contrapositive apart, and say which is equivalent to it?
  • Can you translate "only if", "if", "unless", "necessary" and "sufficient" into logic?
  • Can you negate a statement with nested quantifiers mechanically?
  • Do you check the domain before believing a quantified claim?

Chapter 02

Proof techniques, and why examples aren't proofs

10 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

A proof is an argument that leaves no case unchecked. A few shapes cover most of the proofs an engineer will ever read or write: direct proof, proof by contrapositive, proof by contradiction, proof by cases, and proofs that something exists. Learning the shapes makes proofs readable. Learning why a thousand confirming examples prove nothing makes them necessary, and this chapter runs famous conjectures that held for every case anyone checked, until they didn't.

What a proof is

A proof starts from definitions and facts already established and reaches a conclusion by steps that each follow by logic alone. Definitions do most of the work, so they have to be exact. An integer n is even if n = 2k for some integer k, and odd if n = 2k + 1 for some integer k. A real number is rational if it equals p/q for integers p and q with q ≠ 0. Almost every first proof consists of unpacking a definition, doing algebra, and packing the result back into a definition.

Direct proof

To prove P→Q directly, assume P and derive Q. Claim: if n is odd, then n2 is odd. Proof: since n is odd, n = 2k + 1 for some integer k. Then

n2=4k2+4k+1=2(2k2+2k)+1,

which is twice an integer plus one, so n2 is odd. ∎ That's the whole pattern: definition in, algebra, definition out. The square at the end marks where the proof stops.

Contrapositive and contradiction

Claim: if n2 is even, then n is even. A direct proof stalls: knowing n2 = 2k says nothing obvious about n. The contrapositive, "if n is odd, then n2 is odd", is exactly what we just proved, and chapter 1 showed that an implication and its contrapositive are equivalent. Done.

Proof by contradiction assumes the statement is false and derives something impossible. The classic example is that 2 is irrational. Suppose it were rational, 2=p/q with the fraction in lowest terms. Squaring gives p2=2q2, so p2 is even, so p is even by the claim above: p = 2m. Then 4m2=2q2, so q2=2m2 and q is even too. Both even contradicts lowest terms, so no such fraction exists. ∎

Euclid's proof that there are infinitely many primes has the same shape. Suppose p1,p2,…,pk were all the primes, and let N=p1p2⋯pk+1. Dividing N by any pi leaves remainder 1, so no pi divides N. But N > 1 has some prime factor (chapter 3 proves that every integer above 1 does), and it isn't on the list. So the list wasn't complete. ∎ Note that N itself needn't be prime. 2 × 3 × 5 × 7 × 11 × 13 + 1 = 30,031 = 59 × 509. The proof only needs N to have a prime factor that's missing from the list.

Cases and existence

Proof by cases splits the situation into possibilities that together cover everything, and proves the claim in each. To show that n2 + n is always even: if n is even, both terms are even; if n is odd, both terms are odd and their sum is even. Every integer is even or odd, so the claim holds. The danger in a case split is a missing case, which is why "without loss of generality" deserves a second look whenever you see it.

To prove that something exists, the most convincing way is to construct it. A non-constructive proof shows existence without producing the object. Here's a famous one: there are irrational numbers a and b with ab rational. Consider 22. If it's rational, take a=b=2. If it's irrational, take a=22 and b=2, since ab=22=2. Either way such a pair exists, and the proof never tells you which case holds. To disprove a universal claim, one counterexample is enough, and finding counterexamples is where a computer earns its keep.

Examples aren't proofs

The example tests five claims that each held for every case their first observers checked. It uses nothing more sophisticated than trial division.

Euler's polynomial n2 + n + 41 produces primes for the forty values 0 to 39, then fails at n = 40, where it gives 412. Fermat believed every number of the form 22k + 1 was prime. The first five are, and in 1732 Euler found that the sixth, 4,294,967,297, is 641 × 6,700,417. No Fermat number beyond the fifth has ever been found to be prime. Goldbach suggested to Euler that every odd composite number is a prime plus twice a square, which holds for every odd composite up to 5,775 and then fails at 5,777 and 5,993. Below 100,000 the example finds no other failures, which is also not a proof that there are none. And 2p − 1 for prime p, the form of every Mersenne prime, is prime for the first four primes and fails at p = 11.

The last line shows the opposite situation, a search that agrees with a proof. The best fractions for 2 come ever closer, but p2−2q2 alternates between −1 and +1 and never reaches 0, as it would have to for p/q=2. The proof above explains why it never will. The search alone, however long it ran, could never say so.

Programs are the same. Testing a function on a thousand inputs shows that it works on those thousand; a proof of a loop invariant (chapter 3) shows that it works on all of them. Tests are how you find the counterexample when the "proof" in your head was wrong, so you need both.

Common traps
  • Assuming what you're trying to prove, often by starting from the conclusion and working "backwards" without checking that each step reverses.
  • Proving the converse instead of the claim.
  • Dividing by something that might be zero, the usual hidden flaw in "proofs" that 1 = 2.
  • A case split with a missing case, especially behind "without loss of generality".
  • Treating many confirming examples as a proof, or one confirming example as evidence for a "for all" claim.
  • Contradiction where a direct proof would do. If the contradiction you reach is just the negation of your assumption, you've written a direct or contrapositive proof in disguise.
Exercises
Exercise 2.1Rational plus irrational

Prove that if r is rational and x is irrational, then r + x is irrational. Which technique fits, and why?

Show solution

Contradiction fits, because "irrational" means "not rational", a statement that something doesn't exist, which is hard to use directly. Suppose r + x were rational, say r + x = a/b, with r = c/d (b, d ≠ 0). Then

x=ab−cd=ad−bcbd,

a ratio of integers with nonzero denominator, so x is rational, contradicting the assumption. ∎ The same argument does not show that the sum of two irrationals is irrational, and that claim is false: 2+(−2)=0.

Exercise 2.2Find the flaw

"Let a = b. Then a2 = ab, so a2 − b2 = ab − b2, so (a + b)(a − b) = b(a − b), so a + b = b, so 2b = b, so 2 = 1." Which step is wrong, and what general rule does it break?

Show solution

The step from (a + b)(a − b) = b(a − b) to a + b = b divides both sides by a − b, which is 0 because a = b. Cancelling a factor is only valid when the factor is nonzero: from xz = yz you may conclude x = y only if z ≠ 0. The last step, from 2b = b to 2 = 1, makes the same mistake again when b = 0. In code, the same bug is a division whose denominator can be zero on some path, which a proof forces you to rule out explicitly.

Exercise 2.3Infinitely many primes of the form 4k + 3

Adapt Euclid's argument to prove that there are infinitely many primes that leave remainder 3 when divided by 4. You may use the fact that a product of numbers that are each 1 more than a multiple of 4 is also 1 more than a multiple of 4.

Show solution

Suppose p1,…,pk were all the primes of the form 4k + 3, and let N=4p1p2⋯pk−1. N is odd and leaves remainder 3 when divided by 4. Its prime factors are all odd, so each has remainder 1 or 3. If every prime factor had remainder 1, then by the given fact N would have remainder 1, which it doesn't. So some prime factor q of N has remainder 3. But q isn't any pi: each pi divides 4p1⋯pk, so dividing N by it leaves remainder pi−1, not 0. So q is a prime of the form 4k + 3 missing from the list, a contradiction. ∎ The same trick fails for primes of the form 4k + 1, because a product of numbers with remainder 3 can have remainder 1. That case needs a different proof.

Checklist
  • Can you write a direct proof that starts from a definition and ends at one?
  • Do you know when to switch to the contrapositive, and when to use contradiction?
  • Can you reproduce the proofs that 2 is irrational and that there are infinitely many primes?
  • Can you check a case split for a missing case?
  • Do you search for counterexamples before trying to prove a conjecture?

Chapter 03

Induction and invariants

11 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

Induction proves a statement for every natural number by proving it for the first one and showing that each case carries over to the next. It's recursion read as a proof, which is why the syllabus calls it the same muscle. Invariants apply the same idea to programs and machines: a property that holds at the start and survives every step holds forever. That's how you prove a loop correct, and how you prove that some states can never be reached at all.

The principle

Let P(n) be a statement about a natural number n. If P(0) is true (the base case), and P(n) implies P(n + 1) for every n (the inductive step), then P(n) is true for every n. Each case knocks over the next, like dominoes. The assumption P(n) inside the step is the inductive hypothesis, and the whole skill lies in using it.

Claim: for every n ≥ 1,

∑i=1ni=n(n+1)2.

Proof. Base case: for n = 1 both sides equal 1. Inductive step: assume the formula holds for n. Then

∑i=1n+1i=n(n+1)2⏟hypothesis+(n+1)=(n+1)(n+2)2,

which is the formula for n + 1. ∎ Notice what induction does and doesn't give you. It verifies a formula you already have, but it doesn't find one. Guessing comes first, often from computing small cases, and chapter 11 has methods for finding closed forms directly.

Induction is recursion

A recursive function and an induction proof have the same skeleton. The base case of the recursion is the base case of the proof. The recursive call on a smaller input is the inductive hypothesis, and the work done after the call returns is the inductive step. To prove a recursive function correct, you prove "it returns the right answer for every input of size n" by induction on n, assuming the recursive calls return the right answers because they're on smaller inputs. If you can't write that proof, the function usually has a bug, most often a missing or wrong base case.

Strong induction and well-ordering

Sometimes P(n + 1) depends on cases further back than P(n). Strong induction lets the step assume P(k) for every k up to n. It proves no more than ordinary induction, but it's often far more convenient. Claim: every integer n ≥ 2 is a product of primes. Proof. If n is prime, it's a product of one prime. Otherwise n = ab with 2 ≤ a, b < n, and by the strong hypothesis both a and b are products of primes, so n is too. ∎ This is the step chapter 2's proof that there are infinitely many primes relied on, and the example runs it as a recursive function.

A variant: every amount of postage from 12 cents up can be made from 4-cent and 5-cent stamps. Check the four base cases 12, 13, 14 and 15 directly. For any larger amount n, the amount n − 4 is at least 12, so by the strong hypothesis it can be made, and one more 4-cent stamp makes n. The step reaches back four cases, which is why it needs four base cases.

An equivalent principle is well-ordering: every nonempty set of natural numbers has a smallest element. It gives proofs by smallest counterexample. If the claim failed somewhere, there would be a smallest n where it fails; you then show that n's failure forces a failure at some smaller number, which is impossible.

Induction, run

The example checks two sum formulas on a thousand cases, runs the factorisation and postage proofs as recursive functions, asserts a loop invariant on every iteration of fast exponentiation, and then explores every reachable position of the 8-puzzle to test an invariant of a state machine.

Invariants

An invariant is a property that holds initially and is preserved by every step. By induction on the number of steps, it then holds after any number of steps. This is the main tool for proving programs correct, an idea due to Robert Floyd and Tony Hoare in the late 1960s. For a loop, you show three things: the invariant holds before the first iteration; if it holds before an iteration, it holds after it; and when the loop exits, the invariant plus the exit condition imply the result you wanted.

Fast exponentiation keeps the invariant r⋅be=xn, where r is the result so far, b the base and e the remaining exponent. Initially r = 1, b = x and e = n. Each iteration either multiplies r by b when e is odd, or squares b and halves e, and either way the product is unchanged. When e reaches 0, the invariant says r = xn. The example asserts the invariant on every iteration and computes 31000 in 16 multiplications instead of 999, because e halves each time. That's chapter 6's modular exponentiation, the operation at the heart of RSA.

Invariants also prove that something is impossible. In the 8-puzzle, read the eight tiles row by row and count the inversions, pairs that are out of order. Sliding a tile sideways doesn't change the reading order at all. Sliding a tile up or down moves it past exactly two other tiles, which changes the inversion count by −2, 0 or +2. Either way the parity of the count never changes. The solved position has 0 inversions, so no position with an odd number can ever reach it. Swapping tiles 7 and 8 creates one inversion, and no sequence of moves will ever undo it. The example's search confirms both halves of the story. Exactly 181,440 positions, half of the 9! arrangements, are reachable, and all of them have even parity, so parity is the only obstacle. The 15-puzzle craze of 1880 made a famous challenge of undoing a swap of 14 and 15 on the 4 × 4 board, and an invariant of the same kind shows that nobody ever could.

When induction goes wrong

The classic fake proof claims that all horses are the same colour. Base case: one horse is the same colour as itself. Step: given n + 1 horses, the first n are the same colour by hypothesis, and so are the last n, and since the two groups overlap, all n + 1 are. The step is fine for n ≥ 2, but from n = 1 to n = 2 the "first one" and the "last one" don't overlap at all, so the chain breaks at its first link. Check that the inductive step works for every n, including the smallest, and especially when an argument talks about "the others" or "the overlap".

Common traps
  • Skipping the base case, or proving it for the wrong starting value.
  • A step that fails for small n, as in the horses proof.
  • Assuming P(n + 1) inside its own proof, which is circular.
  • A hypothesis too weak to carry the step. Often the fix is to prove something stronger, which gives you a stronger hypothesis to use.
  • An invariant that one branch of the loop breaks, such as an early continue or a special case.
  • Proving the invariant but not termination. A loop that never ends also never violates its invariant.
Exercises
Exercise 3.1Exponential beats quadratic

Prove by induction that 2n>n2 for every integer n ≥ 5. Where exactly does the proof use n ≥ 5, and what goes wrong at n = 4?

Show solution

Base case: 25=32>25=52. Step: assume 2n>n2 for some n ≥ 5. Then 2n+1=2⋅2n>2n2, and it's enough to show 2n2≥(n+1)2, that is n2≥2n+1. That holds for n ≥ 3, since n2−2n−1=(n−1)2−2≥2. So 2n+1>(n+1)2. ∎ The step itself works from n = 3, but the base case is what fails below 5: 24=16=42, not greater, and 23=8<9. Both parts are needed, and the starting point is where both hold.

Exercise 3.2Breaking chocolate

A chocolate bar is a grid of m × n squares. Each break splits one piece into two along a grid line. Prove that, however you break it, separating the bar into single squares takes exactly mn − 1 breaks.

Show solution

Use an invariant: number of pieces − number of breaks = 1. Initially there's 1 piece and 0 breaks. Each break takes one piece and makes two, so pieces go up by 1 and breaks go up by 1, and the difference stays 1. At the end there are mn pieces, so there were mn − 1 breaks, whatever order you broke them in. ∎ The same invariant says that a binary tree with L leaves has L − 1 internal nodes, and that combining n sorted lists by pairwise merges always takes n − 1 merges.

Exercise 3.3Binary search, proved

This function returns the first index i with a[i] >= x in a sorted list, or len(a) if there's none: lo, hi = 0, len(a); while lo < hi: mid = (lo + hi) // 2; if a[mid] < x: lo = mid + 1, else: hi = mid; then return lo. State a loop invariant, and prove that the function terminates and returns the right answer.

Show solution

Invariant: every element before index lo is less than x, and every element from index hi onward is at least x. Initially lo = 0 and hi = len(a), so both parts say nothing and hold vacuously (chapter 1). If a[mid] < x, then since the list is sorted every element up to mid is less than x, so setting lo = mid + 1 keeps the first part. Otherwise a[mid] ≥ x, and every element from mid onward is at least x, so setting hi = mid keeps the second. Termination: lo ≤ mid < hi, so each iteration strictly shrinks hi − lo, a natural number, which can't decrease forever. Correctness: at exit lo = hi, and the invariant says everything before lo is less than x and everything from lo onward is at least x, so lo is exactly the first index with a[lo] ≥ x, or len(a) if there's none. ∎ Writing hi = mid - 1 or lo = mid breaks the invariant or the termination argument, which is where the off-by-one bugs of binary search come from.

Chapter 04

Sets, relations and functions

11 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

Sets, relations and functions are the data types of mathematics, and you already program with all three. Two kinds of relation matter most. Equivalence relations, "is the same as", split things into classes, as hash keys, connected components and arithmetic mod n do. Partial orders, "must come before", describe dependencies, and they're why a build can be scheduled at all. Functions, classified as one-to-one, onto or both, are the main tool for counting. Comparing infinite sets the same way shows that some problems have no program at all.

Sets

A set is an unordered collection of distinct elements. Write x∈A for membership, A⊆B when every element of A is in B, and use union A∪B, intersection A∩B and difference A∖B. The power set 𝒫(A) is the set of all subsets of A, and a set of n elements has 2n of them, since each element is either in or out. The Cartesian product A×B is the set of ordered pairs (a, b). Set-builder notation, {x∈A∣P(x)}, is a set comprehension: Python's {x for x in A if p(x)} is the same idea with the same shape.

Relations and their properties

A relation R on a set A is a set of ordered pairs from A×A. Write a R b when (a, b) is one of them, like a ≤ b or "a divides b". Four properties classify relations:

  • reflexive: a R a for every a;
  • symmetric: a R b implies b R a;
  • antisymmetric: a R b and b R a imply a = b;
  • transitive: a R b and b R c imply a R c.

Each property is a "for all" statement over a finite set, so on a small set a program can check it exhaustively, as chapter 1 did with equivalences. Symmetric and antisymmetric aren't opposites. Equality is both, and "differs by at most 1" is symmetric without being transitive: 1 is close to 2 and 2 is close to 3, but 1 isn't close to 3. That relation is the trap behind any "approximately equal" comparison used as if it were equality.

Equivalence relations

A relation that is reflexive, symmetric and transitive is an equivalence relation, a precise version of "the same in the respect that matters". Every equivalence relation splits its set into disjoint equivalence classes, and every split into disjoint parts comes from an equivalence relation. Congruence mod 3 splits the integers into three classes by remainder, which is the foundation of modular arithmetic in chapter 5. "In the same connected component" is an equivalence relation on the vertices of a graph, and union-find is a data structure for maintaining its classes as edges arrive.

Programming languages quietly require equivalence relations. A hash table assumes that key equality is reflexive, symmetric and transitive, and that equal keys have equal hashes. Floating-point NaN breaks reflexivity, because nan == nan is false. In Python, two separately created NaN keys therefore land in a dictionary as two different keys, and you can't look up either one with a fresh NaN. A custom __eq__ that compares within a tolerance breaks transitivity, with similarly strange results.

Partial orders

A relation that is reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive is a partial order. It's "partial" because some pairs may be incomparable: neither 4 divides 6 nor 6 divides 4. Dependencies form partial orders, such as tasks in a build, courses with prerequisites and package versions, and so does "happened before" between events in a distributed system. A partial order is drawn as a Hasse diagram, with smaller elements lower and only the direct relationships shown, so that the rest follow by transitivity.

A chain is a set of mutually comparable elements, such as 1, 2, 4, 8. An antichain is a set of mutually incomparable ones, such as 7 to 12, none of which divides another. These two ideas answer scheduling questions. If the order is a set of dependencies, tasks in an antichain can run in parallel, and the longest chain is the critical path. You need at least as many rounds as the longest chain has elements, and grouping tasks by the length of the longest chain below them achieves exactly that number (Mirsky's theorem). Every finite partial order can also be listed in an order consistent with it, a topological sort, which is how build tools and package managers decide what to do first (exercise 4.2).

Functions

A function f:A→B assigns exactly one element of B to each element of A. It is injective (one-to-one) if different inputs give different outputs, surjective (onto) if every element of B is hit, and bijective if both. From an n-set to an m-set there are mn functions, because each of the n inputs independently picks one of m outputs. There are m(m−1)⋯(m−n+1) injective ones, because each input must avoid the outputs already used. A bijection between two finite sets proves they have the same size without counting either. That idea, the bijection rule, drives much of chapter 9, and the fact that no injection goes from a larger set to a smaller one is the pigeonhole principle of chapter 10.

Checked by enumeration

The example classifies four relations on 1 to 12, lists the classes of congruence mod 3, finds a longest chain and an antichain under divisibility, counts functions between small sets by brute force, and runs Cantor's diagonal construction on a finite list.

The table matches the definitions: divisibility and ≤ are partial orders, congruence mod 3 is an equivalence relation, and "within 1" is neither, since it fails transitivity. The function counts agree with the formulas: 43=64 functions from a 3-set to a 4-set, of which 4 × 3 × 2 = 24 are one-to-one and none are onto, because three inputs can't cover four outputs. From a 4-set to a 4-set, the one-to-one and onto functions are exactly the same 24, the 4! permutations. For finite sets of equal size, injective, surjective and bijective coincide.

Countable and uncountable

Two sets have the same cardinality if there's a bijection between them. For infinite sets that has surprising consequences. The even numbers are as numerous as all natural numbers, via n ↔ 2n. The integers are too, listed 0, 1, −1, 2, −2, and so on, and even the rationals can be listed in a zig-zag through the grid of fractions p/q. A set that can be listed this way is countable. The set of all finite strings over a finite alphabet is countable: list them by length, then alphabetically. Every program is a finite string, so there are countably many programs.

Georg Cantor showed in 1891 that the infinite binary sequences are not countable. Given any list of them, build a new sequence whose i-th bit is the opposite of the i-th bit of the i-th sequence. The new sequence differs from every listed sequence in at least one position, so the list was incomplete. The example runs this on eight rows of eight bits, and the flipped diagonal 10110000 is indeed missing. Now read an infinite binary sequence as a yes-or-no problem about natural numbers, where bit n answers "is n in the set?". There are uncountably many such problems and only countably many programs, so almost every problem has no program that solves it. Turing's proof that the halting problem is undecidable uses the same diagonal trick to name one specific such problem.

Common traps
  • Treating "approximately equal" as an equivalence relation. It isn't transitive.
  • Confusing symmetric and antisymmetric as if they were opposites.
  • Calling a relation a total order when some pairs are incomparable, then sorting by it.
  • Hash keys with a broken equality, such as NaN, mutable objects, or __eq__ without a matching __hash__.
  • Mixing up one-to-one and onto when counting. Write down which side must be covered.
  • Assuming all infinite sets are the same size. Cardinality is about bijections, not intuition.

Exercise 4.1Congruence is an equivalence

Fix an integer n ≥ 1, and define a ~ b to mean that n divides a − b. Prove that ~ is an equivalence relation, and describe its equivalence classes.

Show solution

Reflexive: a − a = 0 = n · 0. Symmetric: if a − b = nk, then b − a = n(−k). Transitive: if a − b = nk and b − c = nl, then adding gives a − c = n(k + l). ∎ The classes are the n residue classes: all integers with remainder 0 when divided by n, all with remainder 1, and so on up to n − 1, because a ~ b exactly when a and b leave the same remainder. Exercise 4.2A topological sort always exists

Prove that every finite nonempty partial order has a minimal element, one with nothing strictly below it. Then use this to prove, by induction on the number of elements, that every finite partial order can be listed so that whenever a is below b, a comes before b.

Show solution

Minimal element: start anywhere. If the current element isn't minimal, move to something strictly below it, and repeat. By transitivity and antisymmetry you can never return to an element already visited (that would put some element strictly below itself), and there are finitely many elements, so the walk stops, at a minimal element. Topological sort: a single element is already listed. For n + 1 elements, pick a minimal element m and put it first. Nothing is below m, so no constraint is violated by placing it first. Removing m leaves a partial order on n elements, which by the inductive hypothesis can be listed consistently, so list it after m. ∎ This proof is an algorithm: repeatedly remove something with no remaining predecessors. That's Kahn's algorithm for topological sorting, and it fails, by finding no minimal element, exactly when the "order" contains a cycle.

Exercise 4.3More problems than programs

Show that the set of all Python programs is countable. Conclude that there is a function from the natural numbers to {0, 1} that no Python program computes.

Show solution

A Python program is a finite string of Unicode characters, a finite alphabet. List all strings of length 0, then of length 1, then 2, and so on, each length in order of code points. Each length contributes finitely many strings, so every string appears at some finite position, and that's a listing. Discarding the strings that aren't valid programs leaves a listing of the programs, so they're countable. A function from ℕ to {0, 1} is an infinite binary sequence, and Cantor's argument shows those can't be listed. If every such function were computed by some program, listing the programs would list the functions, which is impossible. So some function is computed by no program. ∎ The argument shows that uncomputable problems exist, and in fact that almost all problems are uncomputable, without exhibiting one. The halting problem is a concrete example.

Chapter 05

Number theory: primes, GCDs and modular arithmetic

11 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

Number theory was once the purest mathematics there was, and it now secures every connection you make. This chapter builds the toolkit. It covers divisibility and primes, Euclid's algorithm, the oldest algorithm still in daily use, and the modular inverses it produces. It treats modular arithmetic as ordinary arithmetic on remainders, with one rule about division, and ends with the Chinese remainder theorem, which turns one big modulus into several small ones. Chapter 6 assembles these pieces into RSA.

Divisibility and primes

An integer a divides b, written a∣b, if b = ak for some integer k. For any integer a and positive b, the division theorem gives a unique quotient q and remainder r with

a=qb+r,0≤r<b,

and r is what a % b computes. A prime is an integer p > 1 whose only positive divisors are 1 and p. Chapter 3 proved that every integer above 1 is a product of primes. The fundamental theorem of arithmetic adds that the product is unique apart from order, and the proof of uniqueness needs a fact about primes proved below, Euclid's lemma.

Primes thin out, but slowly. The prime number theorem, proved in 1896, says that the number of primes up to n, written π(n), satisfies π(n)∼n/lnn: the ratio tends to 1. So near n, roughly one number in lnn is prime. Near 21024 that's one in about 710, or one odd number in about 355, which is why generating a random 1024-bit prime takes a few hundred tries rather than billions. The example computes π(n) up to ten million with a sieve, and the ratio creeps towards 1: 1.161 at a thousand, 1.071 at ten million.

Euclid's algorithm

The greatest common divisor gcd(a, b) is the largest integer dividing both. Euclid's algorithm, from around 300 BC, rests on one identity:

gcd(a,b)=gcd(b,amodb).

Proof. Write a = qb + r. Any common divisor of a and b divides r = a − qb, and any common divisor of b and r divides a = qb + r. So the two pairs have exactly the same common divisors, and in particular the same greatest one. ∎ Repeating the identity shrinks the second number each time, and when it reaches 0, gcd(g, 0) = g is the answer. Geometrically, each step cuts the largest possible squares off a rectangle, and the side of the last square is the gcd.

How many steps can it take? The remainders shrink fastest when quotients are large and slowest when every quotient is 1, which happens exactly for consecutive Fibonacci numbers. Gabriel Lamé proved in 1844 that the number of steps is at most about five times the number of decimal digits of the smaller number, so Euclid's algorithm is logarithmic. This was one of the first results in the analysis of algorithms. The example runs the worst case, two Fibonacci numbers near a million that take 29 steps, while random pairs of the same size average under 12.

Bézout's identity and modular inverses

Running Euclid's algorithm backwards gives more than the gcd. Bézout's identity says there are integers x and y with

gcd(a,b)=ax+by,

and the extended Euclidean algorithm finds them by carrying the coefficients through each step. For example, gcd(240, 46) = 2 = 240 × (−9) + 46 × 47. Two consequences follow at once.

Inverses. If gcd(a, n) = 1, then ax + ny = 1, so ax≡1(modn): x is an inverse of a modulo n. If gcd(a, n) = d > 1, then every multiple of a mod n is a multiple of d, so none equals 1 and no inverse exists. The example lists the inverses mod 7, where every nonzero number has one, and mod 8, where only the odd numbers do. Python computes inverses directly with pow(a, -1, n).

Euclid's lemma. If a prime p divides ab, then p divides a or p divides b. Proof. Suppose p∤a. Then gcd(a, p) = 1, so 1 = ax + py for some x, y. Multiply by b: b = abx + pby. Both terms are multiples of p, so p∣b. ∎ Uniqueness of prime factorisation follows: if two factorisations differed, a prime from one would divide the product of the other, and so, by the lemma, one of its primes, which then must be the same prime.

Modular arithmetic

Chapter 4 showed that congruence mod n, a≡b(modn), is an equivalence relation. It also respects arithmetic: if a≡a′ and b≡b′, then a+b≡a′+b′ and ab≡a′b′. So you can reduce mod n at any point in a calculation and the final remainder doesn't change. That's what keeps the numbers in chapter 6 small enough to handle, and it's how hash functions, checksums and clock arithmetic work. Addition, subtraction and multiplication behave as usual. Division is the one difference: you can divide by a only if a has an inverse mod n. When n is a prime p, every nonzero residue has one, so arithmetic mod p supports all four operations, a structure called a finite field.

One practical trap: Python's % always returns a result with the sign of the divisor, so -7 % 3 is 2, but C, Java, Go and JavaScript return −1. Code that indexes a hash table with h % size can produce negative indexes in those languages when h is negative.

The Chinese remainder theorem

A problem in the Sunzi Suanjing, a Chinese mathematical text from around the 3rd to 5th century, asks for a number that leaves remainder 2 when divided by 3, remainder 3 when divided by 5, and remainder 2 when divided by 7. The Chinese remainder theorem says that if the moduli m1,…,mk are pairwise coprime, such a system has exactly one solution modulo their product M=m1m2⋯mk. The proof is a construction. With Mi=M/mi, the number

x=∑iriMi(Mi−1modmi)

works, because the i-th term is ri mod mi and 0 mod every other modulus. The inverse exists because Mi is coprime to mi. Uniqueness holds because two solutions differ by a multiple of every mi, hence of M. The answer to the old problem is 23. In computing, the theorem lets you replace one computation mod a large M with several mod small factors. RSA implementations decrypt mod p and mod q separately and recombine, which is about four times faster than working mod n directly.

All of it, computed

The example sieves the primes up to ten million, runs Euclid's algorithm on its worst case and on random pairs, finds Bézout coefficients and inverses, and solves the Sunzi problem by the construction above.

The inverse of 17 mod 3120 is 2753, which is about to become a private key: chapter 6's tiny RSA example uses exactly these numbers. The inverse tables show the rule about division in action. Mod 7, every nonzero number has an inverse. Mod 8, the even numbers have none, because they share the factor 2 with the modulus.

Common traps
  • Negative remainders in languages whose % follows the sign of the dividend.
  • Dividing in modular arithmetic by a number that shares a factor with the modulus.
  • Overflow before the reduction: computing a * b % n in fixed-width integers can overflow before the % applies.
  • Applying the Chinese remainder theorem to moduli that aren't coprime, where solutions may not exist or may not be unique.
  • Trial division for big numbers. Testing primality of a 300-digit number needs chapter 6's methods, not a loop to its square root.
  • Hash tables sized to a power of two with structured keys (exercise 5.3).

Exercise 5.3Why hash tables like prime sizes

A hash table uses bucket h(k) = k mod m, and the keys that arrive are all multiples of 4: 0, 4, 8, 12, and so on. Prove that only m / gcd(4, m) of the m buckets can ever be used. How many buckets get used when m = 64, and when m = 61?

Show solution

Let g = gcd(4, m). Every bucket 4i mod m = 4i − qm is a multiple of g, since g divides both 4 and m, so at most m/g buckets, the multiples of g, can appear. All of them do appear: by Bézout, g = 4x + my, so 4x ≡ g (mod m), and multiplying by j gives every multiple jg as 4(jx) mod m. So exactly m/g buckets are used. For m = 64, g = 4 and only 16 buckets are used, a quarter of the table, so chains are four times longer than they should be. For m = 61, a prime, g = 1 and all 61 buckets are used. This is why textbook hash tables use prime sizes. Tables with power-of-two sizes, which are faster to index, instead scramble the hash bits first so that patterns like this don't survive.

Chapter 06

The road to public-key cryptography

9 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

Public-key cryptography rests on a one-way street. Multiplying two large primes is easy, and recovering them from the product appears to be extraordinarily hard. This chapter proves Fermat's little theorem and Euler's generalisation, builds RSA from them with a complete correctness proof, and generates real 1024-bit primes the way cryptographic libraries do. It then shows why "textbook" RSA, which is mathematically correct, is still insecure. The mathematics is the easy part to get right. The engineering around it is where systems break.

Fermat's little theorem

For a prime p and any integer a not divisible by p,

ap−1≡1(modp).

Proof. Multiply each of the numbers 1, 2, …, p − 1 by a, mod p. The results are all nonzero, since p divides neither factor, and all different, since a has an inverse mod p and ai≡aj would give i≡j. So the products are just 1, …, p − 1 in some order, and multiplying them all together gives

ap−1⋅(p−1)!≡(p−1)!(modp).

Every factor of (p − 1)! is invertible mod p, so cancel it to get ap−1≡1. ∎ Leonhard Euler generalised the theorem to any modulus. Let φ(n) count the numbers from 1 to n that are coprime to n. Then aφ(n)≡1(modn) whenever gcd(a, n) = 1, by the same argument applied to those φ(n) numbers. For n = pq with distinct primes, φ(n)=(p−1)(q−1), since the numbers not coprime to n are the q multiples of p and the p multiples of q, which share n itself.

RSA

Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir and Leonard Adleman published the scheme in 1977. To make a key pair:

  • pick two large random primes p and q, and let n = pq;
  • pick a public exponent e coprime to (p − 1)(q − 1), almost always 65537;
  • compute the private exponent d=e−1modφ(n) with the extended Euclidean algorithm. Implementations often use λ(n)=lcm(p−1,q−1) instead of φ(n), which gives a smaller d that works the same way.

The public key is (n, e) and the private key is d. To encrypt a message m < n, compute c=memodn. To decrypt, compute cdmodn. Both are fast thanks to chapter 3's square-and-multiply with a reduction mod n after each step.

Why decryption works. Since ed≡1(modφ(n)), write ed=1+kφ(n). If gcd(m, n) = 1, Euler's theorem gives

cd≡med=m⋅(mφ(n))k≡m⋅1k=m(modn).

The rare case where m shares a factor with n needs one more step through the Chinese remainder theorem (exercise 6.2). The same algebra gives signatures: Alice computes s=mdmodn, and anyone can check semodn=m. Only the holder of d could have produced s. With chapter 5's numbers, p = 61 and q = 53 give n = 3233, e = 17 and d = 2753, and the message 65 encrypts to 2790 and decrypts back to 65.

Finding large primes

Key generation needs random primes of 1024 bits or more. By the prime number theorem, about one odd number in 355 of that size is prime, so the generator draws random odd candidates and tests each. It needs a fast test, because trial division up to n would take longer than the age of the universe.

Fermat's little theorem suggests one: if an−1≢1(modn) for some a, then n is certainly composite. The converse fails, though. Some composites, the Carmichael numbers, satisfy an−1≡1 for every a coprime to n. The smallest is 561 = 3 × 11 × 17, and in 1994 Alford, Granville and Pomerance proved there are infinitely many. The Miller–Rabin test closes the gap. Write n−1=2sd with d odd. For a random base a, look at the sequence ad,a2d,…,a2sd mod n. If n is prime, the only square roots of 1 mod n are ±1, so the sequence must either start at 1 or pass through −1 before reaching 1. A composite n violates this for at least three quarters of all bases, so 40 independent rounds leave an error probability below 4−40≈10−24. That's far less likely than a hardware fault during the computation.

Built from scratch

The example finds every Carmichael number below 10,000 and shows Miller–Rabin rejecting each one, repeats the tiny RSA example, generates two random 1024-bit primes, and uses the 2048-bit key to encrypt, decrypt, sign and verify. It then demonstrates two weaknesses of textbook RSA. Its random numbers come from Python's random with a fixed seed so that the output is reproducible, which makes these keys worthless. Real keys must come from a cryptographically secure source such as Python's secrets module or the operating system.

The two primes took 55 and 275 odd candidates, around the expected 355, because the number of tries before a success is geometrically distributed (chapter 14). Decryption with a 2047-bit exponent costs about 2,046 squarings and 995 multiplications of 2048-bit numbers, which is why RSA private-key operations are expensive and why implementations use the Chinese remainder theorem to speed them up.

Why textbook RSA is broken

Everything above is mathematically correct, and the last two lines of output show that it's still insecure. First, it's deterministic. The same message always gives the same ciphertext, so an attacker who can guess the possible messages, "yes" or "no", a salary from a short list, simply encrypts each guess with the public key and compares. Second, it's malleable. Multiplying a ciphertext by 2e doubles the hidden plaintext without anyone knowing it, and real attacks have exploited structure like this. Small exponents with small messages are worse still: with e = 3 and m3<n, the ciphertext is just m3 and an ordinary cube root recovers m. Real RSA therefore always uses randomised padding, OAEP for encryption and PSS for signatures, and modern protocols mostly use elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman for key exchange. The Engineer's Citadel covers what to use in practice. The rule from this chapter is simpler: understand the mathematics, then never implement it yourself in production.

The security also rests on an assumption. Nobody has proved factoring is hard. The largest RSA challenge number factored so far, RSA-250 with 829 bits, fell in 2020 after thousands of core-years of computation, and 2048-bit keys are the current minimum recommendation. A large quantum computer running Shor's algorithm would factor efficiently, which is why NIST standardised the post-quantum key-encapsulation scheme ML-KEM in 2024.

Common traps
  • Rolling your own RSA, or any cryptography, outside of learning exercises.
  • Encrypting without padding, which makes ciphertexts deterministic and malleable.
  • Generating keys from a non-cryptographic random generator, or from too little entropy, as on devices that generate keys at first boot.
  • Using Fermat's test alone, which every Carmichael number passes.
  • Exponentiation whose timing depends on the secret exponent, which leaks the key bit by bit to anyone who can time it.
  • Treating factoring as proved hard. It's an assumption with a quantum expiry date.

Show solution

By the Chinese remainder theorem it's enough to prove the congruence mod p and mod q separately, since p and q are distinct primes. Mod p: if p∣m, both sides are 0. Otherwise Fermat gives mp−1≡1(modp), and since ed = 1 + k(p − 1)(q − 1), we get med=m⋅(mp−1)k(q−1)≡m. The same argument works mod q. A number congruent to m mod both p and q is congruent to m mod pq = n. ∎ The argument only needs ed ≡ 1 modulo both p − 1 and q − 1, which is why λ(n)=lcm(p−1,q−1) works as well as φ(n).

Exercise 6.3Shared primes

Two devices generate RSA keys, and because of a weak random generator they happen to pick the same prime p, with different second primes. Their moduli n1=pq1 and n2=pq2 are public. How can anyone factor both, and how expensive is it?

Show solution

Compute gcd(n1,n2) with Euclid's algorithm. It equals p, since that's the only common factor, and then q1=n1/p and q2=n2/p, which reveals both private keys. Euclid's algorithm on 2048-bit numbers takes a few thousand steps, a fraction of a millisecond. This happened in the real world. In 2012 Heninger, Durumeric, Wustrow and Halderman collected millions of public keys from the internet, computed pairwise gcds efficiently, and factored the RSA keys of about 0.5% of the TLS hosts they scanned. Most were embedded devices that generated keys at first boot, before they had gathered enough randomness. The attack needs nothing but chapter 5's oldest algorithm.

Chapter 07

Graphs as mathematics

10 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

A graph is just vertices and edges, which is why it can model networks, dependencies, maps, friendships and state machines. Stage 02 teaches the algorithms that run on graphs. This chapter asks what is always true of them, and proves it. It covers the handshake lemma, Euler's theorem on walks that use every edge, the equivalent ways to define a tree, the exact condition for splitting a graph into two sides, and the sudden appearance of a giant component in a random network once the average degree passes one.

Vocabulary

A graph G = (V, E) is a set of vertices V and a set of edges E, each edge joining two vertices. In a simple graph there are no loops and no repeated edges; a multigraph allows repeats. The degree of a vertex is the number of edges at it. A walk is a sequence of vertices joined by edges, a path is a walk that repeats no vertex, and a cycle is a closed walk that repeats no vertex except the start. A graph is connected if there's a path between every pair of vertices, and its maximal connected pieces are its components, the classes of the equivalence relation "is connected to" from chapter 4. Directed graphs give each edge a direction, and a vertex then has separate in- and out-degrees.

The handshake lemma

In any graph,

∑v∈Vdeg(v)=2|E|.

Proof. Each edge has two ends and contributes exactly 1 to the degree of each, so summing degrees counts every edge twice. ∎ This is double counting, counting the same thing in two ways, a technique chapter 9 uses again. One corollary follows at once. The sum of all degrees is even, so the number of vertices with odd degree must be even: in any group, the number of people who have shaken an odd number of hands is even. The example's random graph has 993 edges, degree sum 1986, and 96 odd-degree vertices, and that last number will be even whatever the random seed.

Euler tours

Graph theory began with a puzzle about the city of Königsberg, whose seven bridges joined two river banks and two islands. Could you walk through the city crossing every bridge exactly once? Leonhard Euler showed in 1736 that you couldn't. Model each land mass as a vertex and each bridge as an edge.

Euler's theorem. A connected graph has a closed walk that uses every edge exactly once, an Euler circuit, if and only if every vertex has even degree. It has an open walk that does so if and only if exactly two vertices have odd degree, and then the walk must start at one and end at the other. Necessity is the argument in the caption. Sufficiency is constructive. Start anywhere and walk along unused edges. Because degrees are even, you can only get stuck back at the start. If unused edges remain, some vertex on your circuit touches one, so walk a detour from there and splice it in. That's Hierholzer's algorithm, linear in the number of edges, and the example uses it to find a circuit through all ten edges of K5, where every degree is 4.

Change one word and everything changes. A Hamiltonian cycle visits every vertex exactly once, and deciding whether one exists is NP-complete (The Engineer's Workbench, chapter 8). No simple degree condition characterises it. The contrast is a good example of how a problem's statement, not its look, decides whether it's easy.

Trees

A tree is a connected graph with no cycles. For a graph with n vertices, the following are equivalent, and each is a useful definition:

  • it's connected and has no cycles;
  • it's connected and has exactly n − 1 edges;
  • it has no cycles and has exactly n − 1 edges;
  • between any two vertices there's exactly one path.

The key step is that every tree with n ≥ 2 vertices has a leaf, a vertex of degree 1: take a longest path, and its end can't have any other neighbour, since that would extend the path or close a cycle. Removing a leaf and its edge leaves a tree on n − 1 vertices, so by induction a tree on n vertices has n − 1 edges. The example's spanning-tree test relies on the third form: n − 1 edges that never close a cycle must form a tree. It counts the spanning trees of Kn by brute force and matches Cayley's formula, nn−2: 16, 125 and 1296. Spanning trees appear in network protocols that switch off redundant links to avoid loops, and in the minimum spanning tree algorithms of stage 02.

Bipartite graphs

A graph is bipartite if its vertices split into two sides with every edge crossing between them: workers and jobs, students and courses, users and items. Theorem: a graph is bipartite if and only if it has no cycle of odd length. One direction is easy. A cycle alternates sides with each step, so it returns to its start only after an even number of steps. For the other, run breadth-first search from any vertex and colour vertices by the parity of their distance. If some edge joins two vertices of the same colour, the two BFS paths to its ends plus the edge itself form a closed walk of odd length, which contains an odd cycle. So if there's no odd cycle, the colouring is a valid split. The proof is the algorithm: BFS either 2-colours the graph or exposes an odd cycle. In the example, a 6-cycle and an 8 × 8 grid are bipartite (colour the grid like a chessboard), while a 7-cycle and the Petersen graph, which contains 5-cycles, are not.

Checked, constructed and simulated

The example verifies the handshake lemma, applies Euler's degree test to Königsberg and constructs a circuit in K5, counts spanning trees, tests four graphs for bipartiteness, and then builds random graphs on 20,000 vertices with increasing average degree.

Random graphs and the giant component

The last table shows one of the most striking facts about networks. Paul Erdős and Alfréd Rényi studied graphs whose edges are chosen at random, and found a phase transition. When the average degree c is below 1, every component is small, a vanishing fraction of the graph: 0.1% at c = 0.5. Just above 1, a giant component emerges containing a fixed fraction β of all vertices, where β solves

β=1−e−cβ.

At c = 1.5 the theory predicts 58.3% and the simulation gives 58.3%; at c = 3, 94.0% against 94.1%. The intuition is a branching process. Explore outward from a vertex: each newly reached vertex brings about c new neighbours on average. Below 1 the exploration dies out quickly. Above 1 it survives with positive probability, and the survivors all join one giant cluster. The same threshold appears in epidemics, where an outbreak becomes large when each case infects more than one other on average, and in the resilience of networks to random failures.

Common traps
  • Confusing Euler and Hamilton: every edge once is easy; every vertex once is NP-complete.
  • Forgetting connectivity in Euler's theorem. Two separate cycles have all even degrees and no single circuit.
  • Checking only one condition for a tree: n − 1 edges with a cycle is disconnected, not a tree.
  • Using undirected degree conditions on directed graphs, where an Euler circuit needs in-degree equal to out-degree at every vertex.
  • Treating "walk", "path" and "cycle" as interchangeable in proofs, where repeated vertices matter.
  • Expecting smooth growth in networks. Connectivity can jump at a threshold, as the giant component does.

Exercise 7.2At least two leaves

Prove that every tree with at least two vertices has at least two leaves. Then show that a tree with n vertices has exactly n − 1 edges.

Show solution

Take a path in the tree with as many edges as possible, with ends u and w. It has at least one edge, since the tree is connected with at least two vertices. If u had a neighbour x other than the next vertex on the path, then either x is on the path, making a cycle, impossible in a tree, or it isn't, and adding it makes a longer path, contradicting maximality. So u has degree 1, and by the same argument so does w: two leaves. For the edge count, use induction on n. One vertex has 0 edges. For n ≥ 2, remove a leaf and its single edge. What remains is still connected, since no path between other vertices passed through a leaf, and still has no cycle, so it's a tree on n − 1 vertices with n − 2 edges by hypothesis. Adding the leaf back gives n − 1. ∎

Exercise 7.3Cracking a keypad with Euler

A lock opens when the last three binary digits typed match a secret 3-bit code, and it never resets. What's the shortest sequence of key presses guaranteed to open it? Use an Euler circuit to construct one.

Show solution

Make a directed graph whose vertices are the four 2-bit strings 00, 01, 10, 11, and add an edge from ab to bc for each 3-bit code abc. Each of the 8 codes is one edge, and every vertex has in-degree 2 and out-degree 2, so the graph has an Euler circuit. Walking it and typing one digit per edge, after an initial two digits for the starting vertex, produces a sequence in which every 3-bit code appears exactly once as a window. That's 8 + 2 = 10 key presses, for example 0001011100, whose eight windows are 000, 001, 010, 101, 011, 111, 110 and 100. No shorter sequence can work, since 10 presses give exactly 8 windows. This is a de Bruijn sequence. For k-digit codes over b symbols it has length bk+k−1 instead of the k⋅bk presses of trying each code separately.

Chapter 08

Colouring, matching and planarity

11 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

Three classic problems about graphs, each with a theorem that says when it can be solved. Colouring is the mathematics of scheduling without conflicts. Finding the minimum number of colours is NP-hard, but a greedy bound always holds. Matching is the mathematics of pairing up: Hall's theorem says exactly when everyone can be matched, and the Gale–Shapley algorithm finds a stable matching that has been used to assign doctors to hospitals for decades. Planarity is about drawing without crossings, and Euler's formula proves that some graphs can never be drawn flat.

Colouring

A proper colouring gives each vertex a colour so that adjacent vertices differ, and the chromatic number χ(G) is the fewest colours that suffice. Colouring models any problem of conflicts. Exams that share a student need different slots, variables that are live at the same time need different registers, and nearby transmitters need different frequencies. Computing χ(G) is NP-hard, and even deciding whether three colours suffice is NP-complete. One bound is always easy, though.

Theorem: χ(G)≤Δ+1, where Δ is the maximum degree. Proof. Colour the vertices one at a time in any order, giving each the smallest colour not used by its already-coloured neighbours. A vertex has at most Δ neighbours, so at most Δ colours are blocked and one of the first Δ+1 is free. ∎ The bound says nothing about how good greedy is on a particular graph, and the order matters enormously. The example colours a crown graph, which is bipartite and so needs only 2 colours: in one order greedy uses 8, in another 2. On a random graph with maximum degree 29, greedy used 9 colours, far below the bound of 30, and ordering by degree first saved one more. That's typical. The bound is a guarantee, not a prediction.

Matching and Hall's theorem

A matching is a set of edges with no shared vertices. In a bipartite graph of people and jobs, it's an assignment where nobody gets two jobs and no job goes to two people. When can everyone on the left be matched? Write N(S) for the set of jobs that someone in a set S of people would accept. If some set S of people likes fewer than |S| jobs between them, they can't all be matched, by the pigeonhole principle. Hall's theorem (1935) says this is the only obstacle: a matching that covers the whole left side exists if and only if

|N(S)|≥|S|for every subset S of the left side.

The proof is an induction on the number of people, splitting on whether some proper subset S has |N(S)| exactly equal to |S|. Algorithms don't check all 2n subsets. They grow a matching with augmenting paths, paths that alternate between unmatched and matched edges and start and end at unmatched vertices. Flipping such a path adds one edge to the matching, and when none exists the matching is maximum. When it falls short, the search also finds the set S that violates Hall's condition, a certificate of failure. In the example's four-person instance, the maximum matching covers only three people, and the reason is that ann, bob and cat between them accept only db and web, three people for two jobs.

Stable matching

Now suppose both sides have preferences: proposers rank receivers and receivers rank proposers. A matching is unstable if some proposer and receiver prefer each other to their assigned partners, because they'd both rather break away. David Gale and Lloyd Shapley showed in 1962 that a stable matching always exists, by an algorithm. Each free proposer proposes to the best receiver they haven't yet proposed to. Each receiver holds on to the best proposal so far and rejects the rest, trading up whenever a better one arrives.

It terminates: each proposer proposes to each receiver at most once, so there are at most n2 proposals. It ends with everyone matched: a receiver, once proposed to, always holds someone, so a proposer rejected by everyone would mean all n receivers are held by the other n − 1 proposers, which is impossible. It's stable: suppose p prefers r to the partner p ended with. Then p proposed to r earlier and was rejected, or later dropped, in favour of someone r preferred, and since receivers only ever trade up, r prefers her final partner to p. So (p, r) doesn't block. ∎

The algorithm isn't neutral. Every proposer gets the best partner they could have in any stable matching, and every receiver gets the worst. The example measures it with 1,000 on each side and random preferences. Proposers end up with, on average, their 8th choice and receivers with their 123rd. Swap the roles with the same preferences, and the former proposers fall to an average of 155th. The National Resident Matching Program, which places US medical graduates in residencies, runs a variant of this algorithm, and in 2012 Shapley and Alvin Roth received the Nobel prize in economics for this work. After a long debate about who should propose, the program now runs it with applicants proposing.

Planarity

A graph is planar if it can be drawn in the plane with no edges crossing, which matters for laying out circuits on a single layer and drawing readable diagrams. A planar drawing divides the plane into faces, counting the unbounded outside, and Euler's formula connects the three counts. For any connected planar drawing,

V−E+F=2.

Proof by induction on E. A connected graph with the fewest edges is a tree, with E = V − 1 and one face, and V − (V − 1) + 1 = 2. Adding an edge between two vertices of a connected drawing, without crossings, cuts one face into two: E and F each go up by 1, and the sum is unchanged. ∎ The example checks the cube, the octahedron, the icosahedron and a 10 × 10 grid.

The formula proves impossibility. In a simple planar graph with at least three vertices, every face is bounded by at least 3 edges and every edge borders at most 2 faces, so 3F≤2E. Substituting into Euler's formula gives

E≤3V−6.

K5 has 10 edges, but 3 × 5 − 6 = 9, so it can't be planar. If the graph has no triangles, every face needs at least 4 edges, which gives E≤2V−4. K3,3, the "three houses, three utilities" puzzle, has 9 edges against 2 × 6 − 4 = 8, so it's not planar either.

The same inequality gives planar graphs a vertex of degree at most 5. If every degree were 6 or more, the degree sum 2E would be at least 6V, contradicting E≤3V−6. Remove that vertex, colour the rest by induction, and put it back. At most 5 neighbours block at most 5 colours, so every planar graph is 6-colourable. The famous four colour theorem brings this down to 4. Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken proved it in 1976 with a computer checking nearly two thousand configurations, the first major theorem proved that way, and Georges Gonthier verified it entirely in the Coq proof assistant in 2005.

The algorithms, measured

The example compares greedy colouring orders, runs Gale–Shapley at scale and checks for blocking pairs, finds a maximum matching and the set that violates Hall's condition, and confirms Euler's formula and the edge bounds.

Gale–Shapley needed 8,133 proposals, against the worst-case bound of a million. With random preferences the expected number is about n ln n, roughly 6,900 for n = 1,000, which is the coupon collector's problem of chapter 13 in disguise. The stability check went through every pair a proposer prefers to their partner and found no blocking pair.

Common traps
  • Reporting a greedy colouring as optimal. It gives an upper bound, and the order can make it arbitrarily bad.
  • Checking Hall's condition one person at a time. The obstacle is usually a group.
  • Assuming a stable matching is fair to both sides. It's optimal for whoever proposes.
  • Using E≤3V−6 to prove planarity. It's necessary, not sufficient.
  • Applying Euler's formula to a disconnected drawing, where V − E + F = 1 + (number of components).
  • Forgetting the outer face when counting faces.

Exercise 8.2A card trick from Hall's theorem

Deal a shuffled 52-card deck into 13 piles of 4. Prove that you can always choose one card from each pile so that the 13 chosen cards contain exactly one of each rank, ace to king.

Build a bipartite graph with the 13 piles on the left and the 13 ranks on the right, joining a pile to a rank if the pile contains a card of that rank. A choice of one card per pile, with all ranks different, is a perfect matching. Check Hall's condition: take any k piles. They hold 4k cards. Each rank has only 4 cards in the whole deck, so those 4k cards must cover at least k different ranks, so |N(S)| ≥ |S|. Exercise 8.3The degree-5 vertex, carefully

Prove E≤3V−6 for a connected simple planar graph with V ≥ 3. Where does the proof use "simple", and where does it use V ≥ 3?

Show solution

Count edge–face incidences: for each face, count the edges on its boundary, an edge that touches the face on both sides counting twice. Each edge contributes exactly 2 to the total. Each face has a boundary of length at least 3, because a face bounded by 1 edge would need a loop and a face bounded by 2 would need two edges between the same vertices, both excluded in a simple graph. When V ≥ 3, the graph isn't a single edge, whose one face has boundary length 2. So 3F≤2E, and F≤2E/3. Euler's formula gives 2=V−E+F≤V−E+2E/3, so E≤3V−6. ∎ A multigraph breaks the bound immediately, since two vertices can be joined by any number of parallel edges without crossings.

Chapter 09

Counting: sequences, selections and arrangements

10 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

Counting is the ground floor of probability and of algorithm analysis. A handful of rules covers almost everything: sum, product, complement, division and bijection, plus the binomial coefficients that count selections. The skill is choosing a way to count in which every object is counted exactly once. A computer makes the habit of checking small cases by brute force almost free, and this chapter's example does exactly that, up to classifying every one of the 2,598,960 possible poker hands.

The sum, product and complement rules

The sum rule: if a set splits into disjoint parts, its size is the sum of their sizes. The product rule: the number of sequences (a1,…,ak) with ai drawn from a set of size ni is n1n2⋯nk. It extends to choices that depend on earlier ones, provided the number of options at each step doesn't. That's the generalised product rule, which counts ordered choices without repetition below. There are 628≈2.2×1014 passwords of 8 characters drawn from letters and digits, because each position independently takes one of 62 values.

The complement rule counts what you want by subtracting what you don't. Passwords with at least one digit are hard to count directly, since the digit could be anywhere and there could be several. Passwords with no digit are easy, 528, so the answer is 628−528. Whenever a problem says "at least one", try the complement first.

Permutations, combinations and the division rule

There are n! ways to order n distinct objects, and P(n,k)=n!/(n−k)! ways to choose an ordered sequence of k of them. To count unordered selections, use the division rule: if every object you want corresponds to exactly d of the things you counted, divide by d. Each set of k elements corresponds to k! ordered sequences, so the number of k-element subsets of an n-element set is

(nk)=n!k!(n−k)!.

The division rule handles repetition too. The 11 letters of MISSISSIPPI can be ordered in 11! ways if you pretend the repeated letters are distinguishable, and every distinct word arises from 4!4!2! of those orderings, since the four I's, four S's and two P's can be shuffled among themselves. So there are 11!/(4!4!2!)=34,650 distinct words. People seated around a round table, where only relative positions matter, can be arranged in n!/n=(n−1)! ways, because each seating corresponds to n rotations.

Bijections

The bijection rule (chapter 4) says that two sets in one-to-one correspondence have the same size. It turns a hard count into an easy one. Subsets of an n-element set correspond to bit strings of length n, one bit per element, so there are 2n of them.

Stars and bars counts the ways to write 10 as x1+x2+x3 with nonnegative integers. Represent a solution as 10 stars and 2 bars in a row, x1 stars, a bar, x2 stars, a bar, x3 stars. Every arrangement of 10 stars and 2 bars is a solution and vice versa, so there are (122)=66 solutions. In general, k nonnegative integers summing to n can be chosen in (n+k−1k−1) ways. Similarly, monotone paths across a 6 × 6 grid correspond to strings of six R's and six U's, so there are (126)=924.

Binomial coefficients and Pascal's triangle

The name comes from the binomial theorem:

(x+y)n=∑k=0n(nk)xkyn−k.

Expanding the product of n factors (x + y) means choosing x or y from each factor, and the term xkyn−k arises once for each way of choosing which k factors contribute x. Setting x = y = 1 gives ∑k(nk)=2n, which is another proof that a set has 2n subsets.

Pascal's rule, (nk)=(n−1k−1)+(n−1k), has a combinatorial proof, an argument that counts one set in two ways. Fix one element of the n-element set. A k-subset either contains it, and then the other k − 1 elements come from the remaining n − 1, or it doesn't, and all k come from the remaining n − 1. Combinatorial proofs are often shorter than algebra and explain why an identity holds.

Every count, checked

The example checks each rule against brute-force enumeration: ordered and unordered choices, the MISSISSIPPI words, stars and bars, the complement rule on a small alphabet, every 5-card poker hand classified one by one, and Pascal's rule and grid paths.

The poker table is a good test of the method, because each formula is a product-rule argument that's easy to get subtly wrong. A full house is a rank for the triple (13 ways), three of its four suits ((43)), a different rank for the pair (12) and two of its suits ((42)): 3,744. Two pair needs care. The two pair ranks form an unordered set, (132), not 13 × 12, which would count every hand twice. Then come the suits for each pair and a fifth card from the 11 remaining ranks. Counting all 2,598,960 hands takes the example a few seconds and confirms all six formulas exactly. The "flush" line includes the 40 straight flushes, which a full hand ranking would separate out.

Common traps
  • Counting ordered selections when order doesn't matter, the "13 × 12 for two pair" mistake, which overcounts by exactly the number of orderings.
  • Adding counts of sets that overlap. The sum rule needs disjoint parts; overlaps need chapter 10.
  • Forgetting the division rule for repeated items, as in words with repeated letters.
  • Off-by-one errors in stars and bars: "at least one each" and "zero allowed" give different formulas.
  • Applying the product rule when the number of options changes depending on earlier choices.
  • Trusting a formula you haven't checked on a small case, when brute force would take seconds.

Chapter 10

Pigeonhole and inclusion–exclusion

10 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

Two principles extend the counting rules. The pigeonhole principle says that if you put more objects than boxes, some box gets two. It sounds trivial, yet it proves that no compressor shrinks every file and that every hash function has collisions, and it proves things exist without finding them. Inclusion–exclusion counts overlapping sets exactly by adding and subtracting in alternation. It explains why the chance that nobody gets their own hat back is almost exactly 1/e, and it counts the passwords that satisfy a policy.

The pigeonhole principle

If n + 1 objects go into n boxes, some box holds at least two. More generally, if N objects go into k boxes, some box holds at least ⌈N/k⌉. In the language of chapter 4: there's no one-to-one function from a larger finite set to a smaller one.

The principle proves several facts engineers rely on. Every hash function has collisions: a 64-bit hash maps infinitely many inputs to 264 outputs, so some outputs are shared. That's unavoidable, and the design question is only whether collisions are hard to find. No lossless compressor can shrink every input: there are 2n strings of n bits but only 2n−1 strings of all shorter lengths combined, so if a compressor shrank every n-bit string, two would compress to the same output and couldn't both be recovered. A compressor that shrinks some inputs must grow others, and the example shows zlib turning 10,000 random bytes into 10,011 while crushing 10,000 bytes of repetitive text into 99. And with 367 people, two must share a birthday. Chapter 12 shows the far more surprising fact that 23 people give better-than-even odds.

Order in every sequence

Pigeonhole arguments often hide in the choice of boxes. Erdős–Szekeres theorem: every sequence of n2+1 distinct numbers contains an increasing or a decreasing subsequence of length n + 1. Proof. Label each position i with a pair (ai,di): the lengths of the longest increasing and longest decreasing subsequences ending at position i. For two positions i < j, if the j-th number is larger, then aj>ai, since it extends any increasing subsequence ending at i; if smaller, dj>di. So no two positions have the same label. If every ai and di were at most n, there would be only n2 possible labels for n2+1 positions, so two would coincide. Contradiction. ∎ The example confirms the theorem on 10,000 random sequences of 10 = 32 + 1 numbers, and shows a sequence of 9 that escapes it, so the bound is tight.

Inclusion–exclusion

The sum rule needs disjoint sets. For overlapping sets, adding the sizes counts the overlap twice, so subtract it: |A∪B|=|A|+|B|−|A∩B|. With three sets the pairwise overlaps are subtracted, but that removes the triple overlap once too often, so it's added back:

|A∪B∪C|=|A|+|B|+|C|−|A∩B|−|A∩C|−|B∩C|+|A∩B∩C|.

The general formula alternates over all intersections of 1, 2, 3, … of the sets. It's correct because an element lying in exactly m of the sets is counted (m1)−(m2)+(m3)−⋯=1 times, by the binomial theorem applied to (1−1)m=0. Inclusion–exclusion usually pairs with the complement rule. It's easier to count the objects that violate at least one condition and subtract them from the total than to count those that satisfy every condition directly.

Four applications

Divisibility. How many numbers from 1 to 1000 are divisible by none of 2, 3 and 5? Let A2,A3,A5 be the multiples of each. Then |A2|=500, |A3|=333, |A5|=200, the pairwise intersections are the multiples of 6, 10 and 15 (166, 100 and 66), and the triple intersection is the multiples of 30 (33). So the answer is 1000 − 1033 + 332 − 33 = 266. The same reasoning gives Euler's totient from chapter 6 as a product over the distinct primes dividing n:

φ(n)=n∏p∣n(1−1p),φ(360)=360⋅12⋅23⋅45=96.

Onto functions. Assign 6 distinct tasks to 3 workers so that nobody is idle. Of the 36 assignments, subtract those that miss some particular worker, 3⋅26, and add back those that miss two particular workers, 3⋅16: 729 − 192 + 3 = 540.

Derangements. n people check their hats and get them back at random. How likely is it that nobody gets their own? A permutation with no fixed point is a derangement. Let Ai be the permutations that fix person i. Any j particular people can be fixed in (n−j)! ways, so inclusion–exclusion gives the number of derangements

Dn=∑j=0n(−1)j(nj)(n−j)!=n!∑j=0n(−1)jj!.

The sum is the start of the series for e−1, so Dn/n!→1/e≈0.368, and in fact Dn is n!/e rounded to the nearest integer. Remarkably, the probability barely depends on n: about 37% whether 9 people check hats or 9,000.

Password policies. How many 8-character passwords over letters and digits contain at least one lowercase letter, one uppercase letter and one digit? Count the violators. Passwords with no lowercase letter number 368, with no uppercase 368, with no digit 528. Those lacking two kinds at once number 108, 268 and 268, and none lack all three. So the policy admits 628−(2⋅368+528)+(108+2⋅268) passwords, about 73% of all of them. A policy meant to strengthen passwords actually shrinks the space attackers must search, by about a quarter.

Checked by enumeration

The example verifies the compression count, Erdős–Szekeres, and each inclusion–exclusion formula above against brute force, including every permutation of up to 9 hats.

The derangement table shows how fast the convergence is. The share of permutations with no fixed point is already 0.367882 at n = 8, against 1/e = 0.367879, because the error of a truncated alternating series is smaller than its first omitted term, here 1/9!. The same series explains why the rounded value n!/e is always exactly right.

Common traps
  • Expecting pigeonhole to say which box. It proves a collision exists, not where it is.
  • Choosing the wrong boxes. The hard part of a pigeonhole proof is deciding what the boxes are.
  • Sign errors in inclusion–exclusion, or stopping before the last intersection term.
  • Using inclusion–exclusion on the conditions you want instead of on the violations, which is usually much messier.
  • Believing any compressor can always shrink data. Random and already-compressed data grow.
  • Assuming password rules increase the search space. They remove options, and help only by banning predictable choices.

Exercise 10.1Five points in a square

Prove that among any five points in a square of side 1, some two are at distance at most 2/2 from each other.

Show solution

Cut the square into four smaller squares of side 1/2. Five points in four squares means some small square contains two of them, counting points on a shared edge as belonging to either square. Two points in a square of side 1/2 are at most its diagonal apart, which is (1/2)2+(1/2)2=2/2. ∎ The proof doesn't find the two points, which is typical of pigeonhole arguments, and the bound is tight: put four points at the corners and one in the centre.

Exercise 10.2A coprime pair

Prove that any n + 1 numbers chosen from {1, 2, …, 2n} include two that are coprime.

Use the n boxes {1, 2}, {3, 4}, …, {2n − 1, 2n}. With n + 1 numbers, some box contains two of them, which are consecutive integers k and k + 1. Any common divisor of k and k + 1 divides their difference, 1, so they're coprime. ∎ The bound is tight: the n even numbers 2, 4, …, 2n contain no coprime pair.

Chapter 11

Sums, asymptotics and recurrences

9 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

Analysing an algorithm comes down to two kinds of mathematics. Loops become sums, and recursion becomes recurrences. This chapter collects the sums that keep appearing, with their closed forms and approximations, including harmonic numbers and Stirling's formula. It defines asymptotic notation precisely enough to use correctly, and solves recurrences three ways: by drawing the recursion tree, by guessing and proving, and by the master theorem, which settles most divide-and-conquer algorithms in one line. It finishes with linear recurrences, and a closed form for Fibonacci numbers that floating point breaks at n = 71.

Sums that keep appearing

An arithmetic sum, such as the comparisons of insertion sort on reversed input, has the closed form ∑i=1ni=n(n+1)/2, which chapter 3 proved by induction. A geometric sum, with ratio r ≠ 1, is

∑k=0nrk=rn+1−1r−1.

Multiply the sum by r, subtract the original, and almost everything cancels. When r > 1 the largest term dominates. That's why an array that doubles its capacity copies fewer than twice as many elements in total as its final size: 1 + 2 + 4 + … + n < 2n. When r < 1 the first term dominates, and the infinite sum converges to 1/(1 − r).

The harmonic numbers Hn=1+12+⋯+1n appear in the expected cost of randomised quicksort, the coupon collector's problem and the analysis of hashing. Comparing the sum with the integral of 1/x, the area under a staircase versus the area under a curve, gives ln(n+1)≤Hn≤1+lnn, and more precisely

Hn=lnn+γ+O(1/n),γ≈0.57722,

where γ is Euler's constant. Factorials have Stirling's approximation, n!≈2πn(n/e)n, with relative error about 1/(12n). Taking logarithms gives logn!=Θ(nlogn), the bound behind every comparison sort's lower limit in The Engineer's Workbench.

Asymptotic notation, precisely

f(n) = O(g(n)) means f grows no faster than g up to a constant: there are c and n0 with f(n)≤cg(n) for all n≥n0. Ω is the matching lower bound, and Θ means both. Little-o, f = o(g), means f/g→0. A practical test: if limf(n)/g(n) exists and is a positive constant, then f=Θ(g). Three habits avoid most mistakes. O is only an upper bound, so "this algorithm is O(n2)" is true of a linear algorithm too, and when you mean a tight bound, say Θ. The "=" in f = O(g) is not symmetric, since O(g) is really a set of functions. And the constants hidden by O decide which algorithm wins at the sizes you actually have.

From code to recurrence

A recursive algorithm's cost satisfies the same recursion as the code. Merge sort on n items sorts two halves and merges them with at most n comparisons, so its comparison count satisfies

T(n)=T(⌈n/2⌉)+T(⌊n/2⌋)+n,T(1)=0.

To solve a recurrence, draw its recursion tree: one node per call, labelled with the work done outside the recursive calls.

The tree suggests T(n) = n log2 n for powers of 2, and a guess can always be proved by induction, which is the substitution method (exercise 11.2). For n = 1,024 the example computes T exactly as 10,240 = n log2 n. For other n, the floors and ceilings change the answer slightly but not the growth: 9,976 against 9,966 for n = 1,000.

The master theorem

Most divide-and-conquer algorithms split a problem of size n into a subproblems of size n/b and do Θ(nc) extra work. For

T(n)=aT(n/b)+Θ(nc),

the master theorem compares c with logba, the exponent that counts the leaves of the recursion tree:

T(n)={Θ(nc)if c>logba (the root dominates)Θ(nclogn)if c=logba (every level costs the same)Θ(nlogba)if c<logba (the leaves dominate)

The recursion tree explains all three cases. Level j has aj nodes each doing (n/bj)c work, so the level totals form a geometric series with ratio a/bc. If the ratio is below 1 the first term, the root, dominates. If it's exactly 1, all logbn levels contribute equally. If it's above 1 the last level, the leaves, dominates, and there are alogbn=nlogba of them.

Binary search (a = 1, b = 2, c = 0) is the middle case, with Θ(logn). Merge sort (2, 2, 1) is also the middle case, Θ(nlogn). Karatsuba's multiplication replaces four half-size products with three (3, 2, 1), and the leaves dominate: Θ(nlog23)=Θ(n1.585), beating the schoolbook n2. Strassen's matrix multiplication uses seven half-size products instead of eight, giving Θ(nlog27)=Θ(n2.807). The theorem doesn't apply when the extra work isn't a polynomial, such as n/logn, or when the subproblems have unequal sizes. Then the Akra–Bazzi method or a direct look at the tree is needed.

Linear recurrences

Recurrences like Fn=Fn−1+Fn−2 have exact solutions. Guess Fn=xn and substitute, which gives the characteristic equation x2=x+1, with roots φ=(1+5)/2≈1.618 and ψ=(1−5)/2≈−0.618. Every solution is a combination Aφn+Bψn, and the starting values F0=0,F1=1 fix the constants, giving Binet's formula:

Fn=φn−ψn5.

Since |ψ|<1, the second term shrinks to nothing and Fn is simply φn/5 rounded. That's mathematically exact for every n, but the example shows it failing in double-precision floating point at n = 71, where F71 has 15 digits and the 53-bit significand can no longer carry the error below one half. For exact large Fibonacci numbers, use integer arithmetic with repeated squaring of a 2 × 2 matrix (chapter 17). The Towers of Hanoi recurrence Mn=2Mn−1+1 is solved by adding 1 to both sides: Mn+1=2(Mn−1+1), so Mn=2n−1.

Measured

The example checks the harmonic and Stirling approximations, computes the merge sort recurrence exactly, measures T(n) divided by the master theorem's prediction for six recurrences, finds where Binet's formula fails in floating point, and counts Hanoi moves by recursion.

In the master theorem table, every ratio settles to a constant as n grows. That's exactly what Θ promises, and the constants differ: about 3 for Karatsuba, 2.33 for Strassen, 1 in the limit for merge sort. The theorem says nothing about those constants, which is why Strassen's algorithm only beats ordinary matrix multiplication for matrices well into the hundreds or thousands of rows, depending on the implementation.

Common traps
  • Reading O as a tight bound. Say Θ when you mean it.
  • Applying the master theorem outside its form, with unequal splits or non-polynomial extra work.
  • Forgetting the base case's cost when you need an exact count rather than a growth rate.
  • Trusting a closed form computed in floating point, as Binet's formula shows.
  • Comparing algorithms by exponent alone when the constants and your actual n decide which is faster.
  • Summing a geometric series in the wrong direction and deciding the smallest term dominates.

Chapter 12

Conditional probability and Bayes' rule

9 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

Probability is counting with weights, and conditional probability is how those weights change when you learn something. Intuition fails here in predictable ways. People stick with their first door in Monty Hall, read a positive result from an accurate test as near-certainty, and underestimate shared birthdays. A disciplined method fixes all three: lay out the outcomes in a tree, assign each its probability, and compute. Bayes' rule, which turns P(evidence | cause) into P(cause | evidence), is the formula behind spam filters, diagnostic tests and every alert that pages an engineer at night.

Sample spaces and the tree method

A sample space is the set of possible outcomes of an experiment, each with a probability, all summing to 1. An event is a set of outcomes, and its probability is the sum of theirs. When the experiment happens in stages, draw a tree: each branch is labelled with the probability of that step given everything before it, and an outcome's probability is the product along its path. The 6.042J text calls this the four-step method: find the outcomes, define the event, assign outcome probabilities, add them up. It sounds pedantic, and it settles arguments that intuition can't.

Take the Monty Hall problem. A car is behind one of three doors and goats are behind the others. You pick door 1. The host, who knows where the car is, opens a different door that has a goat, and offers you the chance to switch to the remaining door. Should you?

The tree settles it: switching wins with probability 2/3. The common intuition, "two doors left, so 50–50", ignores the fact that the host's choice depends on where the car is. The example confirms 2/3 exactly by walking the tree with fractions, and approximately by playing 100,000 games.

Conditional probability

The probability of A given that B has happened is

Pr[A∣B]=Pr[A∩B]Pr[B],Pr[B]>0.

Knowing B shrinks the sample space to B, and the formula rescales so that B's outcomes sum to 1. Rearranged, it's the product rule, Pr[A∩B]=Pr[B]Pr[A∣B], which is what multiplying along a tree branch does. If events B1,…,Bk split the sample space, the law of total probability adds up the branches: Pr[A]=∑iPr[Bi]Pr[A∣Bi].

What you condition on must be exactly what you learned, including how you learned it. With two children, each equally likely to be a boy or a girl, Pr[two boys∣at least one boy]=1/3, since three equally likely families remain: BB, BG, GB. But Pr[two boys∣the older is a boy]=1/2. The information sounds similar, and it leaves different sample spaces.

Bayes' rule

Writing Pr[A∩B] in two ways gives Bayes' rule:

Pr[A∣B]=Pr[B∣A]Pr[A]Pr[B].

It converts what you can measure, how often the evidence appears when the cause is present, into what you want, how likely the cause is given the evidence. A diagnostic test detects a condition 95% of the time and gives a false positive 5% of the time. One person in a hundred has the condition. If you test positive, the chance that you have it is

0.95×0.010.95×0.01+0.05×0.99=0.00950.059≈0.161.

Only about 16%. The test is accurate, but the condition is rare, so the healthy 99% produce far more false positives than the sick 1% produce true ones. Ignoring that is the base rate fallacy. A second independent positive test raises the probability to about 79%, because the first result's posterior becomes the second one's prior. Monitoring has the same structure. An alert that is 99% accurate, watching for a failure that occurs in 0.1% of minutes, will page you mostly for nothing, and the fix is better specificity or a combination of signals, not a lower threshold.

Independence

A and B are independent if Pr[A∩B]=Pr[A]Pr[B], equivalently if learning B doesn't change the probability of A. Independence is a numerical fact to check, not a matter of whether events "seem related". Roll two dice. "The first die is even" is independent of "the sum is 7", since whatever the first die shows, exactly one value of the second makes 7. It is not independent of "the sum is 8". Given an even first die, the second die can always complete 8 (6, 4 or 2), so the sum is 8 with probability 1/6, against 5/36 overall. The example checks both by counting. Assuming independence when it doesn't hold is one of the costliest mistakes in reliability engineering. Disks from the same batch fail together, and replicas in the same rack lose power together, so "two failures at once" is far likelier than the product of the individual probabilities.

Birthdays

How many people must be in a room before a shared birthday is more likely than not? Counting the complement, with 365 equally likely days, n people all have different birthdays with probability

365365⋅364365⋯365−n+1365≈e−n(n−1)/(2⋅365),

and this drops below 1/2 at n = 23. The surprise comes from counting pairs, not people. 23 people form 253 pairs, and each pair has a 1-in-365 chance of matching. Chapter 14 applies the same approximation to random identifiers and hash values, where it decides how many bits you need.

Computed and simulated

The example computes each result exactly with fractions, then simulates it: Monty Hall, the test with its base rate across a million simulated people, the birthday problem, the two-child puzzle, and independence for two pairs of dice events.

The simulations land close to the exact values, within the sampling error chapter 19 quantifies: 0.668 against 2/3, 0.160 against 0.161, 0.510 against 0.507. That's the right role for simulation. It checks a calculation, or explores a problem too messy to calculate. It doesn't replace the calculation when one is available, because it can't tell you whether 0.668 means 2/3 or 0.67.

Common traps
  • Confusing Pr[A∣B] with Pr[B∣A], known in courtrooms as the prosecutor's fallacy.
  • Ignoring the base rate when judging an accurate test for a rare condition.
  • Assuming independence for events that share a cause.
  • Conditioning on what happened rather than on what you learned and how, as in the two-child puzzle.
  • The gambler's fallacy: independent trials have no memory, and a run of losses doesn't make a win "due".
  • Reading a small simulation as exact, without asking how much it could vary.

60% of incoming mail is spam. The word "free" appears in 20% of spam and in 1% of legitimate mail. What's the probability that a message containing "free" is spam?

Exercise 12.2Independence of complements

Prove that if A and B are independent, then so are A and the complement B‾.

Show solution

A splits into the disjoint events A∩B and A∩B‾, so

Pr[A∩B‾]=Pr[A]−Pr[A∩B]=Pr[A]−Pr[A]Pr[B]=Pr[A](1−Pr[B])=Pr[A]Pr[B‾].

∎ Applying the result twice shows that the complements are independent too. So if two components fail independently, they also survive independently, which is what reliability calculations multiply.

Exercise 12.3A host who doesn't know

Change the game: the host doesn't know where the car is and opens one of the other two doors at random. It happens to show a goat. Should you switch now?

Show solution

Draw the tree. You pick door 1. The car is behind each door with probability 1/3, and the host opens door 2 or 3 with probability 1/2 each, regardless. The outcomes where he reveals a goat are: car at 1 and opens 2 or 3 (1/6 + 1/6 = 1/3); car at 2 and opens 3 (1/6); car at 3 and opens 2 (1/6). The total is 2/3. Of that, the car is behind your door with probability 1/3 and behind the other closed door with probability 1/3. So

Pr[switch wins∣goat shown]=(1/3)/(2/3)=1/2.

Now switching doesn't matter. The doors and the goat look identical in both games. The difference is the process that produced what you saw, and conditioning has to account for it.

Chapter 13

Random variables and expectation

8 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

A random variable is a number that depends on chance: the cost of a randomised algorithm, the number of collisions in a hash table, the latency of a request. Its expectation is its long-run average. One fact does most of the work in this chapter, and it's the most useful tool in probability for programmers. Linearity of expectation says the expectation of a sum is the sum of expectations, always, even when the parts depend on each other. Combined with indicator variables, it turns hard problems into a line of algebra. Variance then measures how far reality strays from the average.

Random variables and expectation

A random variable X assigns a number to each outcome of an experiment. Its expectation is the probability-weighted average of its values:

𝔼[X]=∑xx⋅Pr[X=x].

A fair die has expectation (1 + 2 + … + 6)/6 = 7/2. The expectation needn't be a possible value: no roll shows 3.5. An indicator variable IA is 1 if event A happens and 0 otherwise, so 𝔼[IA]=Pr[A]. Indicators are how you turn questions about how many things happen into questions about how likely each one is.

Linearity of expectation

For any random variables X and Y, and constants a and b,

𝔼[aX+bY]=a𝔼[X]+b𝔼[Y].

Proof. Write both sides as sums over outcomes ω: ∑ω(aX(ω)+bY(ω))Pr[ω] splits into a∑ωX(ω)Pr[ω]+b∑ωY(ω)Pr[ω]. ∎ The proof never mentions independence, and it isn't needed. That's what makes linearity so powerful: write a complicated count as a sum of indicators, which may depend on each other in tangled ways, and add up their probabilities.

Fixed points. n people get their hats back in a random order. How many get their own, on average? Let Ii indicate that person i does. Each person gets their own hat with probability 1/n, so the expected number is n⋅1/n=1, for every n. The indicators are dependent, since if n − 1 people have their own hats the last one must too, and it doesn't matter.

Inversions. How many swaps does insertion sort make on a random permutation? One per inversion, a pair of positions in the wrong order. Each of the (n2) pairs is inverted with probability 1/2, so the expected number is n(n−1)/4. That makes insertion sort quadratic on average, not just in the worst case.

Running maxima. Scanning an array to find its maximum, how often does the "best so far" change? Position i holds a new maximum exactly when its value is the largest of the first i, which for a random order has probability 1/i. The expected number of updates is 1+12+⋯+1n=Hn≈lnn, only about 7.5 for a thousand values. That's also the expected number of times a "keep the best candidate" loop has to write.

Empty bins. Throw n balls into n bins at random. Each bin stays empty with probability (1−1/n)n≈1/e, so about 37% of the bins are empty, and the same fraction of hash buckets is unused when a table has as many keys as buckets.

Waiting times and the coupon collector

If each trial succeeds independently with probability p, the number of trials up to and including the first success has a geometric distribution with expectation 1/p. One trial always happens, and with probability 1 − p you're back where you started, so 𝔼=1+(1−p)𝔼, which solves to 1/p.

The coupon collector's problem asks how many random draws it takes to collect all n kinds of coupon. Split the process into phases. Once you have i kinds, each draw is new with probability (n − i)/n, so that phase takes n/(n − i) draws on average. By linearity the total expectation is

∑i=0n−1nn−i=n(1+12+⋯+1n)=nHn≈nlnn.

Variance

The expectation says nothing about spread. The variance measures it as the expected squared distance from the mean, and the standard deviation σ is its square root:

Var[X]=𝔼[(X−μ)2]=𝔼[X2]−μ2.

A die has 𝔼[X2]=91/6, so Var[X]=91/6−49/4=35/12. Unlike expectation, variance adds only for independent variables, or more generally uncorrelated ones: Var[X+Y]=Var[X]+Var[Y]. So the sum of 10 dice has variance 350/12 ≈ 29.2 and standard deviation about 5.4, not 10 times the single die's 1.7. The spread of a sum grows like the square root of the number of terms, which is why averages are stable (chapter 19). For dependent variables, add twice their covariance.

Expectations hide the spread, and in engineering the spread is often what matters. The coupon collector's average for 50 kinds is 225 draws, but in the example one run needed 706. Request latency has the same character: a service with a fine mean latency can have a tail that ruins the experience for one request in a hundred, which is why production systems measure percentiles, not just averages (The Engineer's Watch).

Simulated

The example estimates each expectation above by repeating the experiment thousands of times and compares the average with the formula.

Every simulated mean lands within a fraction of a percent of its formula, and the fixed-point experiment adds something the formula didn't promise: the variance is about 1 as well. That's a hint of the Poisson distribution of chapter 14, which the number of fixed points approaches as n grows.

Common traps
  • Thinking linearity needs independence. It never does; that's its power.
  • Multiplying expectations of dependent variables: 𝔼[XY]=𝔼[X]𝔼[Y] needs independence.
  • Assuming 𝔼[1/X]=1/𝔼[X], or that a function of the average is the average of the function.
  • Adding variances of correlated variables, such as replicas that fail together.
  • Reporting an average without a spread, when the tail is what users feel.
  • Confusing the mean with the median for skewed quantities such as latency or wealth.

Chapter 14

Distributions and tail bounds

9 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

A handful of distributions cover most random quantities in computing, and each comes from a recognisable mechanism. Recognise the mechanism and you know the distribution. Tail bounds answer the question engineers actually ask: how unlikely is something far from average? Markov's inequality needs only the mean, Chebyshev's adds the variance, and the Chernoff and Hoeffding bounds show that sums of independent pieces almost never stray far. These bounds give randomised algorithms their guarantees, explain why the "power of two choices" balances load so well, and tell you how many bits a random identifier needs.

The Poisson distribution is the binomial's limit when n is large, p is small and λ=np stays fixed: Pr[X=k]=e−λλk/k!. It models the number of keys in one bucket of a hash table, or requests arriving in one second from many independent users. The geometric distribution is memoryless: having failed 100 times doesn't change the chance of success on the next try. The normal distribution appears whenever many independent contributions add up, a fact chapter 19 makes precise with the central limit theorem. Be wary of using it in the far tails, where it can be wrong by orders of magnitude.

Markov and Chebyshev

Markov's inequality. For a random variable X ≥ 0 and any a > 0,

Pr[X≥a]≤𝔼[X]a.

Proof. 𝔼[X]≥a⋅Pr[X≥a], since X contributes at least a whenever X ≥ a and never contributes a negative amount. ∎ It's weak, but it needs nothing except the mean and non-negativity. If an algorithm's expected running time is 1 second, at most 10% of runs take 10 seconds or more. Restarting runs that exceed twice the expected time therefore gives a guarantee, since each attempt succeeds with probability at least 1/2.

Chebyshev's inequality. For any X with mean μ and variance σ2,

Pr[|X−μ|≥t]≤σ2t2.

Apply Markov to the non-negative variable (X−μ)2 with a=t2. It says a variable is rarely more than a few standard deviations from its mean: at most 1/9 of the probability lies beyond 3σ, whatever the distribution. Applied to an average of n independent samples, whose variance is σ2/n, it proves the law of large numbers: averages converge to the mean.

Chernoff and Hoeffding

When X is a sum of independent pieces, its tails are far thinner than Chebyshev admits. They're exponentially small. Hoeffding's inequality: if X is a sum of n independent variables each between 0 and 1, then

Pr[X−𝔼[X]≥t]≤e−2t2/n.

The Chernoff bound measures the deviation relative to the mean μ: Pr[X≥(1+δ)μ]≤(eδ/(1+δ)1+δ)μ, which is at most e−δ2μ/3 for 0<δ≤1. The proofs apply Markov's inequality to esX, whose expectation factors into a product because the pieces are independent. That's why independence is essential.

At 600 heads, 6.3 standard deviations above the mean, the exact tail is 1.36×10−10. Markov says only that it's below 0.83, Chebyshev below 0.025, Chernoff below 8×10−5 and Hoeffding below 2×10−9. This is the arithmetic behind "with high probability" in randomised algorithms. Errors that need many independent pieces to go wrong together are exponentially unlikely, so a modest number of repetitions makes failure rarer than a hardware fault.

Balls into bins and the union bound

Throw n balls into n bins at random, the model for hashing n keys or assigning n jobs to n servers. The average load is 1, but the fullest bin holds about lnn/lnlnn balls with high probability. The proof bounds the chance that one particular bin gets k balls, then uses the union bound:

Pr[⋃iAi]≤∑iPr[Ai].

If each of the n bins overflows with probability at most 1/n2, then some bin overflows with probability at most 1/n. The union bound needs no independence at all, which makes it the most used inequality in algorithm analysis, and it becomes useless only when the probabilities it adds up exceed 1.

A small change in the process has a dramatic effect. Pick two random bins for each ball and put it in the less full one, and the maximum load drops to about lnlnn/ln2+O(1). That's an exponential improvement, from a single extra probe. This "power of two choices", analysed by Azar, Broder, Karlin and Upfal in 1994, is why load balancers such as NGINX and Envoy can sample two backends at random and send the request to the less loaded one, instead of tracking every server's load. In the example, 100,000 balls give a fullest bin of 8 with one choice and 3 with two.

The birthday bound for identifiers

Chapter 12's birthday calculation sets the size of random identifiers. Among k values drawn uniformly from N possibilities, the chance of any collision is about

1−e−k(k−1)/2N,

which reaches 1/2 near k≈1.18N. For b-bit identifiers, N=2b, so collisions become likely after about 2b/2 values, not 2b. A billion random 64-bit IDs collide with probability about 2.7%, which is not safe for a primary key. Version 4 UUIDs carry 122 random bits, and a trillion of them collide with probability around 10−13. The rule of thumb: a random identifier needs about twice as many bits as the logarithm of how many you'll create, plus a margin for the failure probability you can tolerate. The same bound says that a hash with b-bit outputs offers only about b/2 bits of collision resistance, which is why cryptographic hashes are 256 bits long.

Exact tails, bounds and simulations

The example computes the exact binomial tail with big integers alongside the four bounds, simulates balls into bins with one and two choices, applies the birthday bound to identifiers and checks it by drawing random 32-bit IDs until one repeats, and measures geometric waiting times.

The last line connects back to chapter 6: waiting for a 1-in-355 event takes 357 tries on average, matching the mean of 355, but more than 1,000 tries in 6% of runs. That's why the example there found one prime after 55 candidates and the other after 275. Geometric waits are memoryless and long-tailed, and a key generator that waits 1,000 candidates isn't broken.

Common traps
  • Applying Markov's inequality to a variable that can be negative.
  • Using Chernoff or Hoeffding without independence, where correlated failures can make large deviations common.
  • Trusting a normal approximation in the far tails, where it can be off by orders of magnitude.
  • A union bound whose terms add up to more than 1, which proves nothing.
  • Sizing random IDs as if collisions needed 2b values rather than about 2b/2.
  • Modelling bursty traffic as Poisson, which assumes independent arrivals.

Chapter 15

Randomised algorithms

9 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

Randomness in an algorithm is a tool, not a compromise. A random pivot defeats every bad input for quicksort at once. Contracting random edges finds a graph's minimum cut with almost no machinery. Multiplying by a random vector checks a matrix product faster than recomputing it, and a few random replacements sample a stream of unknown length uniformly. This chapter analyses each one with the tools of the last three chapters, and shows how repetition turns a small chance of success, or a small chance of error, into near-certainty.

Las Vegas and Monte Carlo

Randomised algorithms come in two kinds. A Las Vegas algorithm always gives the right answer, and only its running time is random: randomised quicksort and quickselect are examples. A Monte Carlo algorithm runs in bounded time but may be wrong with some small probability: the Miller–Rabin test of chapter 6, Karger's algorithm and Freivalds' check below. The two convert into each other. Stop a Las Vegas algorithm after twice its expected time and Markov's inequality bounds the chance it hasn't finished by 1/2, which gives a Monte Carlo algorithm. A Monte Carlo algorithm whose answers can be checked becomes Las Vegas by rerunning it until the check passes.

Amplification is what makes Monte Carlo algorithms practical. If one run errs with probability at most p, and errors are one-sided, so that a "composite" from Miller–Rabin is always right, then k independent runs all err with probability at most pk. With two-sided errors, take a majority vote of k runs, and the Chernoff bound of chapter 14 makes the error exponentially small in k.

Randomised quicksort

Quicksort with the first element as pivot takes n(n−1)/2 comparisons on sorted input, as The Engineer's Workbench showed. With a uniformly random pivot, the expected number of comparisons is about 2nlnn on every input, so no input is bad, only some unlucky runs, and chapter 14's bounds show those are rare.

Proof. Name the elements by rank, z1<z2<⋯<zn. Two elements zi and zj, with i < j, are compared at most once, and only if one of them is the first pivot chosen from the range zi,…,zj. If a pivot strictly between them comes first, they're split into different sides and never meet. Each of the j−i+1 elements in the range is equally likely to be chosen first, so they're compared with probability 2/(j−i+1). By linearity of expectation,

𝔼[comparisons]=∑i<j2j−i+1=2(n+1)Hn−4n≈2nlnn.

∎ For n = 2,000 that's 24,730, and the example's 20 runs on sorted input ranged from 23,291 to 28,530, against 1,999,000 for the fixed pivot. Quickselect, which recurses into only the side containing the k-th element, has expected cost linear in n by a similar argument, about 3.39n for the median.

Karger's minimum cut

A cut of a graph splits its vertices into two non-empty groups, and its size is the number of edges between them. The minimum cut measures how close a network is to falling apart. David Karger's 1993 algorithm is almost absurdly simple: pick a random edge, merge its two endpoints into one vertex, keeping all other edges and discarding the edges that become loops, and repeat until two vertices remain. The edges between them form a cut.

Analysis. Fix a minimum cut with k edges. Every vertex has degree at least k, since otherwise the edges at that vertex would form a smaller cut. So a graph with m vertices has at least mk/2 edges, and a random edge belongs to the cut with probability at most k/(mk/2)=2/m. The cut survives all contractions, from n vertices down to 2, with probability at least

∏m=3n(1−2m)=13⋅24⋅35⋯n−2n=2n(n−1).

That's small, but amplification fixes it. Repeat (n2)lnn times, keep the smallest cut found, and the chance of missing the minimum is at most (1−1/(n2))(n2)lnn≤e−lnn=1/n. The bound is pessimistic. On the example's graph of two 8-vertex cliques joined by 2 edges, the guarantee is 0.83% per run and the observed rate is 40%, because random edges almost always fall inside the dense cliques.

Freivalds' check

Someone hands you n × n matrices A, B and C and claims C = AB. Recomputing AB costs about n3 operations, or n2.81 with Strassen's algorithm. Rusins Freivalds observed in 1977 that you can check the claim with three matrix–vector products, in O(n2) time: choose a random vector r of 0s and 1s and test whether A(Br) = Cr. If C = AB, the test always passes. If not, let D = AB − C, which has some nonzero entry dij. Whatever the other entries of r are, at most one of the two values of rj makes row i of Dr zero, so the test catches the error with probability at least 1/2. Twenty independent trials miss it with probability below one in a million. The example plants one wrong entry among 3,600 and catches it in 51% of single trials.

Reservoir sampling

To choose k items uniformly at random from a stream whose length you don't know in advance, such as log lines or network packets, keep the first k. Then, when the i-th item arrives (counting from 1), keep it with probability k/i, replacing a uniformly random member of the sample. Claim: after i items, each one is in the sample with probability k/i. Proof by induction on i. It holds for i ≤ k. When item i + 1 arrives, it enters with probability k/(i + 1). An item already present, there with probability k/i by hypothesis, is evicted only if the newcomer enters and picks its slot, with probability ki+1⋅1k=1i+1. So it remains with probability ki⋅ii+1=ki+1. ∎ Jeffrey Vitter analysed this method in 1985 as Algorithm R, and it needs only k slots of memory, however long the stream.

Measured

The example runs each algorithm many times: quicksort with a fixed and a random pivot on sorted input, Karger's algorithm 2,000 times, Freivalds' check 1,000 times, reservoir sampling 100,000 times, and quickselect for the median of 100,001 values.

Quickselect was correct all 20 times, as a Las Vegas algorithm must be, while its cost varied from 1.79n to 5.08n around the expected 3.39n. The reservoir frequencies all sit within a few thousandths of 3/10, which is the uniformity the induction proved.

Common traps
  • Predictable randomness when an adversary chooses the input. If attackers can predict your pivots or hash seeds, the worst case comes back.
  • Running a Monte Carlo algorithm once and trusting it without amplification.
  • Confusing expected time with worst-case time in a deadline or SLA.
  • Biased sampling: rand() % n is not uniform unless n divides the generator's range, and shuffling by sorting with a random comparator isn't uniform either.
  • Assuming the input is random instead of randomising the algorithm, which is what makes the guarantee hold for every input.
  • Repeating runs that aren't independent, for example by reseeding with the same value, which makes amplification useless.

Exercise 15.1How many minimum cuts?

Use the analysis of Karger's algorithm to prove that a graph with n vertices has at most (n2) minimum cuts. Show that the cycle on n vertices has exactly that many.

Show solution

For each particular minimum cut, one run of the algorithm outputs exactly that cut with probability at least 2/(n(n−1))=1/(n2). A run outputs only one cut, so these events are disjoint for different cuts, and their probabilities sum to at most 1. So there are at most (n2) minimum cuts. ∎ In a cycle every cut has at least 2 edges, and removing any 2 of the n edges splits the cycle into two paths, so every pair of edges is a minimum cut: (n2) of them. On a cycle, Karger's algorithm always succeeds, because it always ends with some minimum cut, which shows that the bound for one particular cut can be far below the chance of finding a minimum cut.

Exercise 15.2Amplifying a primality test

A Miller–Rabin round declares a composite number "probably prime" with probability at most 1/4. How many rounds make the error at most 2−128? Why can't you do the same with a test whose errors go both ways?

Show solution

The errors are one-sided: a "composite" verdict comes with a witness and is always right, so the test is fooled only if every round is fooled, with probability at most (1/4)k=2−2k. For 2−128, k = 64 rounds. With two-sided errors, k runs can't simply be "anded" together, because a single wrong "yes" and a single wrong "no" are both possible. Instead take the majority of k runs. If each run is right with probability at least 2/3, the Chernoff bound shows the majority is wrong with probability e−ck for some constant c. That's still exponential in k, with a worse constant.

Exercise 15.3A shuffle that isn't uniform

This shuffle swaps each position with a random position anywhere in the list: for i in range(n): j = randrange(n); a[i], a[j] = a[j], a[i]. Show that it can't produce all n! orders with equal probability, and give the correct version.

Show solution

The loop makes n independent choices of n values each, so there are nn equally likely execution paths. If every one of the n! orders were equally likely, each would arise from nn/n! paths, which must be a whole number. For n = 3 that's 27/6, not an integer, so some orders are more likely than others. The correct Fisher–Yates shuffle draws j from the not-yet-fixed part only: for i in range(n - 1, 0, -1): j = randrange(i + 1), then swap. That gives n × (n − 1) × … × 1 = n! paths, one per order, and it's what Python's random.shuffle does. Counting execution paths is a quick way to show that a randomised procedure can't be uniform.

Chapter 16

Vectors, matrices and solving systems

10 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

Linear algebra is the mathematics of doing the same thing to many numbers at once. Vectors represent data: embeddings, pixels, features, word counts. Matrices represent linear transformations, and multiplying matrices composes transformations. Gaussian elimination solves systems of equations and reveals their rank, least squares fits a model to noisy data, and conditioning tells you when floating point will quietly ruin the answer. Watch 3Blue1Brown's Essence of Linear Algebra first for the geometry; this chapter adds the computation.

Vectors and the dot product

A vector in ℝn is a list of n numbers, added and scaled entry by entry. The dot product multiplies matching entries and adds them up, and it has a geometric meaning:

𝐮⋅𝐯=∑i=1nuivi=‖𝐮‖‖𝐯‖cosθ,

where ‖𝐮‖=𝐮⋅𝐮 is the length and θ the angle between the vectors. So the cosine similarity 𝐮⋅𝐯/(‖𝐮‖‖𝐯‖) measures how closely two vectors point the same way, ignoring their lengths. It's how search engines compare documents and how retrieval systems compare embeddings (The Engineer's Foundry). The example compares word-count vectors of three sentences. "The cat sat on the mat" and "the cat lay on the rug" score 0.75. A sentence about stocks still scores 0.47 against each, purely because all three share "the" and "on". That's why real systems discount common words, for example with tf–idf weighting, or use learned embeddings.

Matrices are transformations

A matrix A with n columns turns a vector 𝐱 into A𝐱, and this transformation is linear: A(a𝐱+b𝐲)=aA𝐱+bA𝐲. Linearity means a matrix is completely determined by what it does to the basis vectors 𝐞1,…,𝐞n, and those images are exactly its columns. A𝐱 is the combination of A's columns with weights x1,…,xn.

Applying B and then A is the single transformation AB, and that's the definition of matrix multiplication. Note the order: the matrix written on the right acts first. Because order matters for transformations, it matters for matrices. Rotating by 90° then stretching horizontally is not the same as stretching then rotating, and the example shows SR ≠ RS. The determinant is the factor by which a transformation scales areas, or volumes in higher dimensions, negative if it flips orientation. It multiplies, det(AB)=detAdetB, and it's zero exactly when the transformation squashes space into a lower dimension, losing information that can't be recovered.

Solving systems: Gaussian elimination and rank

A system of linear equations is a matrix equation A𝐱=𝐛. Gaussian elimination solves it using row operations that don't change the solutions: swap two rows, scale a row by a nonzero number, add a multiple of one row to another. Each column in turn gets a pivot, and the entries below it are cleared. The number of pivots is the rank of A, the number of genuinely independent equations, which is also the dimension of the space of A's outputs. There are exactly three possible outcomes.

  • Rank equals the number of unknowns: a unique solution.
  • A row reduces to "0 = nonzero": no solution, because the equations contradict each other.
  • Otherwise: infinitely many solutions, forming a space of dimension n − rank.

The example produces all three with exact fractions. In the last two systems the third row is the sum of the first two, so the rank is 2, and whether the right-hand side respects the same relationship decides between infinitely many solutions and none. A square matrix has an inverse exactly when its rank is full, equivalently when its determinant is nonzero. Elimination costs about n3/3 multiplications. To solve a system, eliminate. Don't compute A−1 and multiply, which costs more and loses more accuracy.

Least squares

Data rarely satisfies a model exactly. Fitting a line y = a + bx to 50 noisy points gives 50 equations in 2 unknowns, with no exact solution. Least squares chooses the parameters that minimise the sum of squared errors ‖Aβ−𝐲‖2. Geometrically, it projects 𝐲 onto the space spanned by A's columns, and the residual is then perpendicular to every column, which gives the normal equations:

ATAβ=AT𝐲.

From points generated by y = 2 + 0.5x plus noise, the example recovers y = 1.998 + 0.517x. Every linear regression is this computation. Libraries usually solve it with a QR decomposition instead of forming ATA, because forming it squares the condition number discussed next.

Conditioning: when floating point betrays you

Some systems are inherently sensitive: a tiny change in the data causes a large change in the solution. The condition number κ(A) measures this. In floating point, with relative precision about 10−16, you can lose up to log10κ digits of the answer, whatever algorithm you use. The classic example is the Hilbert matrix Hij=1/(i+j−1), whose rows are nearly parallel. The example solves H𝐱=H𝟏, whose exact answer is all ones, with careful floating-point elimination. The worst error is 4.6×10−13 for n = 4, 1.4×10−7 for n = 8, and 6×10−3 for n = 12, where κ is about 1016 and nearly every digit is gone. Exact fractions get every entry exactly 1, at a cost that grows quickly with n. The practical lessons are to use partial pivoting, which the example does, prefer well-conditioned formulations, and check the error, not just the residual: a solution can satisfy the equations to many digits and still be wrong.

All of it, from scratch

The example builds everything from lists and the standard library: cosine similarity, composition and determinants, exact Gaussian elimination, a least-squares fit, the Hilbert matrix in floating point and in fractions, and the cost of schoolbook multiplication.

Multiplying two n × n matrices the schoolbook way takes n3 multiplications, a billion for n = 1000. Strassen's algorithm (chapter 11) reduces the exponent to 2.807, and optimised libraries such as BLAS, and GPUs, make dense linear algebra fast by arranging those multiplications to suit caches and parallel hardware. That's why numerical Python code hands its matrices to NumPy rather than looping over lists as this example does.

Common traps
  • Assuming AB = BA. Matrix multiplication composes transformations, and order matters.
  • Computing an inverse to solve a system, instead of eliminating or factorising.
  • Deciding rank with floating-point equality. Use a tolerance, or exact arithmetic for small problems.
  • Ignoring conditioning and trusting a small residual as proof of an accurate answer.
  • Solving least squares by the normal equations on ill-conditioned data, which squares the condition number.
  • Mixing up rows and columns, or vectors of the wrong shape, the most common bug in matrix code.

Chapter 17

Eigenvectors: what a matrix does in the long run

10 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

Most vectors change direction when a matrix acts on them. Eigenvectors don't: they're only stretched, by a factor called the eigenvalue. Finding them simplifies the matrix, turning repeated application into repeated multiplication by numbers, which is why eigenvectors explain long-run behaviour. A Markov chain settles into its eigenvector for eigenvalue 1. PageRank is the dominant eigenvector of the web's link matrix. Fibonacci numbers grow like the golden ratio because that's an eigenvalue. Power iteration finds the dominant eigenvector by nothing more than multiplying repeatedly.

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

A nonzero vector 𝐯 is an eigenvector of a square matrix A, with eigenvalue λ, if

A𝐯=λ𝐯.

Rewriting this as (A−λI)𝐯=𝟎 shows that A−λI sends a nonzero vector to zero, so it must squash space, and chapter 16 says that happens exactly when its determinant is zero. The eigenvalues are therefore the roots of the characteristic polynomial det(A−λI)=0. For

A=(2112),det(A−λI)=(2−λ)2−1=λ2−4λ+3,

with roots 3 and 1. Solving (A−3I)𝐯=𝟎 gives 𝐯=(1,1), and (A−I)𝐯=𝟎 gives (1,−1).

Diagonalisation and powers

If an n × n matrix has n independent eigenvectors, put them in the columns of V and the eigenvalues on the diagonal of Λ. Then A=VΛV−1, and the middle factors cancel in a power:

Ak=VΛkV−1.

Raising a diagonal matrix to a power just raises each eigenvalue to that power. In the eigenvector coordinates, A is nothing but independent stretches, and in the long run the largest eigenvalue dominates everything. Fibonacci numbers are the standard example. The step (Fn+1,Fn)↦(Fn+2,Fn+1) is multiplication by (1110), whose characteristic polynomial λ2−λ−1 has roots φ and ψ, exactly chapter 11's characteristic equation. Diagonalising gives Binet's formula. For exact large Fibonacci numbers, raise the matrix to the n-th power by repeated squaring in integer arithmetic: the example gets all 209 digits of F1000 from 16 matrix multiplications, with none of the floating-point trouble chapter 11 found at n = 71. Not every matrix can be diagonalised. (1101) has only one eigenvector direction, and rotations of the plane have no real eigenvectors at all (exercise 17.2).

Power iteration

Start with almost any vector, multiply by A, rescale to length 1, and repeat. Write the starting vector in eigenvector coordinates as c1𝐯1+c2𝐯2+⋯. After k steps the components are ciλik, so the one with the largest |λ| wins, and the vector turns towards 𝐯1 with error shrinking like |λ2/λ1|k. That's power iteration, the simplest eigenvector algorithm, and it needs only matrix–vector products, so it works on enormous sparse matrices. On the matrix above, with ratio 1/3, it reaches 12 digits in 26 steps. The ratio is everything, though. The example's last lines need 27 iterations at ratio 0.5, 175 at 0.9 and 1,833 at 0.99, matching log(10−8)/log(ratio).

Markov chains

A Markov chain moves between states at random, with the next state depending only on the current one. Its transition matrix P has Pij as the probability of moving from state i to state j, so each row sums to 1. If 𝝅t is the row vector of probabilities at time t, then 𝝅t+1=𝝅tP. A stationary distribution satisfies 𝝅P=𝝅: it's an eigenvector, on the left, for eigenvalue 1. Take a weather model: a sunny day is followed by sun with probability 0.9, and a rainy day by sun with probability 0.5. Solving 𝝅P=𝝅 gives 𝝅=(5/6,1/6), so 5 days in 6 are sunny in the long run, whatever the starting day. Starting from rain, the example reaches 0.5 on day 1, 0.825 on day 5 and 0.833333 by day 30. If the chain can reach every state from every other, and doesn't cycle with a fixed period, the stationary distribution is unique and every starting point converges to it.

PageRank

In 1998 Sergey Brin and Larry Page ranked web pages with a Markov chain. Imagine a random surfer who, at each step, follows a random link on the current page with probability d = 0.85, and otherwise jumps to a page chosen uniformly at random. A page's PageRank is the long-run fraction of time the surfer spends there, the stationary distribution of this chain. That definition is recursive in a useful way. A page is important if important pages link to it, and the eigenvector equation is exactly what resolves the circularity. The random jumps make every page reachable from every other, which guarantees a unique answer, and they bound the second eigenvalue by d, so power iteration converges at a geometric rate. Real implementations also have to handle pages with no outgoing links, which the example's graph avoids.

The example's nine pages show why counting links isn't enough. The page "post2" has five incoming links, more than any other, but ranks only third. Three of its links come from spam pages that nobody links to, and a link from an unimportant page passes on little importance. "Home" and "blog", with three links each from pages that matter, rank first.

Symmetric matrices, like the one in the figure, are especially well behaved. The spectral theorem says their eigenvalues are real and their eigenvectors can be chosen perpendicular, so they're pure stretches along perpendicular axes. Covariance matrices are symmetric, and their eigenvectors are the principal components of a dataset, the directions of greatest variation, which is the idea behind principal component analysis and, more generally, the singular value decomposition.

Common traps
  • Assuming every matrix can be diagonalised. Some lack enough eigenvectors.
  • Expecting real eigenvalues from every real matrix. Rotations have complex ones.
  • Running power iteration when the top two eigenvalues are close, where convergence crawls.
  • Forgetting to normalise during power iteration, so the numbers overflow or vanish.
  • Using right eigenvectors for a Markov chain whose rows are the "from" states. The stationary distribution is a left eigenvector.
  • Ignoring dangling nodes in PageRank, pages without outgoing links, which leak probability out of the chain.

Chapter 18

Derivatives, gradients and optimisation

9 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

For an engineer, calculus is mostly about rates and optimisation. The derivative is the best linear approximation to a function, the gradient points uphill, and gradient descent walks downhill to minimise a loss, which is the engine inside machine learning. This chapter covers three ways to compute derivatives. By hand, with the rules. Numerically, with finite differences that fight a losing battle against floating point. And automatically, with dual numbers and backpropagation, which is how every machine learning framework does it. It ends with the step size that makes gradient descent diverge, and Newton's method, which doubles its correct digits at every step.

The derivative

The derivative of f at x is the limit of the slope between nearby points,

f′(x)=limh→0f(x+h)−f(x)h,

and it gives the best linear approximation near x: f(x+h)≈f(x)+f′(x)h. The rules for computing derivatives follow from the definition: sums differentiate term by term, the product rule gives (fg)′=f′g+fg′, and the chain rule gives

(f(g(x)))′=f′(g(x))⋅g′(x).

The chain rule is the one that matters most for computing. Any program built from elementary operations is a long composition of functions, and the chain rule says its derivative is the product of the derivatives of the steps, which is exactly what automatic differentiation exploits.

Numerical derivatives and their two errors

The obvious way to differentiate a function you can only evaluate is to pick a small h and compute the forward difference (f(x+h)−f(x))/h. It has two errors that pull in opposite directions. The truncation error comes from the curvature the straight line ignores, about h|f′′(x)|/2, so it shrinks as h shrinks. The rounding error comes from subtracting two nearly equal floating-point numbers, each correct to a relative precision ϵ≈10−16, and it's about ϵ|f(x)|/h, so it grows as h shrinks. Their sum is smallest when h≈ϵ≈10−8, and even then the derivative is good to only about 8 digits. The central difference (f(x+h)−f(x−h))/2h cancels the curvature term, leaving a truncation error proportional to h2. Its best h is about ϵ1/3≈10−5, with an error near 10−11.

Automatic differentiation

Automatic differentiation computes derivatives exactly, up to ordinary rounding, by applying the chain rule to the program itself. The neatest version uses dual numbers, numbers of the form a+bε with the rule ε2=0. Expanding a function at a+ε, every term beyond the linear one vanishes:

f(a+ε)=f(a)+f′(a)ε.

So if you implement addition, multiplication and the elementary functions on dual numbers, where sin(a+bε)=sina+bcosaε and so on, then running any program on the input x+1ε returns the function's value and its derivative at once. The example implements this in a dozen lines and differentiates xsinxex at x = 1, matching the derivative worked out by hand with the product rule to all 12 printed digits.

Dual numbers are forward mode, one pass per input variable. A neural network has millions of parameters and one loss, and it needs the derivative of the loss with respect to every parameter. Reverse mode does that in a single backward pass. It records the computation, then applies the chain rule from the output back towards the inputs, accumulating each intermediate's contribution. Reverse mode applied to neural networks is backpropagation, and it's what PyTorch and JAX compute when you ask for a gradient.

Gradients

For a function of several variables, the partial derivative ∂f/∂x is the derivative with respect to one variable with the others held fixed. Collected into a vector, the partials form the gradient:

∇f=(∂f∂x1,…,∂f∂xn).

The gradient points in the direction of steepest increase, and its length is the rate of increase in that direction, because the linear approximation f(𝐱+𝐡)≈f(𝐱)+∇f⋅𝐡 is a dot product, largest when 𝐡 points along ∇f (chapter 16). At a minimum of a smooth function the gradient is zero. The converse fails: a zero gradient can also mark a maximum or a saddle point, rising in one direction and falling in another.

Gradient descent

To minimise f, repeatedly step against the gradient:

𝐱k+1=𝐱k−η∇f(𝐱k),

where η is the step size, called the learning rate in machine learning. Its choice is critical. On a quadratic bowl whose steepest curvature is L, each step multiplies the error along that direction by 1−ηL, so the iteration converges only if 0<η<2/L (exercise 18.2). The example minimises x2+10y2, where L = 20, so the limit is 0.1. A tiny step of 0.001 needs 6,901 iterations, 0.05 needs 132, and 0.09 needs 70. At 0.099 the y-coordinate overshoots and oscillates, taking 627 iterations, and at 0.101 it diverges. The ratio between the steepest and the flattest curvature, here 20/2 = 10, is the problem's condition number. The step must suit the steepest direction, so progress along the flattest direction is slow, and that's why badly scaled features slow down model training. A function is convex if the segment between any two points on its graph lies above the graph. For convex functions every local minimum is global, and gradient descent with a suitable step finds it. Neural network losses aren't convex, and practice relies on variants such as stochastic gradient descent, momentum and Adam that cope with noise, saddles and poor conditioning.

Newton's method

To solve f(x) = 0, replace f by its linear approximation at the current guess and solve that instead:

xk+1=xk−f(xk)f′(xk).

Near a simple root, Newton's method converges quadratically: the error is roughly squared at each step, so the number of correct digits doubles. For 2, with f(x)=x2−2, the iteration is x←(x+2/x)/2, the Babylonian method, and from x = 1 the example's correct digits go 1, 2, 5, 11, 24, 48. Applied to the gradient instead of f, Newton's method minimises using curvature as well as slope, converging in very few steps. In high dimensions the matrix of second derivatives is too large to form and invert, which is why first-order methods like gradient descent dominate machine learning.

Common traps
  • Making h as small as possible in a finite difference, past the point where rounding error dominates.
  • Dropping the inner derivative of the chain rule, the most common hand-differentiation mistake.
  • A learning rate above 2/L, which diverges, or far below it, which crawls.
  • Treating a zero gradient as a minimum. It may be a maximum or a saddle.
  • Differentiating at a kink, such as |x| or ReLU at 0, where the derivative doesn't exist and libraries pick a convention.
  • Checking a gradient implementation only on easy points. Compare it with central differences at random points.

Chapter 19

Sampling, estimation and confidence intervals

9 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

Statistics turns a sample into a statement about a population, with an honest account of the uncertainty. This chapter covers estimators and bias, which is why the sample variance divides by n − 1, and the square-root law for standard errors. The central limit theorem explains why averages are approximately normal even when the data isn't, and confidence intervals are built on it. A 95% interval promises something more modest than most people think. The bootstrap gives an interval when no formula exists. The chapter ends with sampling bias, the error that no amount of data reduces.

Populations, samples and estimators

A parameter describes a population, such as the true conversion rate of every visitor or the mean latency of every request. A statistic is computed from a sample, and an estimator is a statistic used to guess a parameter. An estimator is unbiased if its expectation equals the parameter. By linearity of expectation (chapter 13), the sample mean x¯ is an unbiased estimator of the population mean μ.

The obvious estimator of the variance, the average squared deviation from x¯, is biased low. The sample mean sits in the middle of its own sample, so deviations from it are systematically smaller than deviations from the true mean. Working through the expectation gives 𝔼[∑i(xi−x¯)2]=(n−1)σ2, so dividing by n − 1 instead of n makes the estimate unbiased. That's Bessel's correction, and it's why statistics.stdev and statistics.pstdev give different answers. The example estimates the variance of a die, 35/12 ≈ 2.917, from samples of 5: dividing by n averages 2.340, a fifth too low, and dividing by n − 1 averages 2.925.

Standard error and the square-root law

The mean of n independent observations, each with variance σ2, has variance σ2/n (chapter 13: variances of independent variables add, and dividing by n divides the variance by n2). Its standard deviation, the standard error, is

SE(x¯)=σn.

Precision grows with the square root of the sample size: four times the data halves the error, and a hundred times the data gains one decimal digit. That square root governs the cost of every measurement and every experiment. The example's means of n exponential values have standard deviations 0.445, 0.183 and 0.101 for n = 5, 30 and 100, matching 1/n.

The central limit theorem

The central limit theorem says that the mean of n independent, identically distributed observations with finite variance is approximately normal for large n, with mean μ and standard deviation σ/n, whatever the shape of the original distribution. That's why normal-based methods work on averages of data that is anything but normal. "Large" depends on the data. The exponential distribution is strongly skewed, with skewness 2, and the skewness of a mean of n values shrinks like 2/n. The example measures 0.87 at n = 5, 0.35 at n = 30 and 0.21 at n = 100. For very skewed or heavy-tailed data, such as request latencies or revenue per user, much larger samples are needed, and for distributions with infinite variance the theorem doesn't apply at all.

Confidence intervals

Since x¯ is approximately normal with standard deviation σ/n, it lies within 1.96 standard errors of μ with probability 95%. Turning that around gives the 95% confidence interval

x¯±1.96sn,

with the sample standard deviation s in place of the unknown σ. What "95%" means is subtle. The procedure produces intervals that contain the true value in 95% of repeated samples. Any particular interval either contains it or doesn't. The figure shows 20 such intervals from the same population.

Replacing σ by s adds uncertainty that matters for small samples. William Gosset, a chemist at the Guinness brewery publishing as "Student" in 1908, worked out the exact distribution for normal data, the t distribution, whose critical values exceed 1.96: 2.776 for samples of 5, 2.045 for samples of 30. The example checks coverage over 10,000 samples. With n = 5 from a normal population, the 1.96 interval covers only 87.6% of the time and the t interval 95.1%. With skewed data, even the t interval falls short, 88.4% at n = 5 and 92.9% at n = 30, because its derivation assumed normality. The method's assumptions are part of the method.

The bootstrap

For a median, a 90th percentile or a ratio of two means, there's often no convenient formula for the standard error. Bradley Efron's bootstrap (1979) estimates it by simulation. Treat the sample as a stand-in for the population, draw many new samples of the same size from it with replacement, compute the statistic on each, and take the middle 95% of the results as the interval. The example does this for the median of 50 skewed values with 5,000 resamples and gets (0.688, 1.123), which just contains the true median ln 2 ≈ 0.693. The bootstrap is easy to apply to almost any statistic, and it inherits whatever is wrong with the sample, so it can't fix a biased one.

Bias you can't compute away

Every formula above assumes the sample is random. When it isn't, more data only makes you more confident in the wrong answer, because a larger sample shrinks the variance and leaves the bias untouched. In 1936 the Literary Digest polled over two million readers, drawn largely from telephone directories and car registrations, and predicted a landslide for Alf Landon. George Gallup, with a far smaller but more representative sample, correctly predicted Franklin Roosevelt's victory. In software the same mistake takes quieter forms. A study of the users who answered a survey describes the people who answer surveys. Latency measured only on successful requests misses the ones that timed out, and an analysis of active accounts ignores everyone who churned, which is survivorship bias.

Common traps
  • Reading a 95% interval as "95% probability the true value is inside". The 95% describes the procedure, not this interval.
  • Using 1.96 with tiny samples, where the t distribution is needed, and normality matters.
  • Applying normal-based intervals to heavy-tailed data such as latencies, without checking.
  • Dividing by n when estimating a variance from a sample.
  • Believing a bigger sample cures bias. It only makes a biased answer look more precise.
  • Concluding two groups differ, or don't, from whether their intervals overlap. Compute the interval for the difference instead.

Exercise 19.1The mean's mean and variance

For independent observations x1,…,xn with mean μ and variance σ2, prove that 𝔼[x¯]=μ and Var[x¯]=σ2/n.

Show solution

By linearity, 𝔼[x¯]=1n∑i𝔼[xi]=1n⋅nμ=μ. For the variance, scaling a variable by c scales its variance by c2, and variances of independent variables add, so Var[x¯]=1n2∑iVar[xi]=1n2⋅nσ2=σ2/n. ∎ The first result needs no independence. The second does. With positively correlated observations, such as repeated measurements of the same user, the variance of the mean is larger, and treating them as independent produces intervals that are too narrow.

Exercise 19.2How big a sample?

You want to estimate a conversion rate that's around 10% to within ±0.5 percentage points, with 95% confidence. How many visitors do you need?

Show solution

A proportion p estimated from n visitors has standard error p(1−p)/n. We need 1.960.1×0.9/n≤0.005, so n≥1.962×0.09/0.0052≈13,830. Halving the margin to ±0.25 points would need four times as many, about 55,000, which is the square-root law again. If you don't know p in advance, use p = 0.5, which maximises p(1 − p) and so gives a sample size that's always enough.

Exercise 19.3An interval for latency

A sample of 1,000 request latencies has mean 120 ms and standard deviation 300 ms. Is 120±1.96×300/1000, about 120 ± 19 ms, a trustworthy 95% interval for the mean latency? What would you report instead?

Show solution

A standard deviation 2.5 times the mean signals a heavily skewed, long-tailed distribution: most requests are fast and a few are very slow. With 1,000 observations the central limit theorem helps, but convergence is slow for such data, so the interval's real coverage may fall short of 95%, and a few extreme requests can move the mean a lot. A bootstrap interval is a useful check. More importantly, the mean may be the wrong summary. Users experience the distribution, so report percentiles such as the median, p95 and p99, each with a bootstrap interval if precision matters. A mean of 120 ms is compatible with one request in a hundred taking several seconds.

Chapter 20

A/B tests and Simpson's paradox

10 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

An A/B test is a randomised controlled experiment, the most reliable way to learn whether a change causes an improvement. The statistics fits on a page: a two-proportion test, a p-value and a sample-size formula. Most failed experiments fail on process instead. Tests are too small to detect the effect they're looking for. People check every morning and stop at the first good day, or test twenty metrics and report the one that moved. And they compare groups that weren't randomised, where Simpson's paradox can make every subgroup say one thing and the total say the opposite.

Why randomise

Observational data shows correlations, and correlations have many possible causes. Users who enable a feature may convert more because the feature helps, or because the kind of user who explores settings was going to convert anyway. Assigning users to versions A and B at random breaks every such link. Any difference in who ends up in each group, motivation, device, country, is due to chance alone, and chance is exactly what statistics can quantify. So a difference between the groups beyond what chance explains is caused by the change itself. That's why randomised experiments are the standard of evidence, from drug trials to product changes.

Hypothesis tests and p-values

Version A converts 1,000 of 10,000 users, 10.0%, and version B converts 1,080 of 10,000, 10.8%. Is B better, or was it luck? Start from the null hypothesis that there's no difference, and ask how surprising the data would be if it were true. Under the null, both groups share one rate, estimated by pooling them as p^=2080/20000=0.104, and the difference in observed rates has standard error p^(1−p^)(1/nA+1/nB)≈0.0043. The test statistic is

z=p^B−p^Ap^(1−p^)(1nA+1nB)=0.0080.0043≈1.85.

The p-value is the probability, if the null hypothesis were true, of a difference at least this large in either direction. Here it's 0.064. By the usual convention, a result is significant if p < 0.05, so this one isn't. Three readings of that number are wrong. A p-value isn't the probability that the null hypothesis is true. It isn't the probability that B is no better. And "not significant" doesn't mean "no effect". The 95% interval for the lift, from −0.05 to +1.65 percentage points, includes zero, but it also includes improvements that would be well worth having. The experiment was simply too small to tell. The significance level, 5%, is the false-positive rate you accept: if there's truly no effect, 5% of experiments will still come out "significant".

Power and sample size

The power of a test is the probability of detecting an effect that's really there. To detect a lift from rate p1 to p2 with significance level α and power 1−β, each group needs about

n=(z1−α/2+z1−β)2(p1(1−p1)+p2(1−p2))(p2−p1)2

users, where the z values are standard normal quantiles, 1.96 and 0.84 for the conventional 5% and 80%. Detecting 10% → 11% takes 14,749 users per group. The effect size sits squared in the denominator, so halving the lift you want to detect quadruples the sample: 57,760 per group for 10% → 10.5%, against 3,839 for 10% → 12%. Decide the sample size before the experiment starts. An underpowered test mostly returns "not significant", and when it does come out significant, the estimated effect is likely to be exaggerated, because only the lucky overestimates crossed the threshold.

Peeking

The most common way to ruin an experiment is to check it repeatedly and stop as soon as it looks significant. Each check is another chance for noise to cross the threshold, and stopping at the first crossing keeps the false alarms and discards the corrections. The example runs 2,000 A/A tests, comparisons of two identical versions where any "win" is false by construction. Checked once at the end, 4.7% come out significant, close to the 5% promised. Checked after every 500 users and stopped at the first p < 0.05, 25.6% do. If you need to monitor an experiment as it runs, fix the number of looks in advance and spend the 5% across them, or use sequential methods designed for continuous monitoring, which several experimentation platforms provide.

Many comparisons

Test 20 independent metrics on a change that does nothing, and the chance that at least one is "significant" at 5% is 1−0.9520≈64%. The example's 5,000 simulated experiments give 63.6%. Report the one metric that moved, and you've reported noise. The Bonferroni correction tests each of m metrics at α/m, here 0.0025, which brings the chance of any false positive back to about 5%. Less conservative procedures control the expected share of false discoveries instead. The simplest defence is procedural: choose one primary metric before the experiment, and treat the rest as exploration that needs its own follow-up experiment.

Simpson's paradox

A 1986 study in the British Medical Journal compared treatments for kidney stones. Open surgery (A) succeeded in 78% of its 350 patients and a less invasive percutaneous procedure (B) in 83% of its 350. B looks better. But split by stone size, A was better for small stones, 93% against 87%, and better for large stones, 73% against 69%.

This is Simpson's paradox. There's no arithmetic error. Each overall rate is a weighted average of its group rates, A's weighted 75% towards large stones and B's 77% towards small ones, so the overall comparison mostly compares case mixes, not treatments. Stone size is a confounder: it influenced which treatment doctors chose, and it influenced the outcome. Here the subgroup comparison is the right one, because stone size was known before treatment and affects both. That depends on the causal story, though, not on the arithmetic, and the same numbers could call for the overall comparison if the subgroup variable were itself a result of the treatment.

Randomisation prevents the paradox in a properly run A/B test, because it balances confounders across the groups. It returns whenever you compare groups that weren't randomised: users who chose a feature against users who didn't, this month against last month when the traffic mix has shifted between platforms or countries, or segments sliced from an experiment after the fact.

Common traps
  • Peeking and stopping at the first significant result, which multiplies the false-positive rate.
  • Reading a p-value as the probability that the null hypothesis is true.
  • Reading "not significant" as "no effect", especially from an underpowered test.
  • Testing many metrics and reporting the winner without correcting for the number of tests.
  • Comparing groups that weren't randomised, where confounders and Simpson's paradox take over.
  • Ignoring a sample ratio mismatch. Groups meant to be equal that aren't usually mean a bug in the assignment (exercise 20.3).

Exercise 20.1A borderline test

A has 500 conversions from 5,000 users and B has 560 from 5,000. Compute the z statistic and the two-sided p-value, and say what you'd do next.

Show solution

The rates are 10.0% and 11.2%, and the pooled rate is 1060/10000 = 0.106. The standard error is 0.106×0.894×(2/5000)≈0.00616, so z = 0.012/0.00616 ≈ 1.95, and the two-sided p-value is about 0.051, just above 0.05. Don't round it down, and don't extend the test until it crosses the line, which is peeking. The honest summary is that the data is suggestive but inconclusive, with a 95% interval for the lift of roughly 0 to 2.4 points. If the decision matters, run a new experiment sized in advance for the smallest lift worth detecting.

Exercise 20.2Simpson's paradox as weighted averages

Write each treatment's overall success rate in the kidney stone study as a weighted average of its two group rates, and explain the reversal from the weights.

Show solution

Each overall rate is a weighted average of the group rates, weighted by how many patients were in each group:

A:87350(0.931)+263350(0.730)=0.249×0.931+0.751×0.730=0.78,
B:270350(0.867)+80350(0.688)=0.771×0.867+0.229×0.688=0.826.

A's group rates are higher in both groups, but three quarters of A's weight sits on the large-stone rate, the lower one, while three quarters of B's weight sits on the small-stone rate, the higher one. A weighted average can reverse a comparison whenever the weights differ this much. A fair overall comparison applies the same weights to both treatments, for example the share of small and large stones among all 700 patients, which ranks A first.

Exercise 20.3A suspicious split

An experiment meant to assign users 50/50 ends with 50,600 users in A and 49,400 in B. Could that be chance? What should you do with its results?

Show solution

Under a fair 50/50 split of 100,000 users, the count in A is binomial with mean 50,000 and standard deviation 100,000×0.25≈158. An excess of 600 is 3.8 standard deviations, with a two-sided p-value of about 0.00015. That's almost certainly not chance. A sample ratio mismatch like this usually means users are being lost or duplicated unequally between the arms: a redirect that drops some B users, a bot filter applied to one arm, a crash in the new version that keeps some users from being logged. The missing users are unlikely to be typical, so the comparison is no longer between randomised groups. Find the cause before trusting any metric from the experiment. Experimentation platforms check for this automatically for exactly this reason.

The twenty rules

One per chapter, each linked back to it. Read them before you write a proof, analyse an algorithm, or trust a number someone hands you.

  1. An implication fails only when its premise holds and its conclusion doesn't; never trade it for its converse.
  2. Examples can disprove a claim but never prove one; search for counterexamples first, then prove.
  3. Every correct loop has an invariant and every correct recursion has an induction proof; write them down.
  4. Equality you hash on must be an equivalence relation, and dependencies must form a partial order.
  5. Divide in modular arithmetic only by numbers with an inverse, and find inverses with Euclid's algorithm.
  6. Understand the mathematics of cryptography, and never deploy your own implementation of it.
  7. Know which graph problems are easy and which merely look easy: every edge once is easy, every vertex once is NP-complete.
  8. A greedy colouring is an upper bound, a stable matching favours whoever proposes, and an edge count can prove a graph isn't planar.
  9. Count each object exactly once, and check every counting formula against brute force on a small case.
  10. For "at least one", count the complement; for overlaps, include and exclude.
  11. Turn loops into sums and recursion into recurrences, and say Θ when you mean a tight bound.
  12. Draw the tree, respect the base rate, and condition on exactly what you learned.
  13. Break a count into indicators and add their probabilities; linearity of expectation needs no independence.
  14. Bound the tail, not just the average, and remember that collisions arrive after about the square root of the space.
  15. Randomise the algorithm rather than hoping for random input, and amplify a small chance of success by repetition.
  16. Read a matrix as a transformation, solve systems by elimination, and check conditioning before trusting floating point.
  17. Long-run behaviour belongs to the dominant eigenvector, and the eigenvalue ratio decides how long the run is.
  18. Differentiate automatically when you can, keep the step size below 2/L, and never push a finite difference's h towards zero.
  19. A sample shrinks error like one over the square root of its size, and no size cures a biased sample.
  20. Randomise, size the test in advance, don't peek, choose one primary metric, and beware comparisons that weren't randomised.
