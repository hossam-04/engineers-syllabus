How to use this

This is a map, not a schedule. The order is chosen so that each stage rests on something you already understand, which is why it is worth following even when a later stage looks more exciting. Seven rules decide whether you finish it.

If you already have a degree or a few years of work behind you, skip ahead to where to start and take the diagnostic first.

  • 01
    Build more than you read. Every stage below ends with something you ship. A stage is not finished when you have watched the lectures. It is finished when the project runs and someone other than you has used it.
  • 02
    Budget 100 to 200 hours per major subject. This is the figure the Teach Yourself Computer Science authors settled on, and it holds up. Anything under that produces recognition without understanding.
  • 03
    One language, all the way down, before a second. Depth in one language transfers. A shallow acquaintance with six does not. Pick Python or JavaScript and stay there until stage 07.
  • 04
    Never run more than two stages at once. Mathematics pairs well with algorithms. Craft pairs well with anything. Three concurrent subjects means three abandoned subjects.
  • 05
    Write about what you learn, in public. A learning log forces you to articulate the thing you half-understand. It is also, in practice, the strongest hiring signal a self-taught engineer can produce.
  • 06
    Let the assistant write code only after you can read it. Coding agents are leverage on a foundation and a liability without one. The rule that keeps you honest: you must be able to explain every line you ship.
  • 07
    Consistency beats intensity. Ten focused hours a week for three years outruns forty sporadic hours for six months. Protect the streak, not the session.

The prerequisite spine

Most of the curriculum is reorderable. These dependencies are not. Architecture is what makes operating systems and databases legible instead of memorised, and distributed systems are incoherent until you know what a process and a packet actually are.

Time budget

Hours below are study hours, not calendar time. They assume focused work with a project attached, not lectures playing in a browser tab.

One honest caveat. The last column buys you the knowledge of a senior engineer, not the title. Seniority also requires years of shipping alongside other people, absorbing production incidents, and being wrong in public. No reading list shortens that part.

Where to start if you have a degree

A computer science degree covers stages 01 through 08 and largely skips 09 through 15. You were taught what a B-tree is. You were probably never made to refactor someone else's code under test, deploy it, or be woken up when it failed. That gap is the whole of the applied half of this document, and it is what hiring screens for now.

So the short answer is stage 09, after a one-week sweep of stage 00. But a degree certifies attendance, not retention, and the course you took in second year has decayed. Verify rather than assume. Each test below is one question you can answer honestly in an afternoon, and failing one means that stage is not behind you.

The compressed track

A stage you once passed takes roughly a fifth of the listed hours to restore. Re-read your notes, build the one project, and move on. Do not re-study from the top. That turns 2,720 hours into something closer to 1,640.

Sweep 00 – 08 400 h Refresher pace. Projects only, skip the lectures.
Full effort 09, 10, 12 680 h Craft, ship a product, then operate it in production.
Then 11, 13, 14 560 h Distributed systems, security, AI engineering.
Graduate total ~1,640 h 1 yr 7 mo at 20 h per week.

One warning specific to your position. Graduates tend to jump straight to stage 11, because distributed systems feels like the prestigious part and the coursework flattered it. It does not stick before you have operated something real. Kleppmann reads as trivia until you have been woken at three in the morning by a replication lag alert.

Stages 09, 10 and 12 are the whole distance between a computer science graduate and an employable engineer. Spend the 680 hours there first, in that order.

Milestone AYou can build things
stages 00 – 02, 09, 10

Roughly 1,070 hours. You can take a vague idea, decompose it, build it, test it, and put it in front of a user. This is the point where a junior role becomes realistic.

STAGE 00 Foundation

Tools, the shell, and how to learn

4 weeks60 hoursNo prerequisites

Almost nobody teaches this, and it quietly costs beginners hundreds of hours. Before you learn to program, learn to operate the machine you will program on. Every stage after this runs faster because of it.

Companion volumeThe Engineer's Whetstone16 chapters · 16 code examples · 48 exercises0 / 16 readOpen the volume →

What you learn

  • The shell as a language: pipes, redirection, globbing, exit codes, grep, find, sed, awk
  • The filesystem model: paths, permissions, symlinks, environment variables, PATH
  • Git as a data structure, not a list of commands: commits, trees, refs, branching, rebase, merge conflicts, bisect
  • Collaborating on GitHub: forks, pull requests, review comments, issues
  • One editor to fluency, with its keybindings, search, and multi-file navigation
  • Reading documentation and error messages instead of pasting them somewhere
  • Asking a question well: minimal reproduction, what you tried, what you expected
  • Study technique: spaced repetition for facts, deliberate practice for skills, retrieval over rereading

STAGE 01 Foundation

Programming foundations

3–4 months300 hoursAfter stage 00

The longest single stage, and the one people rush hardest. The goal is not syntax. It is the ability to hold a problem in your head, break it into parts, and express those parts as code that someone else can read. Do not be the person who never quite understood recursion.

Companion volumeThe Engineer's Primer20 chapters · 20 code examples · 60 exercises0 / 20 readOpen the volume →

What you learn

  • Values, types, variables, expressions, statements, scope
  • Control flow, iteration, and the loop invariants that make it correct
  • Functions as the unit of abstraction: arguments, return values, purity, side effects
  • Recursion, base cases, the call stack, and why deep recursion blows up
  • Collections: lists, dictionaries, sets, tuples, and the cost of each operation
  • Strings, encodings, and why UTF-8 exists
  • Errors and exceptions: raising, catching, and failing loudly at the right boundary
  • Files, streams, serialisation, JSON and CSV
  • Objects and classes, composition, interfaces, and when not to use inheritance
  • Automated testing from week one, with a real test runner
  • Debugging with a debugger: breakpoints, stepping, inspecting state
  • Modules, packages, dependencies, virtual environments, lockfiles
  • Memory: the stack, the heap, references, mutation, and aliasing bugs
  • Reading unfamiliar code and changing it without breaking it

Resources

  • Course
    CS50x Harvard · start in C. The pointer chapter is the point
  • Course
    Berkeley CS 61A abstraction, recursion, interpreters · do this or SICP
  • Book
    Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs Abelson & Sussman · chapters 1–3 with exercises
  • Practice
    Exercism ~100 small problems with human mentorship
  • Course
    The Odin Project if the web is your destination, use this as the spine

Ship this

Four programs, each in a different shape. A command-line tool with arguments and a help screen. A program that reads messy real-world data and produces a clean report. A small game with state. A script that talks to a public API and caches its results.

Each with tests, a README, and no framework doing the thinking for you.

STAGE 02 Theory

Data structures and algorithms

3–4 months250 hoursAfter stage 01

This is the vocabulary of the field. Not because interviews ask for it, though they do, but because a problem you can name is a problem you can look up. Implement every structure once, from nothing, before you ever use the library version again.

Companion volumeThe Engineer's Workbench8 chapters · 8 code examples · 24 exercises0 / 8 readOpen the volume →

What you learn

  • Asymptotic analysis: big-O, big-theta, amortised cost, space complexity
  • Arrays, dynamic arrays, and why appending is amortised constant time
  • Linked lists, stacks, queues, deques, circular buffers
  • Hash tables: hashing, collision resolution, load factor, resizing
  • Trees: binary search trees, balancing, heaps, priority queues, tries
  • Graphs: adjacency lists and matrices, BFS, DFS, topological sort, cycle detection
  • Shortest paths: Dijkstra, Bellman–Ford, A*; minimum spanning trees
  • Union-find with path compression
  • Sorting: merge, quick, heap, insertion, counting and radix; stability; when each wins
  • Binary search and its off-by-one traps, including search over answer space
  • Recursion to divide-and-conquer to dynamic programming: memoisation, tabulation, state design
  • Greedy algorithms and the exchange argument that proves them
  • Backtracking, pruning, constraint problems
  • Two pointers, sliding window, prefix sums, monotonic stacks
  • Bit manipulation and its idioms
  • NP-completeness and reductions: recognising when no efficient answer exists
  • Probabilistic structures: Bloom filters, count-min sketch, HyperLogLog

Resources

  • Book
    The Algorithm Design Manual Skiena · the war stories are the reason to pick this over CLRS
  • Course
    MIT 6.006 Introduction to Algorithms full lectures, notes and problem sets
  • Course
    Stanford Algorithms Tim Roughgarden · four-part specialisation
  • Practice
    NeetCode 150 a curated set beats grinding 500 random problems
  • Book
    Introduction to Algorithms CLRS · a reference to look things up in, not to read cover to cover

Ship this

A library implementing every structure above from scratch, with tests and complexity documented per method. Then solve around 150 curated problems, and for each one you fail, write down which pattern you missed.

STAGE 03 Theory

Mathematics for computing

2–3 months150 hoursRun in parallel with stage 02

Computer science is applied mathematics that got out of hand. You do not need a mathematics degree, but the discrete half is not optional, and proof by induction is the same muscle as recursion. Run this alongside algorithms, not before them.

Companion volumeThe Engineer's Compass20 chapters · 20 code examples · 60 exercises0 / 20 readOpen the volume →

What you learn

  • Logic and proof: implication, quantifiers, contradiction, and induction
  • Sets, relations, functions, equivalence classes, partial orders
  • Combinatorics: permutations, combinations, pigeonhole, inclusion–exclusion
  • Discrete probability: conditional probability, Bayes, expectation, variance, linearity of expectation
  • Common distributions and tail bounds; randomised algorithm analysis
  • Graph theory as mathematics: connectivity, colouring, matchings, planarity
  • Number theory: modular arithmetic, GCD, primes, and the road to public-key cryptography
  • Recurrence relations and the master theorem
  • Linear algebra: vectors, matrices, transformations, rank, eigenvectors
  • Calculus to the level of derivatives, gradients, and optimisation
  • Statistics you will actually use: sampling, confidence intervals, A/B tests, Simpson's paradox

Resources

  • Video

    MIT 18.06 Linear Algebra Gilbert Strang
  • Book
    Think Stats Downey · statistics through code rather than notation

STAGE 04 Systems

Computer architecture

2–3 months150 hoursGateway to stages 05, 06, 08

Without a solid mental model of how the machine works, every higher abstraction you learn stays brittle. This is the stage that turns performance from superstition into arithmetic, and it unlocks operating systems and databases behind it.

Companion volumeThe Engineer's Mill20 chapters · 20 code examples · 60 exercises0 / 20 readOpen the volume →

What you learn

  • Number representation: binary, hex, two's complement, overflow
  • IEEE 754 floating point, and why 0.1 + 0.2 is not 0.3
  • Boolean logic, gates, multiplexers, adders, the ALU
  • Sequential logic: flip-flops, registers, the clock
  • Instruction set architecture, machine code, and reading assembly
  • The fetch-decode-execute cycle; a datapath and a control unit
  • The memory hierarchy: registers, L1/L2/L3, RAM, disk, and their real latencies
  • Cache lines, locality, associativity, and cache-friendly data layout
  • Pipelining, hazards, branch prediction, speculative execution
  • Instruction-level and data-level parallelism, SIMD
  • How a program becomes a process: compiling, assembling, linking, loading
  • The stack frame, calling conventions, and how buffer overflows happen
  • I/O, interrupts, DMA, the system bus

Resources

  • Course
    Nand2Tetris, parts I and II build a working computer from a NAND gate upward
  • Book
    Computer Systems: A Programmer's Perspective Bryant & O'Hallaron · if you read one systems book, this
  • Course
    Berkeley CS 61C Great Ideas in Computer Architecture
  • Book
    Computer Organization and Design Patterson & Hennessy · the RISC-V edition

Ship this

The full Nand2Tetris stack: gates, ALU, CPU, assembler, virtual machine, and a compiler for its language. It is the first project most people build that is complex enough to make software engineering feel necessary.

Then take one slow program and make it four times faster using only cache locality.

STAGE 05 Systems

Operating systems and concurrency

2–3 months150 hoursRequires stage 04

Every line of code you write is run by an operating system, and most production incidents are arguments with it. Concurrency lives here too, and it is the subject where confident guessing does the most damage.

Companion volumeThe Engineer's Loom20 chapters · 20 code examples · 60 exercises0 / 20 readOpen the volume →

What you learn

  • Processes: address space, fork and exec, process states, signals
  • Threads, context switching, kernel versus user mode, system calls
  • Scheduling: round robin, priority, multi-level feedback, fairness
  • Concurrency primitives: mutexes, semaphores, condition variables, atomics
  • Race conditions, deadlock, livelock, starvation, and the four Coffman conditions
  • Concurrency models beyond threads: event loops, async/await, actors, CSP channels
  • Virtual memory: paging, page tables, the TLB, page faults, swapping
  • Memory allocation, fragmentation, garbage collection strategies
  • File systems: inodes, directories, journalling, durability and fsync
  • I/O models: blocking, non-blocking, multiplexed, asynchronous; epoll and kqueue
  • Interprocess communication: pipes, sockets, shared memory, message queues
  • Virtualisation and containers: namespaces, cgroups, what Docker actually is

STAGE 06 Systems

Networking and the web

6–8 weeks120 hoursRequires stage 04

Almost everything you build will be reached over a network, and almost every mysterious latency problem lives in this stage. Learn it top-down, starting from HTTP, which is where your work touches it.

Companion volumeThe Engineer's Exchange20 chapters · 23 code examples · 60 exercises0 / 20 readOpen the volume →

What you learn

  • The layered model and encapsulation; what each layer is actually responsible for
  • HTTP in depth: methods, status codes, headers, caching, cookies, content negotiation
  • HTTP/2 multiplexing and HTTP/3 over QUIC; head-of-line blocking
  • TCP: the handshake, sequence numbers, retransmission, flow control, congestion control
  • UDP and when losing packets is the right trade
  • IP addressing, subnets, routing, NAT, IPv6
  • DNS: resolution, record types, TTLs, and why it is always DNS
  • TLS: handshake, certificates, chains of trust, certificate authorities, mTLS
  • Sockets: the API underneath every client and server you have used

  • RPC over the network: gRPC, protocol buffers, schema evolution

STAGE 07 Systems

Languages, interpreters and compilers

6–8 weeks120 hoursRequires stages 01, 04

Understanding how a language is implemented makes every language easier to pick up and makes your own code better, because you stop guessing what the runtime does. It is also the most enjoyable stage in this document.

  • Tree-walking interpreters versus bytecode virtual machines
  • Intermediate representations, optimisation passes, code generation, register allocation
  • Runtime memory management: reference counting, tracing collectors, generational GC
  • Closures, first-class functions, tail calls, continuations
  • Paradigms compared: imperative, object-oriented, functional, logic
  • Immutability, algebraic data types, pattern matching, and what they buy you
  • Foreign function interfaces and language interoperation

STAGE 08 Systems

Databases and data modelling

2–3 months180 hoursRequires stage 04

Data sits at the centre of almost every program worth writing, and very few engineers understand how the database actually executes their query. This stage pays for itself the first time you read an execution plan instead of adding an index at random.

Companion volumeThe Engineer's Ledger20 chapters · 22 code examples · 60 exercises0 / 20 readOpen the volume →

What you learn

  • The relational model, keys, referential integrity, normal forms, and when to denormalise
  • SQL properly: joins, subqueries, CTEs, window functions, grouping sets, set operations
  • Indexes: B-trees, LSM trees, composite and covering indexes, selectivity, index-only scans
  • Query planning and execution: reading EXPLAIN ANALYZE, join strategies, statistics
  • Transactions and ACID; isolation levels; the anomalies each one permits
  • Concurrency control: two-phase locking, MVCC, optimistic concurrency, deadlock handling
  • Durability: write-ahead logging, checkpoints, crash recovery, replication lag
  • Storage engines: row stores versus column stores, page layout, compression
  • Schema design and safe online migrations under live traffic
  • NoSQL families: document, key-value, wide-column, graph, time-series, and the fit for each
  • Caching: Redis, cache-aside and write-through, TTLs, stampedes, invalidation
  • Full-text search and inverted indexes
  • Analytical data: OLTP versus OLAP, warehouses, star schemas, ETL and ELT
  • The object-relational mismatch, and where ORMs help and where they mislead

Milestone BYou understand the machine
stages 00 – 08

Roughly 1,360 hours. You can reason about a program from the transistor to the HTTP response, and debug it at any layer in between. Most working engineers never reach this point. It is what separates competence from seniority later.

STAGE 09 Craft

Software craft and engineering practice

3–4 months220 hoursStart early, never finish

This is where a programmer becomes an engineer. Everything before this stage is about making code work. This stage is about making code that a team can change safely two years from now, which is the actual job.

Companion volumeThe Engineer's Craft19 chapters · 83 code examples · 53 exercises0 / 19 readOpen the volume →

What you learn

  • Naming, function size, and comments that explain why rather than what
  • Managing complexity: deep modules, narrow interfaces, information hiding
  • SOLID principles, read critically rather than as scripture
  • Composition over inheritance, dependency inversion, the seams that make code testable
  • Design patterns, and the far more useful skill of recognising when you do not need one
  • Testing: unit, integration, end-to-end; the test pyramid and its critics
  • Test-driven development, property-based testing, snapshot tests, mutation testing
  • Test doubles: fakes, stubs, mocks, and why over-mocking makes tests useless
  • Refactoring in small safe steps with tests as the net
  • Working with legacy code: characterisation tests, seams, strangler fig migrations
  • Code review: what to look for, how to disagree, how to receive it
  • Branching strategies, trunk-based development, feature flags, semantic versioning
  • Systematic debugging: bisect, reduce, hypothesise, verify. Profiling before optimising
  • Technical writing: READMEs, design documents, architecture decision records, RFCs
  • Estimation, decomposition, scope negotiation, and working in iterations honestly
  • Error handling strategy: where to fail, where to retry, what to log, what to surface

STAGE 11 Systems

Distributed systems and architecture

4–6 months280 hoursRequires stages 05, 06, 08

Once a second machine is involved, everything you assumed about time, order and failure stops being true. This is the deepest stage in the curriculum and the one that most defines a senior engineer. Do not start it before you have built something real, or none of it will stick.

What you learn

  • Why distribute at all: throughput, availability, latency, and the cost of each
  • The eight fallacies of distributed computing, and how each one bites
  • Replication: single-leader, multi-leader, leaderless; synchronous and asynchronous trade-offs
  • Partitioning and sharding, rebalancing, consistent hashing, hot keys
  • Consistency models: linearizability, sequential, causal, eventual; read-your-writes

  • Failure handling: timeouts, retries with jittered backoff, circuit breakers, bulkheads, backpressure
  • Load balancing, service discovery, health checks, graceful degradation
  • Batch and stream processing; windowing, watermarks, late data
  • Architecture styles: monolith, modular monolith, services, serverless, event-driven
  • Domain-driven design: bounded contexts, aggregates, ubiquitous language
  • The system design method: requirements, estimates, API, data model, high level, deep dive, bottlenecks

STAGE 15 Mastery

Expert practice

ContinuousThe rest of itRequires everything above

Expertise is not one more subject. It is depth in a chosen area, taste built from having been wrong, and the ability to move other engineers. The technical half of this is the easy half.

Companion volumeThe Engineer's Guild16 chapters · 16 code examples · 48 exercises0 / 16 readOpen the volume →

What you practise

  • Pick one track below and go three levels deeper than your team needs
  • Read primary sources: Dynamo, Bigtable, MapReduce, Spanner, Raft, The Google File System
  • Read production source code of tools you use daily, and fix something in one
  • Contribute to open source until you are trusted with review, not just patches
  • Write design documents that survive contact with sceptical senior engineers
  • Lead a project end to end: scoping, sequencing, risk, stakeholders, landing it
  • Mentor deliberately. Teaching is the fastest way to find the holes in your own model
  • Learn the business: what your company sells, what it costs, where engineering moves the number
  • Develop taste in trade-offs, which means being able to argue both sides honestly
  • Write publicly. A body of written work compounds more than any certification
  • Cultivate the boring virtues: reliability, clear communication, finishing things

Where to specialise

Pick one after milestone B, not before. Breadth first makes the depth mean something, and every track below assumes the whole foundation underneath it.

Backend and distributed systems

Build the thing that stays up

The default track, and the one with the most demand. Depth in stages 08 and 11.

  • Storage engine internals
  • Consensus and replication in practice
  • Stream processing at scale
  • A systems language: Go or Rust

Platform and infrastructure

Build what other engineers build on

For people who like leverage more than features. Depth in stages 05 and 12.

  • Kubernetes internals and operators
  • Developer experience and build systems
  • Networking, service meshes, eBPF
  • Reliability engineering and capacity

AI engineering

Make non-deterministic systems dependable

The fastest-growing track. Depth in stages 03 and 14.

  • Retrieval and ranking quality
  • Evaluation and measurement design
  • Agent architectures and tool design
  • Inference cost and latency engineering

Low-level systems

Build the layers everyone assumes

Smallest job market, highest barrier, deepest satisfaction. Depth in stages 04, 05 and 07.

  • Kernels and drivers
  • Database and storage engines
  • Compilers and runtimes
  • Embedded and real-time systems

Product and frontend

Own what the user actually touches

Underrated, and the track closest to the business. Depth in stages 09 and 10.

  • Rendering and interaction performance
  • Accessibility and design systems
  • Offline, sync and local-first data
  • Experimentation and measurement

Security engineering

Find it before someone else does

Two distinct careers, offensive and defensive. Depth in stage 13.

  • Application and cloud security
  • Detection and incident response
  • Cryptographic engineering
  • Supply chain and software provenance

The ways this goes wrong

These are the failure modes that end self-directed study. Each one feels like progress while it is happening.

  • Tutorial hellFollowing along with a video produces the feeling of competence and none of it. The test is whether you can build a variation of the thing without the video. If not, you have not learned it.
  • Collecting languages and frameworksSix languages at a shallow depth is worth less than one at depth, and hiring managers stopped being impressed by long tool lists some years ago.
  • Skipping the foundations because the assistant writes the codeAn engineer who cannot evaluate generated code is a liability, and the market prices that correctly. Fundamentals are what make the leverage safe.
  • Reading the advanced book too earlyDesigning Data-Intensive Applications is transformative after you have built and operated something, and forgettable before. The same is true of most of the systems canon.
  • Optimising only for interviewsGrinding problems without building systems produces candidates who pass the screen and fail the job, which the industry has now adjusted for.
  • Never shipping to a real userA portfolio of unfinished projects signals the one thing employers screen hardest for. Finish three things completely rather than starting twenty.
  • Learning alone the whole wayCode review, pair programming and public work correct errors you cannot see from the inside. Find people, even if that means open source rather than a job.
  • Treating this document as a queueIt is a map. Loop back, re-read, and let a real problem pull you into a stage early when it does.
