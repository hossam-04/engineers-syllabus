The Engineer's Guild

A guild was where masters trained apprentices, judged one another's work and kept the standards of a craft. This volume is about that last stage: depth in an area you chose, taste built from having been wrong, and the ability to make other engineers better. It covers reading papers and production code, open source and design review, honest trade-offs and estimates, leading projects and mentoring people, the business you work for, and the habits that make a career compound. Every chapter turns its advice into a small program you can run, so the claims are checkable.

How to use this volume

This stage has no end date. Every other stage in the syllabus can be finished; this one is practised for the rest of a career. The syllabus says it plainly: expertise isn't one more subject but depth in a chosen area, taste built from having been wrong, and the ability to move other engineers, and the technical half is the easy half. So this volume isn't a course to complete in a month. Read it through once, then return to the part that matches what you're doing: Part I when choosing and deepening an area, Part II when working in the open, Part III when leading, Part IV when planning the long run. Every chapter has a program. Advice about careers and leadership is usually unfalsifiable, which is why so much of it is useless. Here, each chapter's central claim is turned into a small Python program that runs, using only the standard library: a decision tested for fragility, a paper's idea built small, a codebase mapped and traced, a patch reviewed, a schedule simulated, a status report computed from data, a team's flow measured. The programs use invented numbers, said so in the text, so read their outputs for the shape of the result rather than as measurements of your situation. Then change the numbers to yours. That's the point of them.

Do the exercises in your own work. Most of them ask you to apply the chapter to something real: your area, your codebase, your next design document, your current project. They're more useful done slowly, over months, than all at once. Several, such as the decision journal in chapter 1 and the work log in chapter 16, are habits to start now and keep.

The deliverable is a body of work, not a project: a maintained open-source contribution, a written archive others cite, and at least one system in production that other engineers depend on and that you didn't build alone. That takes years, and the appendix describes what each part looks like when it's done. Begin all three at once, small, and let them grow together.

The one habit

Almost everything in this volume depends on one habit: write it down. The decision and what you expected, in a journal. The paper's argument, in a summary. The design, in a document. The trade-off, as ranges. The project's state, as a forecast. What you did this week, in a log.

Writing is how judgement becomes something you can check, share and improve. Memory flatters its owner and fades; a written record does neither. The engineers people trust most are usually the ones whose thinking they can read.

Chapter 01

What mastery is

13 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

Expertise isn't one more subject to study. It's three things that grow together: depth in an area you chose, taste built from having been wrong, and the ability to make other engineers better. The first is the one you can plan. Pick the area deliberately, go several levels deeper than your team needs, and expect it to take years rather than months. This chapter is about choosing well, and about knowing how fragile your choice is.

Depth, taste and leverage

Every stage before this one taught breadth: enough of each layer to work across all of them. That's the foundation, and it's what makes an engineer reliable. What makes one exceptional is different. When the database is losing writes at three in the morning, or a design review needs someone who has seen this exact failure before, teams don't call the person who knows a little of everything. They call the person who knows one thing all the way down.

That depth is the first part of expertise. The second is taste: a sense of which designs will age well and which will hurt, which risks are real and which are noise. Taste isn't mysterious. It's a large store of cases, most of them mistakes, yours and other people's, compressed into fast judgement. The third is leverage: the ability to make that judgement useful to others, through review, design documents, mentoring and writing. An expert whose knowledge stays in their head helps one project at a time. One who can teach it changes how a whole organisation builds.

This volume covers all three. Part I is about depth: choosing an area and reading your way into it, through papers and production code. Part II is about working in the open, where taste is built and tested: open source, design documents, trade-offs and public writing. Part III is about leading: estimation, sequencing, communication and mentoring. Part IV is about the long game: the business, measurement, finishing things and a career that compounds.

Three levels deeper

The syllabus asks you to go "three levels deeper than your team needs", which needs unpacking. Think of knowing a technology at four levels. At level 1 you can use it: write the queries, call the API, follow the guide. At level 2 you can configure and debug it: tune it, read its logs, explain why it's slow. At level 3 you know its internals and failure modes: how it stores data, what it does under partition or overload, which of its guarantees are weaker than they sound. At level 4 you can change it: fix bugs in its source, contribute features, or build a smaller version yourself and explain every trade-off you made.

Most teams need level 1 of most of what they use and level 2 of a few things. "Three levels deeper" means being at level 3 or 4 in something your team relies on, so that you're the person who can answer the questions nobody else can. It doesn't mean ignoring everything else. The T shape, broad across the top and deep in one place, is the usual picture, and the breadth matters: depth in databases is worth far more to someone who also understands the network, the operating system and the product than to someone who doesn't.

Choosing where to go deep

The hub's tracks, backend and distributed systems, platform, AI engineering, low-level systems, product and frontend, and security, are broad families. Inside each are areas narrow enough to master: storage engines, consensus and replication, query planning, cryptographic engineering, rendering performance. Choose an area, not a track. Four questions help.

  • Leverage: how often do your team's hardest problems land in this area? Depth that your work uses every week compounds; depth that your work never touches decays.
  • Curiosity: would you read about it on a free weekend, for years? Mastery takes a decade of attention, and only genuine interest survives that long.
  • Adjacency: how much of what you already know carries over? An area next to your strengths gets you to level 3 sooner, and the early progress keeps you going.
  • Demand: how many teams, anywhere, need a deep expert in it? This matters less than people think, because real depth in almost anything is rare, but it matters.

None of these is decisive on its own, and the honest way to combine them is to write them down, weight them, and then ask how much your answer depends on numbers you guessed. The example does exactly that, for a backend engineer on a payments team weighing five areas.

What the example shows

The weights matter less than you'd expect. Databases wins, two points ahead of distributed systems, and it stays the winner over wide ranges of every weight. Leverage could fall from 3 to 1.5, or curiosity rise from 3 to 4.5, before security overtook it. Arguing about whether curiosity deserves a weight of 3 or 4 is mostly wasted breath.

The scores matter more. A single point, databases' leverage at 4 instead of 5, or its curiosity at 2 instead of 3, hands the win to distributed systems. That's the useful output. It tells you the decision is a near tie between two areas, decided by two judgements you could check. Is the team's hardest work really in the database? Spend a week reading database internals and see whether your curiosity score holds up. A decision matrix is worth less for its answer than for showing you which question to go and answer.

And notice what didn't come close: machine-learning infrastructure, with the highest demand of all, came last, because nothing in this engineer's work or experience touches it. Demand without leverage or adjacency makes for a slow, lonely start.

How long it takes

Peter Norvig's essay Teach Yourself Programming in Ten Years borrows a finding from research on expertise in chess, music and sport: world-class performance takes about ten years of deliberate practice, and there's no known shortcut. Engineering is no different. That sounds discouraging until you notice what it implies: if you start now and keep going, you'll have it, and most people never start. A useful level 3 in one area takes perhaps two years of steady effort alongside a job, and level 4 several more.

The effort needs a shape, or it dissolves into reading whatever crosses your feed. Chapter 16 of The Engineer's Whetstone covers deliberate practice; here, the concrete version is a standing list of the next three things you'll read or build in your area, renewed whenever one is done. The rest of Part I is about two of the best sources: the papers that founded your area, and the production code that implements it.

Taste comes from being wrong

Taste grows from cases, and the most instructive cases are your own mistakes, but only if you notice them. Memory is generous to its owner: a year later, you'll remember having been fairly sure the migration would be risky, whatever you actually said. The cure is a decision journal. Before a decision that matters, write a few lines: what you decided, what you expected to happen, how confident you were, and what would prove you wrong. Months later, read it back. The surprises are the lessons, and they're the raw material of taste.

Other people's mistakes count too, and they're cheaper. Postmortems, published incident reports, design documents that describe a rejected alternative and why: read them in your area as you'd read case law. Over time you'll find you can predict the failure before reading how it ended. That's what taste is.

Common traps
  • Specialising before you have breadth, so the depth has nothing to connect to.
  • Choosing by demand alone, an area that's popular but that your work never touches.
  • Staying at level 2 everywhere, because each new tool feels like progress.
  • Treating a decision matrix's answer as the decision, rather than as a pointer to what to check.
  • Remembering your judgement as better than it was, because nothing was written down at the time.

Exercise 1.1Score your own areas

Change the example's areas, scores and weights to your own. Which area wins, and what's the smallest single change that would make the runner-up win? What would you do in the next month to find out whether that change is true?

Show solution

Your numbers are yours, but the shape of a good answer is the same. Suppose storage engines beat stream processing by three points, and the smallest flip is your curiosity about storage engines dropping by one. Then the next month's task is a test of that score: read the first four chapters of Database Internals and one paper, LSM trees or a B-tree variant, and notice whether you look forward to it. If the flip were leverage instead, the test would be different: list the last ten hard problems your team hit and count how many were really storage problems. Either way, the exercise turns a vague career question into a small experiment with an answer. If no single change flips the result, the decision isn't close, and you can stop deliberating and start.

Exercise 1.2Describe level 3

Pick a technology your team uses every day. Write down three things someone at level 3 would know about it that someone at level 2 wouldn't.

Show solution

For PostgreSQL, for example. At level 2 you can read EXPLAIN ANALYZE and add an index. At level 3 you know, first, that an UPDATE writes a whole new row version and leaves the old one for vacuum, so a table updated constantly bloats, and a long-running transaction anywhere stops vacuum from reclaiming it. Second, that the planner's row estimates come from sampled statistics with an independence assumption, so correlated columns produce wild misestimates that no index fixes. Third, that synchronous_commit, fsync and replication settings decide what a "committed" transaction survives, and that the defaults trade some of that for speed. Each of these is a failure mode, not a feature, which is the mark of level 3: you know how it breaks. If your three items read like documentation of features, push further.

Chapter 02

Reading a paper

12 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

The papers behind your area are its primary sources: the ideas as their inventors stated them, with the reasoning, the alternatives they rejected and the measurements they had. They're short, and most engineers never read them. Read each one in passes, with a question in mind. Then build its central idea small enough to run, because until you've built it you've only read about it.

Why go to the source

Everything you've learned about distributed systems passed through several hands before it reached you: the paper, the talk about the paper, the blog post about the talk, the vendor's documentation, the colleague's summary. Each retelling keeps the conclusion and drops the reasoning. The paper still has it. It says what problem the authors faced, which designs they considered, why they chose this one, what they measured, and, often in a sentence nobody quotes, what it doesn't handle well.

That last part is why primary sources build taste. Dynamo's authors say plainly that their design suits applications that can tolerate conflicting versions. MapReduce's authors say its model fits computations that can be expressed as map and reduce, and leave the rest unsaid. Read the paper and you know the system's boundaries as its designers saw them, which no summary preserves. And most of the classics are twelve to fifteen pages long. A paper a month is a modest habit, and in five years it's a foundation few engineers have.

Read in three passes

Srinivasan Keshav's short note How to Read a Paper describes the method most researchers use, and it works just as well for engineers. Never read a paper once from start to end. Read it three times, each pass deeper, and stop after any pass if the paper isn't worth more.

  • First pass, ten minutes. Title, abstract and introduction; the section headings; the conclusion; a glance at the references. Afterwards, answer Keshav's five Cs: the category of paper (a system, a measurement, an analysis), its context (what it builds on), whether its assumptions look correct, its main contributions, and its clarity. Most papers stop here.
  • Second pass, an hour. Read the whole paper but skip proofs and fine detail. Study the figures and the evaluation: what workload, on what hardware, compared with what? Note what you don't understand, and the references you'd need.
  • Third pass, several hours. Reconstruct the paper: make the same assumptions and rebuild the design yourself, then compare. This is where you find the hidden assumptions and the weak spots, and it's the pass that makes the ideas yours.

Go in with a question, as you would with a debugger. "How does GFS handle a chunkserver dying mid-write?" focuses a second pass far better than "learn about GFS". And read the evaluation sceptically. Every paper measures the workloads its design is good at. The interesting question is always which workloads it didn't measure, and why.

Building the core small

For an engineer, the best third pass is to build the paper's central idea, small enough to run in a page of code. Not the system, which took a team years, but the mechanism the paper is about. Building forces every vague sentence into a decision. "The master re-executes failed tasks" becomes the question of what state a failed task leaves behind, and whether running it twice could double-count anything. The places where you get stuck are the places where you didn't understand the paper, even though you thought you did.

MapReduce, the 2004 paper by Jeffrey Dean and Sanjay Ghemawat, is the classic case. Its idea fits in a sentence: express a computation as a map function, applied to each piece of input independently, and a reduce function, applied to all the values for each key, and let the system handle distributing the work, moving the data and surviving failures. The figure shows the flow, and the example builds it: a word count over eight input splits, with three of the paper's refinements.

What the example shows

A combiner cuts the shuffle, by how much depends on the data. Running the reduce function on each map task's own output before sending it reduced the pairs shuffled from 71 to 56, 21% fewer. The saving is modest here because each split is one short sentence, so few words repeat within a split. On real inputs, where a 64 MB split contains "the" thousands of times, the paper's combiner cuts traffic by orders of magnitude. The example also shows the condition the paper relies on: the combiner gave the same answer only because addition doesn't care how you group it. Try it with an average instead of a sum, and exercise 2.2 shows what breaks.

Re-execution is safe because map is pure. Map task 3's worker died, its local output vanished, the task ran again elsewhere, and the result was identical. This is the paper's deepest design decision, easy to miss on a first read: by asking users for functions with no side effects, the system can retry anything without coordination. A map function that wrote to a database, or read the clock, would make retries visible and the whole fault-tolerance story would collapse.

Backup tasks attack the tail. A job finishes when its slowest task does, and a few slow machines, a bad disk, a noisy neighbour, can hold up hundreds of healthy ones. The paper's fix is to start a second copy of each task still running near the end and take whichever finishes first. In the simulation, the median job finished 65% sooner. Its constants are invented, so read that for its direction; the paper itself reports that one sort job took 44% longer with backup tasks switched off. The same idea reappears a decade later in Dean and Barroso's The Tail at Scale as hedged requests, which is one reason to read papers in sequence.

Reading the critics too

A paper is an argument, and arguments have opponents. In 2008, David DeWitt and Michael Stonebraker published "MapReduce: A major step backwards", arguing that it ignored forty years of database research: no schemas, no indexes, brute-force scans. Some of the criticism aged badly and some aged well; within a few years, SQL layers such as Hive appeared on top of Hadoop, and later systems such as Spark kept the fault-tolerance idea while dropping the rigid two-stage model. Reading the paper, its critics and what came after tells you more about the design space than any of them alone. When you finish a paper, look for who argued with it.

Making it a habit

Habits survive where intentions don't. Three that work: a reading list of five papers in your area, ordered so each builds on the last; a one-page summary for each, written after the second pass, with the problem, the key idea, the evidence and your doubts; and company. A reading group of three colleagues meeting every fortnight to discuss one paper will carry you through papers you'd abandon alone, and the discussion surfaces what you missed. Papers We Love, a community with chapters in many cities, keeps a curated repository of papers by topic and is a good place to start. Chapter 3 suggests a first reading list for distributed data systems.

Common traps
  • Reading a paper once, front to back, and getting lost in section 3.
  • Reading the summary instead, and losing the reasoning and the stated limits.
  • Trusting the evaluation, without asking which workloads it left out.
  • Stopping at understanding, and never building the core to test it.
  • Reading alone, without writing anything down, so a month later little remains.

Exercise 2.2A combiner that lies

Change the example to compute the average length of the words starting with each letter, and use the reduce function as the combiner. Why is the answer wrong, and how would you fix it?

Show solution

An average of averages isn't the overall average. If one map task saw two words starting with "t" of lengths 3 and 5, its combiner sends 4; if another saw one word of length 10, it sends 10; the reducer averages 4 and 10 to 7, but the true average of 3, 5 and 10 is 6. Addition is associative and commutative, so partial sums combine correctly; averaging isn't. The fix is to combine something that does compose: have map emit (length, 1) pairs, have the combiner and reducer add both parts, giving (total length, count), and divide only at the very end. The paper's condition, that the combining function must be commutative and associative, is exactly this.

Exercise 2.3A map that isn't pure

A map function samples 10% of its input at random to make the job faster. What goes wrong when the system re-runs a failed task or a backup task, and what would you do instead?

Show solution

Each run of the task picks a different random 10%. If a map task fails after some of its output was read, or a backup copy and the original both finish, the reducers can see output from two different samples, so the totals are neither one sample nor the other, and the job's result changes from run to run. The paper's guarantee, that the output equals what a single sequential run would produce, holds only for deterministic functions. The fix is to make the sampling deterministic: decide whether to keep each record by hashing its key or position with a fixed seed, so every run of the task keeps exactly the same records. The general lesson applies far beyond MapReduce: anything that a system might retry must give the same result each time, which is the same property chapter 10 of The Engineer's Fleet asks of message consumers.

Chapter 03

The canon

10 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

A handful of papers, published over about ten years, founded the distributed data systems most of the industry now runs on. Read them in order and they become a conversation: each one answers a problem the last one left open. This chapter gives a reading order with the one lesson each paper teaches, then reads the most surprising of them, Spanner, closely enough to build its central trick: making timestamps agree with real time by waiting out the clocks' uncertainty.

A conversation in seven papers

The syllabus names six papers: The Google File System, MapReduce, Bigtable, Dynamo, Spanner and Raft. Read in publication order, with Chubby added, they tell a story about one question: how do you build a dependable system out of thousands of machines that fail all the time?

YearPaperThe lesson that transfers
2003The Google File SystemTreat failure as the normal case, and design for your real workload, not a general one.
2004MapReduceA restricted programming model buys automatic distribution and fault tolerance.
2006BigtableA sorted, versioned map, built on a file system and a lock service, can serve both batch and online work.
2006ChubbyPut consensus in one small, well-tested service, and let everything else lean on it.
2007DynamoWhen availability matters more than agreement, accept conflicting versions and reconcile them.
2012SpannerWith bounded clock uncertainty, a global database can have real-time ordering, at the price of waiting.
2014RaftConsensus can be designed to be understood, and understandability is a correctness property.

Two lines of descent run through the table. Google's papers keep strong coordination and spend engineering on making it cheap: a single master in GFS, a lock service under Bigtable, Paxos inside Spanner. Amazon's Dynamo goes the other way: no master, writes accepted anywhere, conflicts resolved afterwards, because a shopping cart that's sometimes inconsistent beats one that's sometimes unavailable. The Engineer's Fleet covers the theory behind that choice. The papers show it being made, by people with real constraints, and that's the part that builds judgement.

Read the first pass of all seven in a weekend, then one a month in depth. For each, find the sentence where the authors state the assumption everything else depends on. GFS assumes large files written by appending. Dynamo assumes applications can merge conflicting versions. Spanner assumes clocks whose error is known and small. When a system built on one of these designs misbehaves, it's very often because that assumption stopped being true.

Spanner's problem

Spanner, from James Corbett and colleagues at Google in 2012, is a database spread across data centres on several continents that still gives transactions a property called external consistency: if one transaction commits before another starts, in real time, the first gets the smaller timestamp. That sounds obvious, and it's very hard, because the two transactions may run on machines thousands of kilometres apart whose clocks disagree.

Why it matters: suppose an administrator revokes Bob's access to a folder, and a moment later, a colleague uploads a salary file to it. The revoke committed first, in real time. If the machines' clocks disagree, the upload can still get the earlier timestamp. Then a read of the database "as of" some moment between the two timestamps, the kind of consistent snapshot every backup, report and replica uses, sees the salary file in the folder and Bob still allowed to read it. No single machine did anything wrong; the timestamps simply lied about the order.

TrueTime and commit wait

Spanner's answer has two parts. The first is TrueTime, a clock API that doesn't return a time but an interval: TT.now() returns [earliest, latest], guaranteed to contain the true time. Google keeps the interval narrow with GPS receivers and atomic clocks in every data centre; the paper reports an uncertainty, called epsilon, that typically varied between about 1 and 7 milliseconds.

The second part is a rule. When a transaction commits, Spanner takes its timestamp from the top of the interval, latest, a time no earlier than now, and then waits, before letting anyone see the commit, until TT.now().earliest has passed that timestamp. After the wait, the timestamp is certainly in the past everywhere. Any transaction that starts afterwards, on any machine, will read a clock whose interval lies entirely after it, and so get a larger timestamp. The figure shows one commit, and the example tests the rule against the alternative of stamping commits with the local clock.

What the example shows

Local clocks get the order wrong a third of the time. With clocks up to 7 ms off in either direction, and the second transaction starting within 5 ms of the first becoming visible, 3,446 of 10,000 causally ordered pairs received timestamps in the wrong order. The example prints one: the revoke stamped later than the upload, so a snapshot between them shows the salary file with Bob still allowed in. Real gaps between transactions are usually longer than 5 ms, so real error rates are lower, but any rate above zero breaks the guarantee.

Commit wait gets it right every time, for a fixed price. With the wait, none of the 10,000 pairs was misordered, and every commit took 14 ms longer, exactly twice epsilon. In the real system, much of that wait overlaps with the Paxos round that replicates the commit, so it costs less than it seems, but the trade is fundamental: certainty about order is bought with time, in proportion to how uncertain the clocks are.

Better clocks make every write faster. At 1 ms of uncertainty the wait falls to 2 ms. That's why Google put atomic clocks in its data centres, and why the idea has spread. Amazon's Time Sync Service and its open-source ClockBound daemon now give ordinary cloud machines a clock with a stated error bound. Other databases make a different trade. CockroachDB, without special clocks, assumes a maximum offset and, instead of waiting at commit, restarts a read that encounters a value whose timestamp falls inside its uncertainty window. Reading Spanner closely is what lets you see that these are variations on one idea.

Reading the canon today

None of these systems is used today exactly as published, and that's not the point. Hardware changed, Bigtable's successors look different, and Raft has largely replaced the Paxos implementations of the earlier papers. What lasts is the method each paper shows: name the workload, state the assumptions, pick the weakest guarantee that serves the application, and measure. When you design something, try writing the first page of the paper you'd publish about it: the problem, the assumptions and the key idea. If you can't, the design isn't ready.

Common traps
  • Reading the papers out of order, and missing what each one was answering.
  • Copying a design without its assumptions, such as GFS's append-only workload for a database.
  • Trusting a timestamp from a machine's clock to order events across machines.
  • Treating a clock's error as zero, when it's milliseconds and sometimes much more.
  • Reading only Google's line, or only Amazon's, and taking one set of trade-offs as the only one.
Exercises
Exercise 3.1The assumption under each design

For Bigtable, Dynamo and Raft, name the assumption the design depends on, and a situation where it stops being true.

Show solution

Bigtable assumes a reliable lock service and file system underneath it: it uses Chubby to make sure only one master and one server per tablet are active, and GFS for durability. If Chubby is unavailable for long, Bigtable becomes unavailable too; the paper reports exactly this, as a small fraction of downtime. Dynamo assumes applications can reconcile conflicting versions of an object, merging two shopping carts, say. For a bank balance, where merging two concurrent withdrawals produces money that doesn't exist, the assumption fails and Dynamo's model is the wrong one. Raft assumes a majority of servers can communicate and that messages are eventually delivered. In a partition that leaves no majority, it correctly refuses to make progress, so a cluster of two data centres with an even split can't elect a leader. Each answer names when the design is wrong, which is the point of reading assumptions first.

Exercise 3.2When the clock lies

Change the example so one node's clock is 20 ms off while TrueTime still claims 7 ms of uncertainty. What happens, and what does Spanner do to prevent it?

Show solution

Commit wait stops protecting you. The guarantee depends on the interval really containing true time; with a node 20 ms off, its "latest" can be earlier than true time, so a timestamp taken there can still precede a transaction that commits later somewhere else, and misordered pairs reappear. In the example, give the first node an offset of 0.020, 20 ms fast, and about three quarters of the pairs come out in the wrong order despite the wait. Spanner defends the assumption rather than the algorithm: each machine's time daemon checks itself against several time masters, and a machine whose local clock drifts beyond its bound, or that can't reach enough masters, removes itself from service. The lesson generalises: when an algorithm's correctness rests on an assumption, the system needs a mechanism that detects the assumption failing and stops, not just an algorithm that's correct when it holds.

Exercise 3.3Two ways to pay for order

Spanner waits at commit; CockroachDB restarts reads that see an uncertain value. Which workload suffers more under each approach?

Show solution

Commit wait taxes writes: every read-write transaction pays about twice epsilon, whether or not any reader would have been confused, so a write-heavy workload with small, frequent transactions pays most. Reads are free of it. CockroachDB's uncertainty restarts tax reads instead, and only some of them: a read restarts when it meets a value written within the clock uncertainty window just before its own timestamp, which happens mostly when reads closely chase recent writes to the same keys, such as a hot counter read and written constantly. With a large maximum offset, those restarts become frequent. So Spanner's approach is predictable and suits workloads that can afford a few milliseconds per write; CockroachDB's is cheaper on average without special clocks but less predictable under contention. Both are ways of paying for the same thing, which is ignorance of what time it is.

Chapter 04

Reading production code

10 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

The source of the tools you use every day is the most accurate documentation they have, and reading it is how level 2 becomes level 3. But nobody reads a large codebase from the first line. Map it first: its files, its entry points, its dependencies, its history. Then trace what actually runs for one real call, because the code you read and the code that runs are not always the same. And then fix something in it, which is the fastest way to understand any of it.

Why read the source

Documentation describes what the authors intended; source code describes what happens. When they disagree, the code wins, and the disagreements are exactly the cases you need to know about: the edge where a timeout isn't enforced, the default that's different from the one in the manual, the fast path that skips a check. Every expert you admire in an area has read the code at its centre. Linus Torvalds' "use the source, Luke" is a joke, and also the most practical advice in this volume.

Production code teaches something papers can't: what a design looks like after ten years of bug reports. The paper describes the idea; the code shows the idea plus every special case that real users found. Reading the special cases, and the commit messages that explain them, is how you learn which parts of a design are fragile.

Map before you read

Approach a codebase the way you'd approach a city: get a map before walking the streets. Four questions, each answered with a tool rather than by reading.

  • What's here, and how big is it? List the files and count their lines. A thousand-line file in a project of hundred-line files is where the complexity lives.
  • Where does it start? Find the entry points: main, the public API, the request handler, the __all__ of a Python package. Everything else is reached from these.
  • Who depends on whom? Draw the import graph. A module everything imports is the foundation; one that imports everything is the glue.
  • What changes, and why? git log --format= --name-only | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn | head lists the most-changed files, which are usually the ones that matter and the ones that hurt. git log -L and git blame show why a line is the way it is.

The tests are part of the map. A project's test suite is a list of the behaviours its authors cared enough to pin down, written as runnable examples. Reading the tests for a function before the function is often faster than reading the function.

Trace what runs

A map shows what could run. A trace shows what does, for one concrete input. Debuggers, profilers and logging all give traces; in Python, sys.setprofile lets a few lines of code record every function call, which is what the example uses on the standard library's json package. Tracing first, then reading only the functions on the path, turns a codebase of thousands of lines into a few dozen that matter for your question.

What the example shows

The map comes first and costs nothing. Six modules, about 1,400 lines in all, with the work split between encoder.py and decoder.py, and decoder.py leaning on scanner.py. The seven names in __all__ are the whole public surface. That's enough to know where to look for any question about the package, before reading a single function body.

The code you read isn't always the code that runs. The trace of json.dumps found only three Python calls, then nothing: the encoding itself ran in C, in the _json module, which encoder.py imports if it can. Someone reading _make_iterencode, the most interesting function in the file, would be reading code that, in an ordinary run, never executes. Switch the accelerator off, as CPython's own tests do, and the same call made 49 Python-level calls through eight distinct functions, with identical output. The count is high because each time a generator resumes it counts as a call, and the pure-Python encoder is a tree of nested generators.

This pattern, a readable implementation with a fast one beside it, is common: Python's heapq, datetime and decimal do it too, as do many libraries in other languages. When a fast path exists, the slow one is usually the specification, and the tests check that the two agree. Knowing which one runs is part of reading the code.

Reading strategies that work

Start from a question. "Where does this library decide to retry?" gives you a search term, a trace to run and a place to stop. "Understand this library" gives you nothing. Follow one request end to end. For a web framework, a database or a message broker, pick one operation and trace it from the socket to the disk and back; most of the architecture is on that path. Keep a map document as you go: the modules, their roles, the path you traced, the surprises. It's your notes for the next time, and, cleaned up, it's documentation the project probably lacks.

Read the history of the lines that puzzle you. A strange condition in a function is usually a bug fix, and git log -S 'that condition' finds the commit that added it, whose message, linked issue or review thread explains the case it handles. That's the special-case knowledge that makes level 3.

Then fix something

The syllabus asks you to read the source of a tool you use daily "and fix something in one". Fixing is the fastest way in because it forces every step: reproduce the bug, find the code responsible, understand it well enough to change it safely, write a test that fails before and passes after, and explain the change to a maintainer who knows the code better than you do. Start with a bug you've actually hit, or a "good first issue" in a project you use. A one-line fix with a good test and a clear explanation teaches more about a codebase than a week of reading. Chapter 5 is about the rest of that journey.

Common traps
  • Reading from the first line of the first file, without a map or a question.
  • Reading code that never runs, such as the pure-Python fallback beside a C accelerator.
  • Skipping the tests, the clearest statement of what the code is meant to do.
  • Ignoring the history, when the commit message explains the strange line.
  • Reading without ever changing anything, so understanding is never tested.

CPython ships two JSON encoders, one in C and one in Python. Find out how its test suite makes sure both are tested and give the same results.

It then defines two mixin classes, PyTest and CTest, that point a test at one or the other, and most test classes are written once and inherited by both, so every test runs against both implementations. If the C accelerator isn't available, the C half is skipped. It's a pattern worth copying in your own code whenever you keep a fast path beside a simple one: one set of tests, run against both, so they can't drift apart. And it's the kind of thing you only learn by reading the tests, because the documentation doesn't mention it.

  • Do you read the tests and the commit history of puzzling code?
  • Have you fixed something, with a test, in a tool you use every day?

Chapter 05

Open source, from patch to reviewer

12 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

Contributing to open source is less about code than about attention: a maintainer has little of it, and every patch spends some. The contributors who become trusted are the ones whose patches cost the least to accept: small, tested, explained, and in the project's style. This chapter looks at a project from the maintainer's side, runs the checks a careful maintainer runs on two incoming patches, and maps the path from first patch to reviewer.

A ladder of trust

Every open-source project has a rough ladder, whether or not it's written down. Users run the software. Reporters file issues good enough to act on. Contributors send patches. Regulars send patches often enough that maintainers recognise their names. Reviewers are trusted to judge other people's patches, and maintainers decide what the project is. Each step up is granted, not claimed, and it's granted for one reason: the person has reliably made the maintainers' work easier.

The syllabus asks you to "contribute to open source until you are trusted with review, not just patches". That's a statement about the top of the ladder, and it takes months, sometimes years, in one project. It's worth it because review is where the deepest learning happens: you see every proposed change to a system you care about, and the maintainers' comments on your reviews teach you their taste. It's the apprenticeship the guild in this volume's title refers to.

Choosing a project

Contribute to something you use, so the bugs you find are real and your motivation survives the slow parts. Before your first patch, check that the project is alive and welcoming. Are pull requests answered within weeks, not months? Is there a CONTRIBUTING file, a code of conduct, a label such as "good first issue"? Read a few recent reviews: are the maintainers patient with newcomers, or curt? A large, famous project is not necessarily the best place to start. A mid-sized one where your patch will be read by a human this week teaches more.

Start below the code. Reproduce someone else's bug report and add the missing details. Answer a question in the issue tracker. Improve a confusing paragraph of the documentation. These cost maintainers almost nothing to accept, and they teach you how the project works and who decides what.

The maintainer's view

Most open-source projects are maintained by a few people, often in their spare time, with far more incoming work than time. A patch, however well meant, is a request for their attention: to read it, understand it, check it, and then maintain the result for years. The patches that get merged quickly are the ones that make that easy. Before reading a single line of the diff, a careful maintainer asks a series of quick questions, and the example automates them: does it apply to the current main branch, do the tests pass, does it add a test, does it keep the public interface, is it small enough to review, and does its message explain why? Some projects also require a sign-off line, the Developer Certificate of Origin, in which the author certifies they have the right to submit the code.

The example sets up a small project, a slugify function with an open issue: accented letters are dropped, so "Café Société" becomes caf-soci-t. Two contributors send patches, created with git format-patch and applied in a fresh clone with git am, which is how many projects, Git and the Linux kernel among them, take patches by email.

What the example shows

Theo's patch costs almost nothing to accept. Five changed lines, a regression test that names the issue, a subject in the imperative mood under fifty characters, a body that says what was wrong and why the fix works, and a sign-off. A maintainer can read it in a minute, see the test, and merge.

Sam's patch might even work, and it doesn't matter. It also handles accents. But it renames slugify to make_slug, which breaks every existing caller, so the existing tests fail on import. It adds no test, so nothing proves it fixes the issue. Its message, "fixed the accents bug.", says nothing a maintainer needs, and it has no sign-off. Each of these is fixable, and a kind maintainer will say so, but each round trip costs days. Contributors who send patches like Theo's get merged in hours and noticed; contributors who send patches like Sam's get asked for changes, and often drift away.

None of the checks looked at whether the code was good. They are the cheap, mechanical checks, and that's the point: by passing them, you spend the maintainer's attention only on the part that needs a human, the design.

From patches to review

The step from regular contributor to reviewer comes from doing reviewer's work before you're asked. Read other people's pull requests and leave useful comments: a failing case you tried, a question about a design choice, a pointer to a related issue. Triage new issues: reproduce them, label them, find the duplicates. Answer questions from users. Be consistent over months; maintainers notice who shows up every week. When your review comments start matching what the maintainers would have said, they'll start asking you to review, and eventually give you the permission to approve.

A good review, there as at work, is specific, kind and prioritised. Say what must change before merging and what's merely a suggestion; Conventional Comments' labels, such as issue, suggestion and nitpick, make the difference explicit. Explain why, so the contributor learns something. And review the design before the details: a perfect variable name in the wrong approach is wasted effort for everyone.

Being a good citizen

Maintainer burnout is the commonest way open-source projects die. Every issue that says "this is broken, fix it" without a reproduction, every complaint about the pace of a volunteer project, every unreviewable thousand-line patch, adds to it. Be the opposite. Say thank you. Close your own stale pull requests. If your company depends on a project, say so, and ask whether it can sponsor the maintainers or give engineers time to help. A project where several companies' engineers share the maintenance survives the departure of any one person; a project with a single exhausted maintainer, however popular, does not.

Common traps
  • A first contribution that's a large feature, unannounced, in a project you don't know yet.
  • Mixing a fix with reformatting or renaming, so the real change is buried in the diff.
  • No test, so nothing proves the fix, and nothing stops the bug returning.
  • A message that says what, not why, or nothing at all.
  • Treating maintainers as a support desk, when most are volunteers with little time.

Chapter 06

Design documents

11 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

A design document is how a senior engineer changes a system before writing the code: by thinking it through in writing, and letting sceptical colleagues find the flaws while they're cheap. A good one says what it proposes in its first paragraph, puts numbers on its goals, admits what it won't do, shows the alternatives it rejected and why, and says how it will roll out and roll back. Most weak documents fail the same few checks, and this chapter automates them.

What a design document is for

A design document does three jobs. It's a tool for thinking: writing the design down forces the vague parts into the open, and many designs change, or die, while the document is being written. It's a tool for agreement: the people whose systems the change touches can object before a line of code depends on the design, when changing it costs an afternoon rather than a quarter. And it's a record: a year later, when someone asks why the system works this way, the document answers, alternatives and all.

Not every change needs one. A design document earns its cost when a change is hard to reverse, crosses team boundaries, or has several plausible approaches. A one-week change inside your own service, with an obvious design, needs a good pull request description instead. The test is simple: if someone could reasonably say "why didn't you just...?" after it's built, write the document first.

The shape of a good one

Templates vary between companies, but the strong ones share a shape, because each section answers a question a reviewer will otherwise ask.

Two sections do most of the work and are most often missing. Non-goals prevent the scope argument: "personalised ranking is a separate project" stops a reviewer asking why the design doesn't handle it, and stops the project growing to include it. Alternatives considered is where a document earns trust. A proposal with no alternatives reads as the first idea its author had; one that shows two alternatives, each tested or priced, and explains why it lost, reads as a decision. Always include doing nothing, with its cost, because it's the alternative a sceptical manager is weighing.

Writing for sceptical readers

The syllabus asks for documents that "survive contact with sceptical senior engineers". Sceptics aren't obstacles; they're the cheapest testing your design will ever get. Write for them. Put the proposal in the first paragraph, so nobody has to read four pages to find out what you want. Replace adjectives with numbers: not "search is slow" but "p95 is 2.4 seconds at 40 queries a second". State your assumptions where they can be attacked. And anticipate the obvious objection in the text: if everyone will ask "why not just add an index?", answer it in the alternatives section before they do.

Keep it short. A design document that takes an hour to read gets skimmed; one that takes fifteen minutes gets read. Move detail into appendices, and link to the prototype rather than describing it. Diagrams help when they show the change, the new box and the arrows that move, not the whole system.

A first-pass reviewer

Most weak documents fail the same mechanical checks, long before anyone argues with the design: no stated proposal, goals without numbers, adjectives where measurements should be, no alternatives, risks with no mitigation, a rollout with no way back. The example runs those checks on two drafts of the same document, a search migration, the first as a first draft often looks and the second after revision.

What the example shows

The weak draft fails before the design is discussed. Forty-nine words produce eight notes: six missing sections, no stated proposal, and four sentences whose only content is adjectives: slow, faster, scalable, robust, easy, significantly, seamless. A reviewer handed this document has nothing to agree or disagree with. "Make search much faster" can't be wrong, which is exactly why it isn't useful.

The revised draft gives the reviewer something to argue with. It states the proposal and its headline number in the first paragraph, gives a measurable goal three times today's peak load, rules two things out of scope, prices two alternatives and the cost of doing nothing, gives each risk a mitigation, and has a rollout whose rollback is a flag. Its one note is a false alarm: "the slow queries are prefix matches" uses "slow" to identify which queries, not to make a claim. That's worth remembering. A check like this is a checklist, not a judge. It finds what's missing; it can't tell you whether the design is good. That's what the sceptical humans are for.

Running the review

Send the document to the right readers, not everyone: the owners of every system it touches, someone who will use the result, and at least one person you expect to disagree. Give them a few days to comment in the document, where each objection gets a thread and an answer. Hold a meeting only if threads remain unresolved, and spend it only on those. Record the decision and its date at the top, so readers know whether they're reading a proposal or a plan.

Decisions outlive their documents, and documents go stale as designs change during building. Two habits help. Update the document when the design changes, or mark it superseded with a link to its replacement. And for the individual decisions inside it, keep short architecture decision records, the format Michael Nygard proposed: a title, the context, the decision and its consequences, one decision per file, numbered, never edited after acceptance, only superseded. A folder of them is the history of why your system is the way it is.

Surviving contact

The hardest part of a design review isn't technical. When a reviewer finds a real flaw, the right response is thanks, because they just saved you weeks. Separate the design from yourself: the goal is the best design, not your design. Change your mind visibly when the evidence says so, and say what changed it; colleagues trust engineers who can be persuaded far more than ones who can't. And when a decision goes against you after a fair hearing, commit to it fully. "Disagree and commit" works only if the commitment is real, and nothing damages a team's trust faster than someone quietly building the design they lost.

Common traps
  • Burying the proposal on page four, after the history of the problem.
  • Goals without numbers, so nobody can tell later whether it worked.
  • No alternatives, or straw-man alternatives built only to lose.
  • A rollout with no rollback, for a change that will need one.
  • Defending the design instead of testing it, and treating sceptics as opponents.

Chapter 07

Trade-offs, argued both ways

11 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

Taste in trade-offs means being able to argue both sides honestly, and then knowing which fact would settle the argument. Write the uncertain inputs as ranges, not single guesses, run the arithmetic over many possible futures, and look for the assumption that swings the answer. That assumption, not the conclusion, is what the debate should be about. And spend that care where a decision is hard to reverse; most aren't.

Every option costs something

An engineering decision with no downside isn't a decision; it's a chore. The real ones trade something for something: latency for consistency, flexibility for simplicity, money for operational burden, speed now for change later. Juniors argue for the option they like. Seniors can state the best case for each option, including the ones they'd vote against, and say under what conditions each would win. The syllabus calls this "taste in trade-offs, which means being able to argue both sides honestly", and it's a learnable skill with a method.

The method starts by noticing that most trade-off arguments are disguised disagreements about facts. Two engineers arguing "managed service or run our own" usually agree on the costs once they're written down. What they disagree about is a number neither has stated: how fast traffic will grow, how much time running the system would take. Make those numbers explicit, as ranges, and the argument changes from opinion to evidence.

Ranges, not points

A single estimate, "it'll take a quarter of an engineer to run", hides how unsure you are. A range, "somewhere between 15% and 60% of an engineer", states it, and invites the right question: what would narrow it? Once every uncertain input is a range, the arithmetic is simple: draw one value of each input from its range, compute the cost of each option, and repeat thousands of times. Each repetition is one possible future. The result isn't one number but a distribution: how often each option wins, and by how much.

This is called Monte Carlo simulation, and it sounds grander than it is. It's twenty lines of Python and a few honest ranges. Douglas Hubbard's How to Measure Anything argues that the ranges themselves are the valuable part: people asked for a 90% range on a quantity they "can't estimate" usually give a perfectly usable one, and the act of giving it shows which inputs are genuinely uncertain.

Find the assumption that decides it

Not every uncertainty matters equally. Some inputs, however unsure, barely move the answer; one or two decide it. To find them, split the simulated futures by each input, the half where it was low and the half where it was high, and see how much the answer changes between the halves. The input with the biggest swing is the one to argue about, and the one worth paying to measure. Hubbard calls this the value of information: measure what could change the decision, not what's easy to measure.

The example applies this to a familiar choice: three years of running your own event-streaming cluster against a managed service, with traffic growth, the share of an engineer spent operating it, the number of serious incidents and their cost all given as ranges.

What the example shows

The answer is "managed, probably". The managed service is cheaper in 79% of the simulated futures, with a median three-year cost of $488,000 against $638,000. That's a useful answer, and a more honest one than a single number, because it admits a one-in-five chance of being wrong.

Growth decides it. When traffic growth was in the low half of its range, managed won 96% of the time; in the high half, only 62%. No other input swung the answer by more than 22 points. The table then shows the boundary: running your own wins only if traffic grows two and a half times a year or more and operating it takes little time, or at three times a year. So the right conversation isn't "managed or our own?" in the abstract. It's "how confident are we that traffic will more than double every year for three years?" That's a question the product and finance teams can help answer, which turns an engineering argument into a shared one.

The model is simple on purpose. It leaves out lock-in, the cost of migrating away later, and the learning that running your own brings. Leaving things out is fine if you say so, and the omissions are a good place for the other side's case to start.

Arguing the other side

Two techniques make it practical. The first is to steelman: write the strongest case for the option you're against, as its best advocate would, before arguing against it. If you can't write a paragraph your opponent would sign, you don't yet understand the trade-off. The second is the pre-mortem, from the psychologist Gary Klein: imagine it's a year later and your chosen option has failed badly, then write down why. People who struggle to criticise a plan they've just agreed to find it easy to explain a failure that has "already happened", and the reasons they give are the risks to plan for.

One-way and two-way doors

In his 2015 letter to shareholders, Jeff Bezos distinguished two kinds of decision. Some are one-way doors: hard or impossible to reverse, such as choosing a database for a system that will hold years of data, or publishing an API others will build on. These deserve slow, careful analysis. Most decisions are two-way doors: if you're wrong, you walk back through. Treating every decision as a one-way door is how organisations become slow. Part of taste is knowing which kind you face. The managed-service choice is closer to a two-way door than it looks, if you keep your code behind an interface and your data exportable, and making a decision more reversible is often cheaper than analysing it more carefully.

Common traps
  • Arguing conclusions when the disagreement is really about an unstated number.
  • Single-point estimates that hide how uncertain each input is.
  • Measuring what's easy instead of the assumption that decides the answer.
  • Straw-manning the other option, so the comparison proves nothing.
  • Treating every decision as irreversible, and analysing two-way doors for weeks.

Chapter 08

Writing in public

9 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

A body of written work compounds more than any certificate: every post that explains something well keeps working, for readers you'll never meet, for years. Write about what you learned the hard way, in your area, regularly. And write so it gets read: lead with the point, prefer concrete facts to adjectives, cut the hedges and the throat-clearing, and say who did what. Most of those habits can be checked mechanically, and the example checks them.

Why write where others can read it

Writing in public does four things at once. It clarifies: an explanation you've had to write down, for readers who can't interrupt, is one you actually understand, and the gaps show while you write. It compounds: a good post on why your migration locked a table answers the same question for hundreds of engineers who search for it later, without any further effort from you. It builds a reputation in your area that no job title gives: when people in your field know your name from your writing, opportunities find you. And it helps people, which is reason enough.

The syllabus asks for "a written archive others cite". That's a high bar on purpose. It doesn't mean a viral post. It means years of steady, useful writing in one area, until other people link to it when they explain the subject. That's achievable for anyone who writes something worthwhile every month or so and keeps going.

What to write

The best material is whatever cost you the most to learn. The incident that took a day to diagnose, the migration that went wrong, the library behaviour that wasn't documented, the paper you finally understood by building it: each is a post that saves someone else the same day. Explanations of your area, written at the level of a capable engineer who doesn't know it yet, are always needed. Notes on what you read, the one-page summaries chapter 2 suggested, are publishable with a little polish. And small posts count. Simon Willison's habit of publishing short "today I learned" notes, hundreds of them, built one of the most useful archives in the field. A post doesn't need to be long; it needs to be true, specific and findable.

Check what you can share. Anything about your employer's systems needs their permission, and many companies have a review process for engineering posts. Incidents can usually be described without customer data or confidential numbers, and the lesson rarely depends on them.

Writing that gets read

Readers decide in a sentence or two whether to keep going. Put the most important thing first, the way a news story does: what happened, or what the reader will learn. Use specific facts instead of adjectives: "locked the orders table for eleven minutes" rather than "caused some issues". Keep sentences short enough to read in one breath. Say who did what: "we ran the migration", not "the migration was run", because the passive voice hides the actor, and in technical writing the actor is usually the point. Cut the hedges, "I think", "perhaps", "quite", "basically", which make a writer sound unsure without making them more accurate. And cut the throat-clearing, "In this post I want to talk about", which spends the reader's patience before saying anything.

These are habits, so they can be checked. The example measures a draft opening of a blog post about a migration that went wrong, and its rewrite: sentence length, a readability score, and the hedges, throat-clearing and passive constructions in each.

What the example shows

The draft spends its opening on nothing. Its first sentence, "In this post I want to talk a little bit about something that I think is quite important", contains three hedges and a throat-clearing phrase, and tells the reader nothing. Five passive constructions, "mistakes were made by us", "the table was locked by it", keep the events at arm's length. Its sentences average 28 words.

The rewrite starts with the event. "A migration locked our orders table for eleven minutes on a Tuesday afternoon." The reader knows what happened, how bad it was and when, in one sentence, and the next, "Nobody could check out", says why it mattered. Sentences average 15 words, the reading-ease score rises from 58 to 66, and the passives are gone. The one "hedge" left is a false alarm: "nobody could check out" uses "could" literally. Like the design-document reviewer in chapter 6, this checker finds candidates, and a human decides.

The readability formula, Rudolf Flesch's reading ease from 1948, is crude. It counts only sentence and word length, and its syllable counting here is a heuristic. Use it to compare drafts of the same text, never as a target in itself.

Publishing, and keeping going

Publish where you own the archive. A blog on your own domain, even a simple static one, keeps working when platforms change their rules or disappear, and every link to it builds on the same address. Cross-post to community sites if you like, but keep the canonical copy at home. A company engineering blog is a fine second outlet, with a review process that protects you as well as them.

Consistency beats brilliance. One useful post a month, for three years, is thirty-six posts in your area, which is more than most experts have written. Talks multiply the effect: a post that's been well received is most of a conference talk, and a talk is most of a post. Find one reader who'll tell you honestly where a draft loses them, and edit before publishing: most first drafts improve by losing a third of their words, as The Engineer's Craft argues for internal documents too.

Common traps
  • Waiting until you're an expert to write, when explaining what you just learned is the most useful writing there is.
  • Opening with throat-clearing, so readers leave before the point.
  • Adjectives instead of facts, "significant", "robust", "slow".
  • Publishing only on a platform you don't control, so the archive can vanish.
  • Writing once, brilliantly, then never again, when the value is in the archive.

Chapter 09

Estimating honestly

11 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

A plan built by adding up each task's most likely duration is very unlikely to be met, because it ignores how much worse things can go than they can go well, and because waiting for parallel work makes every join later. Estimate each task as a range, combine the ranges along the real dependencies, and talk about dates as probabilities. Commit to a date you're 85% likely to meet, find the tasks that decide it, and update the forecast as you learn.

Why estimates fail

Engineers are often told they're bad at estimating, and the evidence agrees: projects routinely take far longer than planned. In 1979, Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky named the planning fallacy: people estimate their own tasks by imagining how they'll go, a best-case story, and ignore how similar tasks actually went. But optimism is only part of it. Even an honest estimator, who gives the true most likely duration for every task, produces a plan that's usually late, for two mechanical reasons.

The first is skew. A task estimated at five days might finish in four if everything goes well, but in ten if the third-party sandbox is broken or the requirements turn out to be ambiguous. Durations can't go much below their most likely value, and can go far above it, so the average is later than the most likely. Add up the most likely values and you've added up numbers that are each below their task's average. The second is merge bias. When a task waits for two others, it waits for the later of the two, and the later of two uncertain things is later than either is likely to be on its own. Every join in a plan pushes the end date out.

Ranges, and the graph

The cure is to estimate each task as three numbers, best case, most likely and worst case, and to combine them along the dependencies rather than adding them up. That combination is hard by hand and easy by simulation: draw a duration for every task from its range, compute when the project finishes given what waits for what, and repeat thousands of times. The result is a distribution of end dates, from which you read percentiles: P50, the date you're as likely to beat as to miss, and P85, the date you'll meet five times in six.

The simulation also says which tasks matter. In each run, the critical path is the chain of dependent tasks that determined the end date. A task that's on the critical path in most runs decides the schedule, and it's where to spend attention; a task that's rarely on it can slip without consequence. The example builds a seven-task project, the launch of a checkout feature, and does all of this.

What the example shows

The plan's date is a 2% chance. Adding the most likely durations along the longest chain gives seventeen days, the kind of number that goes in a slide. In the simulation, the project finished by day 17 in only 2% of runs. The median was 22 days and P85 was 25.2. Nobody was pessimistic or careless: every task's most likely value was honest. The plan was wrong because of skew and joins, and it would have been wrong for anyone who made it this way.

The task that decides the date isn't on the plan's critical path. Design, the API, the load test and the launch are on the critical path in every run, as they must be. But the UI was on it in only 57% of runs, and the payments integration, absent from the planned path, decided the end date in 43%. Its most likely four days hide a worst case of fifteen, a third-party sandbox that's usually fine and sometimes not. That's where a lead should look first: start it earlier, prototype against the sandbox in week one, or find a fallback.

Waiting for two things takes longer. One task estimated at six days, best four and worst twelve, had a median of 7.1 days. Waiting for the later of two such tasks had a median of 8.2. Every join in a plan quietly adds time like this, and a plan with many parallel streams merging into one launch adds a lot.

Talking about dates

Steve McConnell's Software Estimation separates three things that conversations about dates mix up. An estimate is a prediction: what you think will happen, with its uncertainty. A target is a wish: when the business would like it. A commitment is a promise: the date you agree to be held to. Most estimation arguments are a target being presented as an estimate, or an estimate being heard as a commitment. Name which one you're discussing.

Give estimates as ranges with confidence: "most likely around day 22; 85% confident of day 25". Commit at your P85, not your P50, because a P50 commitment is missed half the time, and repeated misses destroy trust faster than conservative dates. Update the forecast every week as tasks finish and their ranges collapse to actual values. Early in a project the range is wide; it should narrow as you go, and if it doesn't, the project has a problem worth raising now. And when the date you can honestly commit to isn't the target, say so early and offer choices: a later date, less scope, or a different approach. Leaders are trusted for bad news delivered early.

Brooks's law

Fred Brooks's The Mythical Man-Month, from 1975, states the most famous law of software management: adding people to a late project makes it later. New people need weeks to become productive, and someone who knows the project has to teach them, so the team slows down before it speeds up. And communication grows faster than headcount: with n people there are n(n−1)/2 pairs who may need to stay in sync, three channels for three people, sixty-six for twelve. Brooks called his book's subject "the bearing of a child takes nine months, no matter how many women are assigned". Some work divides well; much doesn't. When a project is late, cutting scope or removing a blocker usually helps more than adding people.

Learning from your own record

The best correction for the planning fallacy is your own history. Bent Flyvbjerg's reference class forecasting, used for large infrastructure projects, starts from how long similar past projects actually took, not from the details of this one. You can do the same at a small scale: keep a record of each task's estimate and its actual duration. After a few months, the ratio of actual to estimated, perhaps 1.4 on average, with a long tail, tells you how to adjust, and its spread tells you how wide your ranges should be. Teams that keep this record estimate better within a year, not because they become less optimistic but because they calibrate.

Common traps
  • Adding up most-likely durations and calling the sum a date.
  • Committing at P50, and missing half of your commitments.
  • Ignoring the task with the long worst case, because its most likely value is small.
  • Treating a target as an estimate, or an estimate as a commitment.
  • Adding people to a late project, instead of cutting scope or removing a blocker.

Chapter 10

Leading a project

10 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

Leading a project end to end means owning its outcome, not its code: what's in scope, in what order the work happens, what could kill it, and whether it really lands. Most of the skill is in the order. Do the work that could change the plan first, while little has been built on top of it, and do the comfortable work later. The same project, sequenced risk first, discovers its bad news weeks earlier and throws away far less.

What leading means

A technical lead on a project is rarely the person who writes the most code. They're the person who makes sure the project succeeds: that it's solving the right problem, that the plan is realistic, that the risks are known and handled, that the people who depend on it know what's happening, and that it actually finishes. Tanya Reilly's The Staff Engineer's Path describes this as holding the whole project in your head, so that nothing important is nobody's job. The syllabus lists the parts: scoping, sequencing, risk, stakeholders and landing it. This chapter covers all but stakeholders, which have chapter 11.

Scoping

Start by writing down what "done" means, in a sentence a stakeholder would agree with: "customers can refund an order from the admin screen, and the money reaches their card within a week". Then find the smallest version of the project that achieves it, and list everything else as explicitly out of scope, in the design document's non-goals. Every project grows during its life, and most of the growth is reasonable on its own; the scope list is what lets you say "that's a good idea for phase two" without an argument.

Keep a cut list from the start: the parts you'd drop first if the project ran late, agreed with the stakeholders in advance. Agreeing to cuts when nobody is under pressure is easy. Agreeing to them in the last week, with the date slipping, is a negotiation nobody wins.

Sequencing

Most project plans order work by what's comfortable or what's logically first: build the data model, then the screens, then the reports, and leave the integrations and the hard questions until the pieces they connect exist. That ordering has a hidden cost. The hard questions, will the bank's API support refunds, will the risk team accept rules instead of a model, are the ones whose answers can change the plan, and every week they wait, more work is built on assumptions that might be wrong.

The alternative is to sequence by risk: of the tasks you can start, do first the one with the most uncertainty per day of work, so bad news arrives while it's still cheap. Two techniques sharpen it. A spike is a short, throwaway experiment that answers one question, two days of prototype calls against the bank's sandbox, before committing to the work that depends on the answer. A walking skeleton, Alistair Cockburn's term, is the thinnest end-to-end version of the system, every component present but barely functional, built first so that integration problems appear in week one instead of the last week. The example measures what sequencing is worth.

What the example shows

Something will probably go wrong. The eight tasks each have a chance of revealing a problem that changes the plan, mostly small, but the bank integration has a 35% chance and the fraud rules 20%. Together, at least one goes badly 66% of the time. That's realistic for a project with external dependencies, and it means the plan should assume a surprise, not hope to avoid one.

The order decides what the surprise costs. Comfortable first, the median first surprise came on day 33 of 44, after most of the work was done, and on average 12.5 days of work were thrown away. Riskiest first, respecting the real dependencies, it came on day 7, and the average waste fell to 5.5 days. That's a week of work saved per project, from nothing but the order of the same tasks, plus five extra weeks to react to bad news while there's still room to.

A spike buys time, more than it saves work. Two days of prototypes against the two riskiest unknowns moved the first surprise from day 7 to day 2 while saving little more work. What it bought was knowing early. On a real project, knowing in week one that the bank doesn't support partial refunds means the product decision, cut the feature or change the design, happens while the team is still flexible, which is usually worth more than the days.

Managing risk, on purpose

Keep a risk register: a short list, reviewed weekly, of what could go wrong. Each entry has a description, a rough likelihood and impact, an owner, the mitigation, and a trigger, the observable sign that the risk is happening. "Bank sandbox doesn't support refunds; likely; high impact; owner Ana; mitigation: spike in week 1, fallback of manual refunds; trigger: the refund endpoint returns an error after a day of trying." Ten lines like that, kept current, are worth more than a hundred-row spreadsheet nobody reads.

For a risky project, decide kill criteria before starting: the results that would mean stopping or changing course. "If the spike shows the bank can't support refunds through its API, we stop and propose manual refunds instead." Decided in advance, a kill criterion is a plan. Decided afterwards, under the weight of the work already done, it's a fight against the sunk-cost fallacy, which usually wins.

Landing it

Projects die in their last ten percent. The feature works, the team's attention moves on, and what's left, migrating the last customers, deleting the old code path and its feature flag, writing the runbook, handing over to the team that will own it, never quite happens. The old and new systems then run side by side for years, and the project's real benefit, simplicity, never arrives. A project is done when the old path is gone, the documentation is written, the on-call team knows how to run it, and the success measure from the scope has been checked. Plan that work explicitly, with dates, from the start.

Then close it properly. Tell everyone who depended on it that it's finished and what changed. Hold a short retrospective, what to repeat and what to change, while memories are fresh. And thank the people who did the work, by name, where their managers can see it. Landing a project well is what makes people want to work on your next one.

Common traps
  • Ordering work by comfort, so the questions that could change the plan are answered last.
  • No agreed cut list, so scope negotiations happen in the final week.
  • A risk register that's written once and never read again.
  • No kill criteria, so a failing project continues on sunk cost.
  • Declaring victory at launch, with the old path, the flag and the handover still undone.

Chapter 11

Status, stakeholders and influence

10 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

A project that's going well but communicated badly looks like a project in trouble, and one in trouble but communicated well can still be saved. Know who depends on the project and what each of them needs. Write status updates that put the bottom line first, take their colour from the forecast rather than from mood, and ask for decisions by a date. And since a lead rarely has authority over the people a project needs, learn to move them by persuasion, relationships and clear writing.

Who needs to know

A project's stakeholders are everyone who's affected by it, decides something about it, or must do something for it: the sponsor who funds it, the product lead who decides its scope, the partner teams whose systems it touches, the support team who'll answer questions about it, the engineers who build it. They don't all need the same thing. A useful exercise at the start is to list them and sort them by two questions, the grid that management texts attribute to Aubrey Mendelow: how much influence do they have over the project, and how much does it affect them? The sponsor, high on both, needs regular, short updates and early warning. A partner team that must change an API needs to know exactly what you need and by when. Someone affected but with little say needs to be told before the change reaches them, not after.

Status updates that get read

Most status updates are activity logs: "this week we continued working on the integration and had several meetings". They tell the reader that people were busy, which they assumed, and nothing they need: is it on track, what's the risk, what do you need from me? A useful update answers those in its first two lines, in the order a busy reader needs them. Military writing calls this BLUF, bottom line up front.

The status colour deserves particular care, because it's the one thing everyone reads. Green, amber and red should come from the forecast, not from the lead's mood or fear of looking bad. One workable rule: green if the P85 forecast meets the committed date, amber if only the median does, and red if the median misses it. A rule makes the colour honest and removes the temptation to report "green" until the week the date is missed, the pattern known as the watermelon project: green outside, red inside. The example writes a week's update from the project's own data, for three audiences.

What the example shows

The colour is red, and that's useful. The project is only 43% likely to ship by its committed day 30, and its median forecast, day 30.5, is just past it. Under the rule, that's red, even though the project is close. A red that early, with three weeks of work left and a clear cause, isn't a failure; it's the update doing its job, a warning while there's still time to act. The same project reported as green "because we're nearly there" would become a surprise in three weeks, which is the thing stakeholders never forgive.

Every audience gets its bottom line, and an ask. Leadership gets two lines: the colour with its probability, and a decision needed from the product lead by day 18, whether to ship without partial refunds if the bank can't support them by day 20. Asking for a decision by a date, before the date at which it matters, is what turns a status report from information into action. Partner teams get the blocker and its cost: if the bank's refunds endpoint holds the work up another week, the median moves from day 30.5 to day 35.5. That's five days for five days, because the blocked task is on the critical path. Saying it in days makes the cost concrete to a team that has other priorities. The team gets the detail, and the reason to finish the bank integration first: it has the widest range.

Influence without authority

A technical lead usually can't tell anyone outside their own team what to do, and often not inside it either. Projects need other teams to change their systems, review designs, and give up some of their own priorities. The way this happens is persuasion, and persuasion is a skill with a few reliable parts.

Build the relationship before you need it. The partner team you've helped, reviewed for, or simply talked to, will find time for your request; the one that first hears from you when you need something will put it in a queue. Understand what they want. Every team has goals and pressures; a request framed in terms of their goals, "this also removes the manual reconciliation your on-call does every week", moves faster than one framed in yours. Write it down. A short, clear proposal, the problem, the request, the cost and the deadline, can be forwarded to the person who actually decides, and survives being forwarded. And agree before the meeting. Toyota's practice of nemawashi, talking a proposal through with each person affected before the formal decision, means the meeting confirms an agreement instead of starting an argument.

When persuasion fails and the project is genuinely blocked, escalate well: as a request for a decision, not a complaint. Say what's blocked, what it costs, the options and what you recommend, and copy the other party, so nothing is said behind their back. A good escalation is one the other team would describe as fair. It's how a lead uses the management chain without spending trust.

Meetings, briefly

A meeting is an expensive way to share information and a good way to make a decision. Hold decision meetings with a written pre-read, so the time goes on the disagreement rather than on explaining the background. Finish by reading back the decisions, the owners and the dates, and send them in writing within the hour. Most "we agreed on this" disputes come from meetings whose decisions weren't written down.

Common traps
  • Status updates that are activity logs, with no bottom line and no ask.
  • Colour from mood, green until the week the date is missed.
  • Asking for a decision without a deadline, or after the date it matters.
  • Meeting partner teams for the first time when you need something.
  • Escalating as a complaint, behind the other team's back, instead of as a request for a decision.

Chapter 12

Mentoring

11 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

Mentoring is the most leveraged work a senior engineer does, and it's a skill, not a personality trait. Start from what the other person actually knows, which is often less solid than what they've used. Teach at the edge of that knowledge, in an order where each idea rests on ones already secure. Give feedback that's specific enough to act on. And sponsor as well as advise: use your standing to give people the opportunities that make them grow.

Why mentor deliberately

An engineer who makes three colleagues 20% more effective has added more than most engineers add by their own work, and the effect compounds: those colleagues mentor others. That's the leverage the syllabus means by "the ability to move other engineers". There's a second reason, which the syllabus also names: "teaching is the fastest way to find the holes in your own model". Explaining something to a sharp junior engineer, who asks why at each step, exposes every place where your understanding is a memorised rule rather than a reason. Richard Feynman is often credited with the test: if you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough.

Most mentoring happens by accident: a junior engineer absorbs what they can from code reviews and overheard conversations. Deliberate mentoring does better, because it starts from a diagnosis, follows a plan, and checks whether the plan is working.

Mentor, coach, sponsor

Lara Hogan, writing about engineering management, distinguishes three roles that are often blurred. A mentor gives advice from experience: "here's how I'd approach this". A coach asks questions that help the other person find their own answer: "what have you tried, and what would you try next?" A sponsor uses their standing to create opportunities: recommending someone for a stretch project, naming them in a meeting they're not in, putting their work in front of people who decide promotions. Each has its place. Advice is fastest for facts and conventions; coaching builds judgement, which can't be handed over; and sponsorship is what changes careers, especially for people whose work tends to be overlooked. A good mentor moves between all three and knows which one the moment calls for.

Start from what they really know

People describe what they know by what they've used. A mentee who says they know query planning may mean they've read EXPLAIN output and added indexes when told to. That's real, useful knowledge, and it may rest on nothing: they may not know what a B-tree is, how a page is read from disk, or where the planner's row estimates come from. Knowledge like that is fragile. It works for the cases they've seen and fails, confusingly, on the first case that depends on the missing foundation.

So start with a diagnosis, done kindly: ask them to explain, not to recall. "Walk me through what happens when this query runs" reveals far more than "do you know indexes?". Then map what you learn against what each idea builds on. The example does this for a mentee learning database internals: a small graph of concepts and what each needs, the four the mentee says they know, and three questions. What is fragile, what are they ready to learn now, and in what order should they learn their way to crash recovery?

What the example shows

Three of the four things they know are fragile. Indexes rest on B-trees, pages and the buffer pool, and files and fsync, none of which they know; query plans rest on those and on statistics; replication rests on the write-ahead log. Only transactions stand on their own. That's typical of engineers who learned on the job: competent at the top of the stack, with gaps underneath. It isn't a criticism; it's a map of where their next bug will confuse them.

The frontier is at the bottom. The concepts they're ready to learn now, everything they need already known, are files and fsync, and statistics. Teaching anything else first would mean explaining an idea in terms of others they don't have, which is how explanations lose people. The path to their goal, crash recovery, is four steps, and starts with files and fsync. The example's last line is the practical advice: before teaching anything on the path, ask them to explain its first step. If they can, you're further along than the map says; if they can't, you've found the place to begin.

Teaching at the edge

Lev Vygotsky called it the zone of proximal development: what a learner can't yet do alone but can do with help. Teaching works there and nowhere else. Below it, they're bored; above it, they're lost. The example's frontier is that zone, computed. In practice, three techniques work well. Worked examples, then faded guidance: show a complete solution, then give a similar problem with part of the solution, then one with none. Pairing, then reversing: you drive while they watch and ask; then they drive while you watch and ask. Questions before answers: when they bring a problem, ask what they've tried and what they think is happening before saying what you think. The answer they reach themselves is the one they'll remember, and the question shows them how you think, which is what they most need to learn.

Code review is the mentoring most engineers already do, and it's easy to do it badly. Explain why, not just what: "this query runs once per order, so a page of 100 orders makes 101 queries" teaches; "use select_related" doesn't. Separate what must change from suggestions and nitpicks, and say which is which. And comment on what's good, specifically, because people repeat what's noticed.

Feedback that helps

Feedback is useful when it's specific, timely, about behaviour rather than personality, and private if it's critical. The situation–behaviour–impact model, from the Center for Creative Leadership, gives it a shape: describe the situation, the specific behaviour, and its impact. "In yesterday's incident, you posted updates in the channel every ten minutes, and support could answer customers without asking us" is feedback someone can repeat. "Great job in the incident" is a pleasantry. The same shape works for criticism, and keeps it about something the person can change.

Sponsorship

Advice helps people grow; opportunities make them grow faster. Give your mentee the stretch work: the design document for the next project, the lead on a small incident review, the talk at the team meeting. Back them while they do it, and let them get the credit, in public. Recommend them for things when they're not in the room, by name. And give away the work you'd enjoy doing yourself, because the interesting work is exactly what someone growing needs. The sign that mentoring has worked isn't that they ask you fewer questions; it's that they start answering other people's.

Common traps
  • Taking "I know it" at face value, instead of asking for an explanation.
  • Teaching above the frontier, in terms of ideas the learner doesn't have yet.
  • Answering every question at once, so the learner never builds their own judgement.
  • Feedback too vague to act on, praise or criticism without a specific behaviour.
  • Mentoring without sponsoring, advice in private and no opportunities in public.

Exercise 12.3Six weeks to crash recovery

Using the example's path, plan six weeks of mentoring, an hour a week, to get the mentee to explain crash recovery.

Show solution

Week 1: files and fsync. Have them write a small program that writes a file, kills itself before and after fsync, and checks what survived; ask them to explain the results.   • Do you sponsor as well as mentor: stretch work, public credit, recommendations?

Chapter 13

Learning the business

11 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

Every engineering choice is also a business choice, and the engineers who are trusted with the biggest ones can say what their work is worth in the business's own units. Learn a handful of numbers: revenue, margin, what a customer is worth, what drives churn, what infrastructure costs per unit of work. Then price projects in money, write the assumption beside each number, and rank them by value per week of effort. It won't be precise. It will be arguable, which is far better than unspoken.

Why an engineer needs the numbers

The syllabus asks you to "learn the business: what your company sells, what it costs, where engineering moves the number". There's a practical reason. Senior engineers spend much of their time choosing what to work on, and arguing for it. "This would be cleaner" persuades other engineers. "This saves $110,000 a year and pays for itself in six weeks" persuades everyone, including the people who decide budgets. The second argument needs the numbers, and engineers who have them get a different kind of hearing.

The numbers also change what you think good engineering is. Reliability matters because downtime makes customers leave, not because uptime is a virtue. Performance matters where it changes conversion or cost, and hardly at all elsewhere. Once you see which numbers your work moves, the trade-offs in chapter 7 get much easier to argue.

The handful of numbers

A product business, at its simplest, runs on a few quantities. Revenue, and how it's earned: subscriptions, usage, transactions, advertising. Gross margin: the share of revenue left after the costs of serving customers, which for a software product are mostly infrastructure and support. Churn: the share of customers who leave each month. Lifetime value: what a customer is worth over their whole time as a customer, roughly the margin they bring each month divided by the churn rate. Conversion: the share of trials or visitors who become paying customers. And for an engineer, unit cost: what one request, one invoice or one gigabyte costs to serve.

You can learn your company's versions of these in a week if you ask. Finance teams are usually delighted that an engineer wants to understand the model. Public companies publish most of them in their annual reports. And the pricing page, the support queue and an hour with a salesperson teach the parts no spreadsheet shows: what customers actually buy, and what makes them angry.

Pricing engineering work

With those numbers, an engineering project can be priced in the same currency as every other business decision. The example does it for a small invoicing product, 4,000 paying teams at $49 a month, and three candidate projects: making checkout faster, caching invoice PDFs, and fixing the bulk-invoice failures that exit surveys blame for some churn. Each project's value comes from one assumption, written beside it in the code, where anyone can see it and argue with it.

What the example shows

A customer is worth about $1,360. With a 69% gross margin and 2.5% of customers leaving each month, the average customer stays 40 months and brings $1,360 of margin over that time. That single number makes the rest computable: anything that keeps a customer, or wins one, is worth about that much.

An outage costs less in refunds than in leaving. Customers pay $272 for each hour of service, so a four-hour outage refunded in full costs $1,089. If the same outage makes 1% of customers decide to leave, it costs $54,400 in lifetime margin, fifty times as much. That's why reliability work is worth far more than its "lost minutes" suggest, and why the right way to argue for it is through churn, not uptime percentages.

The smallest project goes first. Faster checkout is worth the most per year, $163,200, but takes six engineer-weeks. Caching invoice PDFs is worth less, $109,440, but takes three, so its value per week of effort, $36,480, is the highest, and it pays for itself in under six weeks. The bulk-invoice fix, at $10,200 a week of effort, comes last despite fixing a real customer pain. Ranking by value per week of effort is a simple form of what Don Reinertsen calls weighted shortest job first: since every week a valuable project waits is value lost, do first the work that delivers the most value per unit of time it occupies the team.

Every figure is an assumption. The conversion gain comes from one A/B test; the PDF share of the cloud bill from one month's invoice; the churn share from exit surveys. Writing each assumption beside its number, as the code does, is the point: it turns "I think checkout matters more" into "your A/B test may be optimistic, so let's check it", which is an argument that can be settled.

The cost of delay

Reinertsen's The Principles of Product Development Flow makes a claim worth remembering: if you quantify only one thing, quantify the cost of delay. It's the value lost for each week a piece of work isn't done, and it's what the ranking above really compares. Teams that don't quantify it make prioritisation decisions by volume and seniority, and routinely delay their most valuable work behind their loudest. Cost of delay also makes the case for small batches and finishing things, the subject of chapter 15: a project half done delivers nothing, so every week spent on two projects at once delays the value of both.

Speaking the business's language

Translate engineering work into the business's units: money, risk and time. Technical debt is interest: "our build takes twenty minutes, and with thirty engineers it costs about $660,000 a year in waiting", rather than "the build is slow". Reliability is churn and revenue at risk. Security is the expected cost of a breach, and the deals lost without a compliance certificate. Performance is conversion and infrastructure cost. None of these needs to be precise. A rough number with its assumption stated is enough to start a real conversation, and it's how engineering work competes fairly for time with everything else a business could do.

Common traps
  • Arguing for work by elegance to people who decide by value.
  • Pricing outages in lost minutes instead of customers who leave.
  • Ranking by total value, ignoring how long each project occupies the team.
  • Precise-looking numbers with hidden assumptions, which nobody can check.
  • Never asking finance for the model, and guessing at the numbers that matter.

Chapter 14

Measuring delivery

10 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

A decade of research on software teams found four measures that predict both speed and stability: how often you deploy, how long a change takes to reach production, how often a deploy causes a failure, and how quickly you recover. They can be computed from data you already have, your Git history and your incident log. Use them to learn, as a team, never to rank individuals, and pair every measure of speed with one of stability, so that improving one can't quietly wreck the other.

Four keys

From 2014, the research programme now called DORA, led by Nicole Forsgren with Jez Humble and Gene Kim, surveyed tens of thousands of engineers about how their teams worked and how their organisations performed. The book that summarised it, Accelerate, published in 2018, identified four measures of software delivery that separated high performers from low:

  • Deployment frequency: how often the team deploys to production.
  • Lead time for changes: how long a committed change takes to reach production.
  • Change failure rate: the share of deploys that cause a failure needing a fix.
  • Time to restore: how long it takes to recover from such a failure. DORA's recent reports call it failed deployment recovery time.

The first two measure speed; the last two, stability. The research's most quoted finding is that they don't trade against each other. The teams that deployed most often also had the lowest failure rates and recovered fastest. Speed and stability come from the same practices: small changes, automated testing and deployment, and fast feedback.

Computing them from your own history

You don't need a vendor's dashboard to know your numbers. Deploys are usually tags or pipeline runs; commits carry timestamps; incidents are in a log or a channel. The example builds a Git repository with six weeks of history, ninety commits, three a day, with deploy tags: for three weeks the team releases once a week, on Friday, and for three weeks it releases every afternoon. An incident log records which deploys caused failures and how long recovery took. The four keys are then computed from the repository itself, using git log between consecutive deploy tags.

What the example shows

The arithmetic first. Weekly releases bundle fifteen changes into each deploy, and a change committed on Monday morning waits until Friday afternoon, so the median lead time is 51 hours. Releasing daily, three changes go out each afternoon and the median lead time is three hours. That part isn't an assumption: it follows directly from the release schedule and the commit times, and it's the same arithmetic in any team.

The stability numbers come from an invented incident log, so read them for their direction, not their size. Two of the three weekly releases caused incidents, against two of the fifteen daily ones, and the weekly failures took far longer to recover from: 4.8 hours at the median against 0.6. The mechanism behind that direction is real, and it's why the research found what it did. A deploy of fifteen changes has fifteen places a problem could hide; finding which one means bisecting a large batch under pressure, and rolling back undoes all fifteen. A deploy of three changes fails less often, because there's less in it, and when it fails, the culprit is one of three changes written that day by people who still remember them.

Goodhart's law

The economist Charles Goodhart observed that a statistical regularity tends to collapse once it's used for control; the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern's paraphrase is the one everyone quotes: "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure". Delivery metrics are especially vulnerable. Set a target for deployment frequency and teams deploy empty changes. Rank engineers by commits and they split work into meaningless pieces. Punish change failures and failures stop being reported. Three rules keep the measures honest. Measure teams, never individuals, because software is delivered by teams and individual metrics are both unfair and gamed. Pair speed with stability, always reporting all four together, so that improving one at the expense of another is visible. And use them for learning, not judgement: the question each month is "what's slowing us down, and what would help?", not "who's behind?".

Learning from incidents

The incident log behind change failure rate is also the richest source of learning a team has, if incidents are examined well. A blameless postmortem asks how the failure happened, not who caused it, on the principle, from safety researchers such as Sidney Dekker, that people's actions made sense to them at the time with the information they had. Find out what that information was, and why it was misleading, and you'll find what to fix: a missing alert, a confusing dashboard, a deploy process that made the mistake easy. Blame finds a person to retrain and leaves the system that tripped them unchanged.

A useful postmortem has a timeline, the contributing factors, plural, since there's rarely one cause, what went well, and a short list of action items, each with an owner and a date. Then the part most teams skip: check, a month later, that the action items were done. The share of postmortem actions completed is itself a revealing measure of whether a team learns from its incidents or only documents them.

Common traps
  • Measuring speed without stability, or the reverse.
  • Ranking individuals by commits, deploys or tickets closed.
  • Setting the metric as a target, and getting empty deploys.
  • Blaming a person for an incident, and leaving the system that tripped them.
  • Postmortem action items that are never done, or never checked.

Chapter 15

Finishing things

10 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

The syllabus calls them the boring virtues: reliability, clear communication, finishing things. They're boring because they're not clever, and they're the reason some engineers are trusted with everything. Finishing has a mathematics, Little's law: at a given rate of completion, the more you have in progress, the longer each thing takes. Starting everything feels productive and makes everything slower. Limit what's in progress, finish before starting, and do what you said you'd do or say early that you won't.

The boring virtues

Ask engineers whom they'd most want on a difficult project and the answers are rarely the most brilliant people they know. They name the ones who do what they say they'll do, who say clearly what's happening, and who finish. Those virtues are boring in the sense that nobody writes papers about them, and they compound: every commitment kept makes the next request to you more likely, and every project finished frees attention for the next. An engineer with modest technical depth and these virtues is often more valuable than a brilliant one without them, and an engineer with both is the one the syllabus is trying to produce.

The previous chapters covered communication: design documents, status updates, writing in public. This chapter is about finishing, which turns out to have a precise mathematical basis, and about reliability, which is mostly a set of habits.

Little's law

In 1961, John Little proved a result that holds for almost any stable system where things arrive, wait, get worked on and leave: the average number of items in the system equals the rate at which they finish multiplied by the average time each spends inside. In symbols, L = λW. It needs no assumptions about the distribution of arrivals or of work, which is why it applies to request queues in a server, cars on a road and tickets on a team's board alike.

Rearranged, it says something uncomfortable about how teams work: the time each item takes, W, equals the number in progress, L, divided by the rate of finishing, λ. If a team finishes three items a week and has twelve in progress, each takes four weeks on average. Have twenty-four in progress at the same rate, and each takes eight. Starting more work doesn't finish it faster; at a given throughput, it only makes every item take longer. And in practice it lowers throughput too, because people juggling several items lose time switching between them, a cost Gerald Weinberg estimated at about a fifth of a person's time for each additional project they carry.

Starting everything, or limiting it

The example runs a team of four for two thousand days, with the same stream of requests, 0.75 a day on average, each needing two to six days of focused work, in two ways. In the first, every request is started as soon as it arrives, and people divide their time between everything in progress, losing a little to each extra item they juggle. In the second, the team works on at most four items at once, one each, and the rest wait in a queue until someone is free. Then it checks Little's law for both.

What the example shows

Both policies finish the same amount of work, 0.74 items a day, because at this load the team has enough capacity either way. The difference is entirely in how long each item takes.

Starting everything makes everything slower. With every request started at once, 5.4 items are in progress on average, people's attention is divided, and each item takes 7.1 days from start to finish. Limiting work in progress to four, 3.4 items are in progress on average and each takes 4.5 days. Little's law holds for both, to within the noise of the simulation: 5.35 against 0.74 × 7.14, and 3.36 against 0.74 × 4.51.

Waiting in a queue is faster than starting at once. The surprising number is lead time, from request to done. Under the work-in-progress limit, items wait in the queue for 1.7 days on average before anyone starts them, and they're still finished sooner, 6.2 days against 7.1. Starting a request immediately feels responsive to whoever asked, and it makes their request, and everyone else's, finish later. That's the case behind the Kanban slogan "stop starting, start finishing", and it applies to a person as much as to a team.

The example's numbers come from invented constants, and the switching cost is deliberately small. Exercise 15.1 shows what happens when the load rises, which is where the difference becomes dramatic.

Finishing, personally

The same law applies to one engineer's work. Five things in progress, a design document, two reviews, a bug and a migration, means each takes five times as long as it needs to, and the people waiting for each see slow progress on all of them. Keep one main thing, finish it, then take the next. Keep a "not now" list for everything else, which is honest about what's waiting rather than pretending everything is in progress. When an interruption comes, ask whether it's more important than the thing in hand; if it isn't, it goes on the list, with a date if the requester needs one.

Finishing also means finishing all of it. Chapter 10's last ten percent applies to every task: the pull request isn't done when it's approved but when it's deployed and verified; the bug isn't done when it's fixed but when the reporter knows; the document isn't done until the decision in it is recorded.

Reliability

Reliability in a person means that what they say will happen does. It's built from small habits. Write down every commitment you make, in one list, so none is forgotten. When you can't keep one, say so as soon as you know, not at the deadline, and offer what you can do instead; a renegotiated commitment costs a little trust, a missed one costs a lot. Follow up: when you ask someone for something, note when you expect it and check then. Close loops: tell people when the thing they asked about is done. Be on time. None of these is hard, which is exactly why doing them consistently is noticed. Over a few years, reliability is what makes people give you the important work, and the important work is what builds a career.

Common traps
  • Starting every request at once, to look responsive, and finishing everything later.
  • Five things in progress, each moving slowly, none finished.
  • "Done" that isn't: approved but not deployed, fixed but not communicated.
  • Missing a commitment silently, instead of renegotiating it early.
  • Commitments kept in your head, where some will be forgotten.

Chapter 16

A career that compounds

11 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

Skills, reputation, relationships and written work all compound, but only if they accumulate in one direction and you can see them. Keep a few lines a week about what you did and what it changed, because you won't remember, and the people deciding your next opportunity won't either. Work on problems that matter in your area. Build the body of work the stage asks for, slowly and with other people. And keep a pace you can sustain for decades, because the compounding only works if you're still going.

What compounds

Some things in a career grow on themselves. Depth in one area makes the next layer of it easier to learn. A reputation for reliable, clear work brings better projects, which build more reputation. Relationships with people who trust you bring opportunities you'd never have found. And written work, the archive from chapter 8, keeps finding new readers. Other things don't compound: a sequence of unrelated jobs, each started from scratch; work nobody saw or remembers; knowledge that left with the last project. The difference is mostly direction and visibility. Effort that adds to the same pile, and that someone can see, compounds; effort scattered across many piles, or invisible, doesn't.

Remembering what you did

Memory is a poor record of your own work. Six months later, the incident you resolved, the design you improved, the colleague you taught are a vague sense of having been busy. That matters when the moment comes to describe your work, in a performance review, a promotion case, an interview or a CV, because what you can't remember can't be counted. Julia Evans calls the cure a brag document: a running record of what you did and why it mattered. The name is half a joke; the practice is entirely serious.

Keep it simple enough to survive. A plain text file, a line or two each week, with the week, what you did, a tag for the kind of work, and, when you have one, the measured result. Five minutes on Friday afternoon is enough. The example takes three months of such a log and turns it into the summaries you'll need: what themes the work fell into, which have evidence, which weeks have nothing written down, and a first draft of the paragraph a review asks for.

What the example shows

The log knows things you'd forget. Twelve entries over twelve weeks show a quarter that combined design work, reliability, writing, leadership and mentoring, with measured results for half of them: a search migration that cut p95 from 2.4 seconds to 240 ms and support complaints from ten a week to one, a fixed export recovering 3,200 rows a night, alerting that caught two incidents before users did, a post with 4,100 readers. Without the log, the review would mention the search migration and little else.

The most frequent theme has no numbers, and that's fine. Mentoring appears four times and never with a measured result. Mentoring rarely produces numbers; its evidence is outcomes in other people. The log's last mentoring entry, "Priya led her first incident review on her own", is the strongest possible evidence of the earlier three, and it's exactly the kind of thing that's forgotten. Write down outcomes like that as carefully as you'd write down a latency figure.

The gaps are the weeks you won't remember. Weeks 5 and 10 have no entries. Perhaps nothing happened; more likely, something did and wasn't written down. The draft paragraph at the end isn't finished prose, but it's a far better start than a blank page the night before a review, and it's assembled entirely from evidence.

Kinds of senior work

Senior engineers don't all do the same job. Will Larson's Staff Engineer describes four common shapes, which he calls archetypes. The tech lead guides one team's approach and execution. The architect owns the direction and quality of a critical area across teams. The solver goes deep on the hardest problems wherever they arise, one after another. The right hand extends a senior leader's reach across an organisation. Each needs the depth from Part I and the leadership from Part III, in different mixtures. Knowing which shape suits you, and which your organisation needs, makes the path less mysterious; the work log's themes are often a good first hint.

Working on important problems

In 1986, Richard Hamming gave a talk at Bell Labs called "You and Your Research", about why some scientists do great work and most don't. Its most famous moment is a question he used to ask colleagues at lunch: what are the most important problems in your field, and why aren't you working on them? Most people, he observed, could name the important problems and had a reason not to be working on any of them. The question translates directly to engineering. What are the problems in your area that, if solved, would matter most to your company, your field or the people who use your systems? Are any of them yours? You don't need to work on the most important problem every day. But a career that never touches one compounds slowly, whatever its effort.

The body of work

The stage's deliverable, described in the appendix, is "a body of work rather than a project": a maintained open-source contribution, a written archive others cite, and at least one system in production that other engineers depend on and you didn't build alone. Each part compounds in its own way. The contribution builds depth and a public record of judgement, through chapter 5's ladder. The archive builds reputation and keeps teaching, through chapter 8's habit. The production system builds the most valuable credibility of all, having built something real with other people that kept working. None of the three can be finished quickly, and none needs to be. Start all three, small, and let them grow together for years.

A pace you can sustain

Compounding needs time, and time needs you to still be doing the work. Burnout isn't dedication taken to its limit; it's the end of the compounding, often for years. Protect sleep, holidays and a life outside work, deliberately, as a professional obligation to your future self. Careers have seasons: some years are for intense learning or a demanding project, some for family or health, and a long career has room for both. The engineers who reach real mastery are rarely the ones who worked hardest for two years. They're the ones who kept going, steadily, in one direction, for twenty.

Common traps
  • Relying on memory to describe a year of your own work.
  • Recording only numbers, and losing the outcomes in other people.
  • Scattering effort across unrelated areas, so nothing compounds.
  • Never asking Hamming's question, and never touching an important problem.
  • A pace you can't sustain, which ends the compounding entirely.

Write down the three most important problems in your area, at your company or in the field, and for each, why you aren't working on it.

The deliverable

The stage asks for "a body of work rather than a project": a maintained open-source contribution, a written archive others cite, and at least one system in production that other engineers depend on and that you didn't build alone. None of the three can be finished in a month, and they aren't meant to be. They're what years of the practices in this volume leave behind, and the lists below describe each when it's done. Start all three now, small, and review them every six months against these lists.

1. A maintained open-source contribution

Not a single patch, but a sustained part of a project that other people use. Chapter 5 is the model.

  1. One project you depend on, where you've contributed over at least a year: fixes, features, documentation and, above all, reviews of other people's work.
  2. A part of it you know well enough that maintainers ask for your review there, or give you the permission to approve changes.
  3. Issues triaged, questions answered and newcomers helped, visibly and kindly, in the project's own channels.
  4. Your contributions in your own words: a short page listing what you did, linked from your archive or CV, with the pull requests and reviews that show it.

2. A written archive others cite

Years of steady writing in your area, until other people link to it when they explain the subject. Chapter 8 is the model.

  1. A site you own, with at least two dozen posts in your area, published on a regular rhythm.
  2. A mix of hard lessons, explanations, reading notes and small tools, each with a specific title and its point in the first sentence.
  3. At least one talk, at a meetup, a conference or your company's engineering forum, made from posts that were well received.
  4. Evidence that it's useful to others: links from other people's writing, questions from readers, a post that keeps being found by search.

3. A system others depend on

Something in production, built with other people, that other engineers rely on and that kept working. Parts III and IV are the model.

  1. A design document that survived review, with alternatives, risks and a rollout, and the decision records that followed it.
  2. A project led end to end: scoped, sequenced risk first, forecast honestly, communicated to its stakeholders, and landed, with the old path removed.
  3. Engineers outside your team who depend on it, and documentation, runbooks and alerts good enough that they rarely need you.
  4. Its delivery and reliability measured, and at least one incident learned from in a blameless postmortem whose actions were done.
  5. Other people who could run it without you, because you taught them, which is the last test of having built something that lasts.

Keep the work log from chapter 16 throughout. In five years it's the record of how the body of work was built, and the evidence, for yourself as much as anyone, that it was.

The sixteen rules

One per chapter, each linked back to it. Read them when choosing what to work on, and again when a project, a review or a conversation goes badly.

  1. Choose an area to go deep in, write down why, and check the judgement your choice depends on.
  2. Read papers in passes, with a question, and build the central idea small enough to run.
  3. Find the assumption each design depends on; that's where it fails.
  4. Map a codebase before reading it, trace what actually runs, then fix something.
  5. Send patches that cost little to accept: small, tested, explained, in the project's style.
  6. Propose in the first paragraph, put numbers on goals, and show the alternatives you rejected.
  7. Write uncertain inputs as ranges, and argue about the assumption that decides it.
  8. Lead with the point, replace adjectives with facts, and publish where you own the archive.
  9. Estimate in ranges along the real dependencies, and commit at P85.
  10. Sequence work so the questions that could change the plan are answered first.
  11. Bottom line first, colour from the forecast, and every update ends with an ask and a date.
  12. Diagnose before teaching, teach at the frontier, and sponsor as well as advise.
  13. Price work in the business's units, with each assumption beside its number.
  14. Measure speed and stability together, for teams, to learn rather than to judge.
  15. Limit work in progress, finish before starting, and renegotiate commitments early.
  16. Write down what you did every week, work on important problems, and keep a pace that lasts.
