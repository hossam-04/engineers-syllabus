The Engineer's Workbench

A workbench is where you make your own tools. Every language hands you a list, a dictionary and a sort, and you can call them for a whole career without knowing what they cost or why. This supplement builds them from nothing: the growable array, the hash table, the heap, the balanced tree and the sorts, then the probabilistic structures that trade a little accuracy for a great deal of memory. It ends where efficient answers stop existing at all.

The one question

A complexity class on its own isn't enough to choose a data structure. "Constant time" hash lookups can take a thousand steps, and "logarithmic" trees can take four thousand.

So for every operation, ask: what does it cost in the worst case, and what makes the worst case happen? Sometimes the answer is "rarely, and it averages out", as with an array that occasionally doubles. Sometimes it's "only when the input is sorted", which in practice is all the time. And sometimes it's "whenever an attacker chooses the keys". Every chapter here is that question asked of one structure.

Chapter 01

Dynamic arrays and ring buffers

9 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

An array is the fastest structure there is: its elements sit side by side in memory, so any one of them is a single address calculation away. Its one flaw is a fixed size. A dynamic array grows by allocating a bigger block and copying, and growing by a multiple rather than a fixed amount is what makes the copying average out to a constant per append. When a queue needs to work at both ends, a ring buffer gets the same guarantee without moving anything at all.

Why contiguous memory wins

An array stores its elements next to each other, so the address of element i is the start address plus i times the element size: constant-time access with no pointers to follow. Contiguity also suits the hardware from stage 04. Reading one element pulls its neighbours into the same cache line, so a loop over an array streams through memory at full speed, while a loop over a linked list jumps to a new, probably uncached, address at every step. Python's list is an array, of references rather than values, and so are Java's ArrayList, C++'s vector and Go's slices.

Growing without paying for it

A fixed-size block can't grow in place, because the memory after it may belong to something else. So when a dynamic array is full, it allocates a new, larger block, copies every element across, and frees the old one. A single append can therefore cost as much as the whole array. What saves it is how much larger the new block is.

With doubling, 50,000 appends caused 16 resizes and 65,535 copies, 1.31 copies per append. The copies form a geometric series, 1 + 2 + 4 + ... + 32,768, which always totals less than twice the final size. That's the amortised cost: some appends are expensive, but averaged over any sequence of appends, each one costs a constant. Growing by half is just as constant, at 2.46 copies per append, and leaves less memory unused. Growing by a fixed 1,000 slots is a different kind of thing. It resizes every 1,000 appends and copies everything each time, so the total is quadratic: 1.2 million copies here, 24.5 per append, and the per-append figure keeps rising as the array grows.

An accounting argument gives the same result without the series. Charge every append three units: one pays for writing the element, and two go into a savings account. When the array doubles from k to 2k, the k/2 elements appended since the last resize have saved k units, exactly enough to pay for copying all k elements. The account never goes negative, so three units per append always covers the work.

Real libraries pick different factors for different trade-offs. Java's ArrayList grows by half; GNU's C++ library doubles a vector, while Microsoft's grows it by half; Go doubles small slices and grows large ones more gently; CPython over-allocates a list by about an eighth plus a small constant. Smaller factors waste less memory, larger ones copy less, and every one of them is a multiple, never a fixed step.

Shrinking without thrashing

An array that never shrinks wastes memory after a burst of deletions, so dynamic arrays usually give space back. The obvious rule, halve the capacity when the array falls to half full, has a trap the second block of output shows. At the boundary, one append fills the array and forces a doubling, and the next pop empties it to half and forces a halving. Alternating the two copies the whole array every time: 2,000 resizes and over two million copies for 1,000 pairs of operations. Shrinking only when the array falls to a quarter full, and then only to half, leaves room to move in both directions. The same 2,000 operations then cost one resize. This gap between the grow threshold and the shrink threshold, called hysteresis, appears wherever a system resizes itself, from hash tables to autoscaling groups.

Queues: the ring buffer

A queue adds at one end and removes at the other, and a plain array is bad at removing from the front: list.pop(0) shifts every remaining element left by one. Under a steady load of 1,000 queued items, 5,000 removals moved nearly five million elements. A ring buffer fixes this by never moving anything. It keeps a head index and a size, writes new items at (head + size) mod capacity, and advances the head on removal, wrapping around to the start of the array when it reaches the end. Both operations are constant time, with no copying, and the memory is allocated once. That predictability is why ring buffers sit inside network cards, audio pipelines, logging systems and the Linux kernel's io_uring interface. When a queue must also grow, use a ring buffer that doubles (exercise 1.3), or Python's collections.deque, a linked list of fixed-size blocks that is constant time at both ends.

Common traps
  • Growing by a constant amount, which turns appends quadratic as the array grows.
  • Shrinking at half full, so operations at the boundary resize every time.
  • Using list.pop(0) as a queue, moving every element on every removal.
  • Treating amortised cost as worst-case cost: one append can still copy the whole array, which matters when latency is the requirement.
  • Keeping pointers or iterators into an array across a resize, since the elements now live somewhere else.
Exercises
Exercise 1.1Count the copies

A doubling array starts with capacity 1. Exactly how many element copies do 2k + 1 appends cause? Check your formula against the example's 50,000 appends.

Show solution

Resizes happen when the array holds 1, 2, 4, ..., 2k elements, and each copies that many, so the total is 1 + 2 + ... + 2k = 2k+1 − 1 copies. For 2k + 1 appends that's just under two copies per append, the worst ratio, because the last append has just paid for a full resize. Fifty thousand appends trigger resizes at 1, 2, ..., 32,768 elements, since 215 = 32,768 < 50,000 < 65,536, so the total is 216 − 1 = 65,535 copies, exactly what the example printed.

Exercise 1.2Why a fixed step is quadratic

Show that growing by a fixed c slots makes n appends cost about n²/(2c) copies, and check it against the example's +1,000 policy.

Show solution

Resizes happen at sizes c, 2c, 3c, ..., up to about n, and each copies the current size, so the total is c(1 + 2 + ... + n/c) ≈ c · (n/c)²/2 = n²/(2c). For n = 50,000 and c = 1,000 that's 2.5 × 109 / 2,000 = 1.25 million, against the example's 1,225,050: its resizes happen at 1, 1,001, 2,001, ..., 49,001 elements, which sum to exactly that. The cost per append, n/(2c), grows with n, which is the signature of a quadratic total. Doubling the array would make it a constant.

Exercise 1.3A ring buffer that grows

Extend the ring buffer so push on a full buffer doubles its capacity instead of failing. What must the copy do that an ordinary array resize doesn't?

Show solution

Allocate an array of twice the capacity and copy the elements in queue order, starting from the head and wrapping around: new[i] = old[(head + i) % cap] for i in range(size). Then set head = 0. A plain slice copy of the old array would keep the wrap-around in the middle of the new array, so the items after the old array's end would be followed by empty slots instead of the items from its start. Copying in queue order "unrolls" the ring. The cost is amortised constant per push, by the same argument as the dynamic array, and it's how ArrayDeque in Java and Rust's VecDeque work.

  • Can you explain why doubling makes appends amortised constant?
  • Can you prove it with either the geometric series or the accounting method?
  • Does your dynamic array shrink with hysteresis?
  • Do your queues use a ring buffer or a deque rather than pop(0)?
  • Do you know when amortised cost isn't good enough?

Chapter 02

Hash tables from scratch

10 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

A hash table turns a key into an array index, so finding anything takes about one step. That holds only while three things stay true: the hash spreads keys evenly, the table never gets too full, and nobody can choose keys that collide on purpose. Every hash table design is a way of handling the collisions that remain.

From key to slot

A hash function turns a key into a large number, and the number modulo the table size picks a slot. If every key landed in its own slot, a lookup would be one array access. They don't. Collisions arrive much sooner than intuition suggests, by the same arithmetic as the birthday paradox: with 365 slots, 23 keys are more likely than not to include a collision (exercise 2.1). So the real design question is what to do when two keys want the same slot, and there are two families of answers.

Separate chaining makes each slot a list, and a lookup scans the list for its key. It degrades gracefully: at an average of one key per slot, chains are short. Open addressing keeps every key in the array itself. On a collision, it probes other slots in a fixed sequence, the next one for linear probing, until it finds the key or an empty slot. Probing avoids the pointers and allocations of chains and reads consecutive memory, which modern hardware rewards, but it is far more sensitive to how full the table is.

Load factor is everything

The first block measures linear probing at four load factors, the fraction of slots in use, against the formulas Donald Knuth derived in 1963. They agree closely. A successful lookup stays cheap for a long time: 1.16 probes at a quarter full, 2.54 at three-quarters. Unsuccessful lookups, which must probe until they hit an empty slot, explode: 2.5 probes at half full, 8.6 at three-quarters, and 47.6 at 90%. The cause is clustering. Keys that collide fill neighbouring slots, so the run of full slots grows, and every key that hashes anywhere into the run extends it further. That's why tables resize long before they're full: Java's HashMap at 0.75, CPython's dict at two-thirds. Resizing allocates a table twice the size and reinserts every key, because each key's slot depends on the table size. As with chapter 1's array, doubling makes the cost amortised constant.

Deletion leaves tombstones

Deleting from an open-addressed table can't simply empty the slot. A key placed further along the probe sequence, because this slot was occupied when it arrived, would become unreachable, since a lookup stops at the first empty slot it meets. So deletion leaves a tombstone: a marker that lookups step over and inserts may reuse. The example's second line shows the cost. After deleting half the keys, the table holds as many live keys as a quarter-full one, but a miss still costs 2.53 probes, the half-full figure, because tombstones count as occupied. They are cleared only when the table is rebuilt. Alternative schemes avoid tombstones by shifting later entries back on deletion, or by Robin Hood hashing, which keeps probe lengths even.

Hash flooding

Every guarantee above assumes keys are spread evenly. The last two lines show what happens when an adversary chooses them. With a weak hash, the key modulo the table size, 2,000 keys that are all multiples of 1,024 land in one bucket, and every lookup scans a chain of 2,000: 1,000.5 comparisons where the mixing hash needs 2.0. This isn't theoretical. In December 2011, researchers showed that a single crafted HTTP request with thousands of colliding form field names could keep a web server's CPU busy for minutes, across PHP, Java, Python, Ruby and others, because those languages parsed request parameters into hash tables with predictable hashes. The fixes are why modern hash tables look the way they do. Python 3.4 adopted SipHash, a keyed hash with a random key per process (PEP 456), so attackers can't predict collisions. Java 8's HashMap turns any long chain into a balanced tree (chapter 4), capping the damage at logarithmic.

What the library versions add

Production hash tables combine these ideas with engineering for the hardware. CPython's dict keeps entries in a compact array in insertion order and a separate small index of slots, which is why dictionaries remember insertion order (guaranteed since Python 3.7). Google's Swiss tables and Rust's hashbrown store a byte of each hash in a metadata array and check sixteen slots at once with SIMD instructions. All of them still depend on the same three conditions: a good hash, a bounded load factor, and keys an attacker can't aim.

Common traps
  • Mutating a key after inserting it, so it now hashes to a different slot and can't be found.
  • Equality and hashing that disagree: equal objects must have equal hashes, or a dictionary holds duplicates.
  • Letting the load factor climb, where missed lookups degrade sharply.
  • Weak or predictable hashes on keys users control, the recipe for hash flooding.
  • Relying on iteration order in languages and versions that don't promise one.
Exercises
Exercise 2.1The first collision

With a 1,000-slot table and a perfectly uniform hash, roughly how many keys can you insert before a collision is more likely than not?

Show solution

The chance that n keys in m slots are all distinct is about e−n²/(2m). Setting that to one half gives n ≈ √(2m ln 2) ≈ 1.18√m. For m = 1,000 that's about 37 keys, under 4% of the table. For m = 365 it's about 23, the birthday paradox. The lesson for design: collisions are a certainty from almost the first insertion, so collision handling is the main path, not an edge case.

Exercise 2.2Deleting from each design

Write the deletion logic for a chained table and for a linear-probing table, and explain why emptying the slot breaks the probing table.

Show solution

Chained: find the key's bucket and remove its entry from the list. Nothing else is affected, because each key is only ever in its own bucket. Probing: find the slot holding the key and replace the entry with a tombstone, as the example's delete does. Emptying it instead breaks every key that was placed further along the same probe run. Suppose cat, owl and emu all hash to slot 2 and sit in slots 2, 3 and 5. Empty slot 3, and a lookup for emu starts at 2, finds cat, steps to the empty slot 3, and stops: emu is still in the table and can no longer be found. The alternative to tombstones is backward-shift deletion, which moves the later entries of the run back to fill the hole.

Exercise 2.3When equality and hashing disagree

A Python class defines __eq__ to compare a customer's email, and __hash__ to return id(self). Two objects with the same email go into a set. What happens, and how do you fix it?

Show solution

The two objects are equal but almost certainly have different hashes, so they land in different slots and the set keeps both: a duplicate the set exists to prevent. Lookups behave just as badly: Customer("ana@x.com") in seen hashes a new object to yet another slot and reports False even though an equal customer is in the set. The rule is that equal objects must have equal hashes, so hash exactly what __eq__ compares: def __hash__(self): return hash(self.email). The key must also never change while it's in a set or dictionary, since a changed email would change the hash. Python enforces half of this rule for you: defining __eq__ without __hash__ makes instances unhashable, so this bug needs a deliberately wrong __hash__.

Checklist
  • Can you explain chaining and open addressing, and when each suits the hardware?
  • Does your table resize well before it's full?
  • Does your probing table handle deletion with tombstones or backward shifts?
  • Are hash and equality consistent for every key type you store?
  • Are keys that users control hashed with a keyed, randomised hash?

Chapter 03

Heaps, priority queues and A*

8 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

A priority queue answers one question over and over: what is the most urgent item right now? A binary heap answers it in logarithmic time with nothing but an array and arithmetic on its indexes. It is the engine inside schedulers, event simulations, top-k queries and every shortest-path search, including A*, which adds a guess at the distance remaining and searches far less of the map.

A tree hiding in an array

A binary heap is a binary tree with one rule, the heap property: every parent is no larger than its children (for a min-heap). It doesn't keep the whole tree sorted, only each parent above its children, which is much cheaper to maintain and still puts the smallest item at the root. The second trick is that the tree is always complete, filled level by level from the left, so it fits in an array with no gaps and no pointers. The root is at index 0, and the children of index i are at 2i + 1 and 2i + 2.

Both operations restore the heap property along one path, so both cost the height of the tree, about log2 n. To push, append the item at the end and sift it up, swapping it with its parent while it's smaller. To pop the minimum, take the root, move the last item into its place, and sift it down, swapping it with its smaller child while either child is smaller.

Building a heap in linear time

The first block compares two ways to turn 100,000 items into a heap. Pushing them one at a time costs up to log n per item, and on descending input, where every new item is the smallest yet and climbs all the way to the root, it costs 1.47 million comparisons. On random input it's far cheaper, because most new items stop near the bottom. Bottom-up heapify works the other way: it sifts down every internal node, from the last one back to the root. It costs about 200,000 comparisons on either input, because it's guaranteed linear. Half the nodes are leaves and never move, a quarter can sift down at most one level, an eighth at most two, and the sum of those distances is less than n. When you have all the items up front, heapify them; Python's heapq.heapify does exactly this.

Popping everything from a heap returns the items in order, which is heapsort: O(n log n) in the worst case, in place, with no extra memory. It's rarely the library sort anyway. It isn't stable, and its jumps between parents and children defeat the cache, so in practice it serves as a safety net: introsort, the usual C++ std::sort, switches to heapsort when quicksort starts going badly (chapter 5).

Priority queues in the wild

Anything that repeatedly asks for the most urgent item is a priority queue. Operating system schedulers pick the next task, event simulations process the earliest event, timers fire the soonest deadline, and "the top 100 of ten million" keeps a heap of 100 (exercise 3.2). One operation the basic heap lacks is changing the priority of an item already inside it, which Dijkstra's algorithm wants. Production code either keeps an index from items to positions (an indexed heap), or does what the example does: push the item again with its new priority and skip the stale copy when it's popped. Python's heapq has no decrease-key at all, so that second approach is standard there.

A*: Dijkstra with a sense of direction

Dijkstra's algorithm, which Sorted Again's graphs module teaches, expands nodes in order of their distance from the start, g. It finds the shortest path, but it searches outwards in every direction equally, including away from the goal. A* orders the queue by g + h instead, where h is an estimate of the distance still to go. If the estimate never overestimates, it's called admissible, and A* is guaranteed to find a shortest path while expanding far fewer nodes. Dijkstra is simply A* with h = 0.

The second block shows how much depends on the estimate. On open ground, the Manhattan distance is exactly right, and A* expands only the 119 cells of the path itself, where Dijkstra expands all 3,600. In a maze with 30% walls, the estimate is optimistic, since walls force detours it can't see, so A* saves less: 2,005 cells against 2,465. Both always find the same length. Two details make it work: the heap holds (g + h, -g, cell), so among equally promising cells the deeper one goes first, and a popped entry that's worse than a distance already found is skipped as stale. Route planners for real road networks go further: stronger estimates built from precomputed distances to landmarks, and preprocessing schemes such as contraction hierarchies that answer continental queries thousands of times faster than plain Dijkstra.

Common traps
  • Building a heap by repeated pushes when bottom-up heapify does it in linear time.
  • Forgetting stale entries when you re-push instead of decreasing a key.
  • An estimate that overestimates, which makes A* fast and wrong.
  • Expecting a heap to iterate in sorted order; only the root is guaranteed to be the minimum.
  • Forgetting that heapq is a min-heap, and getting the order backwards when you need the largest.
Exercises
Exercise 3.1Index arithmetic

In a heap stored from index 0, what are the parent and children of index 5? Why can a binary heap be stored without gaps when an ordinary binary search tree can't?

Show solution

The parent of 5 is (5 − 1) // 2 = 2, and its children are 2 · 5 + 1 = 11 and 12. A heap has no gaps because it's always a complete tree: every level is full except possibly the last, which fills from the left, and push and pop preserve that shape by only ever adding or removing the last array element. A binary search tree's shape depends on the keys, so it has holes wherever a node has no left or right child. Storing it this way would waste space exponentially on a lopsided tree, which is why BSTs use pointers.

Exercise 3.2Top 100 of ten million

Find the 100 largest values in a stream of 10 million, using as little memory as possible. Which heap, what size, and what does it cost?

Show solution

Keep a min-heap of size 100. Push the first 100 values. For each later value, if it's larger than the heap's minimum (its root), replace the root with it and sift down. The heap always holds the 100 largest values seen so far, and its root is the smallest of them, the bar a newcomer must clear. The cost is O(n log k): 10 million items × about 7 levels at worst, and far less on typical data, since most values fail the comparison with the root and cost one comparison. The memory is 100 items. Sorting everything would need all 10 million in memory and O(n log n) time. Python's heapq.nlargest does exactly this.

Exercise 3.3A faster, wrong A*

A game multiplies its A* estimate by 1.5 to make pathfinding faster. What does it gain, what does it lose, and when is that a reasonable trade?

Show solution

It gains speed: an inflated estimate pulls the search harder towards the goal, so it expands fewer cells, especially in open terrain. It loses the guarantee of a shortest path, because the estimate can now overestimate and A* may commit to a route before it has ruled out a shorter one. This is called weighted A*, and it does keep a bound: with a weight of 1.5, the path found is never more than 1.5 times the shortest. For game characters, where a path that looks reasonable and is found quickly beats a perfect one found late, that's a common, sensible trade. For routing a delivery van, where every extra kilometre costs money, it isn't.

  • Do you handle priority changes with an indexed heap or by skipping stale entries?
  • Can you say what makes an A* estimate admissible, and what happens when it isn't?
  • Do you reach for a size-k heap for top-k problems?

Chapter 04

Balanced search trees

7 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

A binary search tree keeps keys in order and finds any of them in time proportional to its height. Insert keys in sorted order and the height becomes the number of keys: the tree has turned into a linked list. Balanced trees prevent this by restructuring themselves with small local rotations as they go, guaranteeing logarithmic height whatever order the keys arrive in.

What a search tree is for

Sorted Again's trees module covers binary search trees: every key in a node's left subtree is smaller than it, every key in the right subtree is larger, an in-order traversal visits the keys in sorted order, and a search follows a single path from the root. Hash tables are faster for exact lookups, so the reason to use a tree is everything a hash table can't do: iterate in order, find every key in a range, find the next key above or below a value, or the k-th smallest. The catch is that every operation costs the height of the tree, and nothing in a plain BST controls the height.

How a tree degenerates

Inserting 4,095 keys in sorted order into a plain BST gives a tree of height 4,095. Each new key is larger than everything before it, so it becomes the right child of the previous one, and the "tree" is a linked list with extra pointers. A search for the largest key walks all 4,095 nodes. Sorted input isn't an unlucky accident: auto-incrementing ids, timestamps, alphabetical imports and already-sorted files are some of the most common inputs there are. Random keys do much better, a height of 24 here, but a structure whose speed depends on the order the data happens to arrive in can't be trusted.

Rotations

A rotation is a local restructuring that changes which of two nodes is the parent while keeping the in-order sequence, and so the search-tree property, intact. It touches three pointers and runs in constant time.

AVL trees: never more than one apart

The AVL tree, the first self-balancing tree (Adelson-Velsky and Landis, 1962), keeps one invariant: at every node, the heights of the two subtrees differ by at most one. After an insertion, the code walks back up the path, updates heights, and wherever a node's balance reaches two, fixes it with a rotation. There are four cases. In the left-left and right-right cases, a single rotation fixes it. In the left-right and right-left cases, the inner child is rotated first to turn it into one of the simple cases. The invariant bounds the height at about 1.44 log2 n, so every search, insert and delete is logarithmic in the worst case.

The output shows the payoff. On the same 4,095 sorted keys that flattened the plain tree, the AVL tree has height 12, exactly what a perfect tree of 4,095 nodes would have, after 4,083 rotations: sorted input triggers one at almost every insertion, and the rotations pack the keys perfectly. On random keys it's 15, well within its bound, and an in-order traversal returns the keys sorted in both cases, confirming the rotations never broke the search-tree property.

The other balanced trees

Red-black trees keep a looser invariant, colouring nodes red or black and allowing a height of up to 2 log2(n + 1). In return, insertions and deletions need fewer rotations, which makes them the usual choice for general-purpose ordered maps: Java's TreeMap, most implementations of C++'s std::map, and many structures inside the Linux kernel. B-trees balance in a different way, with hundreds of keys per node to suit disk pages, which is why databases use them (The Engineer's Ledger, chapter 6). Skip lists get logarithmic expected time from random coin flips instead of rotations; Redis uses them for sorted sets, and LevelDB and RocksDB use them for their memtables. Python's standard library has no balanced tree at all. Where you need one, the popular sortedcontainers package uses a list of sorted lists, which performs very well in practice.

Common traps
  • Assuming a binary search tree is balanced. A plain one is only as good as the order of its input.
  • Feeding sorted keys to an unbalanced tree: ids, timestamps and sorted files make that the common case.
  • Recursive algorithms on a degenerate tree, which exhaust the call stack long before they finish.
  • Using a tree where a hash table suffices, paying a logarithm for ordering you never use.
  • Writing your own balanced tree for production when tested library implementations exist; build one to learn, not to ship.

Show solution

Inserting 7 makes 5 right-heavy: rotate left at 5, giving 6 over 5 and 7. The final tree is perfect: 4 at the root, then 2 and 6, then 1, 3, 5 and 7. Part II

Sorting

Two chapters on the most studied problem in computing. First the comparison sorts, which no algorithm can push below a hard mathematical floor, and how they differ in everything else; then the sorts that never compare at all, and run in linear time by knowing something about their keys.

Chapter 05

Comparison sorts, and when each wins

9 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

There is no best sorting algorithm, only the best one for a given input and requirement. No sort that works by comparing items can beat about n log n comparisons, which is a mathematical floor, not a lack of cleverness. Above that floor, sorts differ in how they handle sorted, reversed and repetitive input, whether they keep equal items in their original order, and how much memory they need. Production libraries combine several to get the best of each.

The floor under every comparison sort

A comparison sort learns about the order only by asking "is this item smaller than that one?" Picture every possible run of the algorithm as a tree of those questions, with a yes branch and a no branch at each one. Each leaf is a final ordering, and since any of the n! orderings of n distinct items might be the right one, the tree needs at least n! leaves. A binary tree with n! leaves has height at least log2(n!), so some input must cost that many comparisons. By Stirling's approximation, log2(n!) is about n log2 n − 1.44n, which for 2,000 items is 19,053.

Seven sorts, four inputs

The example implements the classic algorithms and counts the comparisons each makes on 2,000 items in four arrangements: random, already sorted, reversed, and drawn from only ten distinct values.

Insertion sort slides each item left into place. On random input it's quadratic, a million comparisons, and on reversed input two million, but on sorted input it makes one comparison per item and stops: 1,999. That makes it the fastest sort for tiny or nearly sorted arrays, which is why every hybrid sort uses it for small pieces.

Merge sort splits the list in half, sorts each half, and merges them. It's close to the floor on random input, 19,422 against 19,053, never worse than n log n, and stable, but it needs a second array as large as the input. Its merging of sorted runs is also the basis of external sorting, where data is too large for memory.

Quicksort partitions around a pivot and recurses on each side, in place. Its weakness is the pivot. Always taking the first item is quadratic on sorted and reversed input, 1,999,000 comparisons, exactly n(n−1)/2, because every partition peels off one item. A random pivot makes that vanishingly unlikely, at around 23,000 to 27,000 comparisons on any order. But the same row shows a second weakness: with only ten distinct values, a two-way partition puts all the equal items on one side, and it climbs back to 208,800. Three-way partitioning groups the items equal to the pivot in the middle and never touches them again, which brings the duplicates case down to 13,057. It pays for that with two comparisons per item on distinct keys, about 37,500.

Heapsort (chapter 3) is the steadiest: about twice the floor on every input, in place, with an O(n log n) worst case. It's slow in practice because its jumps through the array defeat the cache, and it isn't stable.

Timsort, which Tim Peters designed for Python in 2002, is a merge sort that first looks for runs of data already in order, ascending or descending, then merges them carefully. On random input it lands near the floor, 19,303. On sorted and reversed input it finds one long run and finishes in n−1 comparisons, and it does well with duplicates too. It's stable, and it's the sort behind Python's sorted(), Java's sort for objects since Java 7, and JavaScript's Array.prototype.sort in V8.

Stability

A sort is stable if items that compare equal keep their original relative order. The last line tests it with records sorted by a key that has only five values: merge sort and Python's sorted() preserve the original order within each key, and heapsort scrambles it. Stability matters whenever you sort records by one field and care about the order from a previous sort: a table sorted by date, then by customer, shows each customer's orders still in date order only if the second sort is stable. It also makes multi-key sorting simple. Sort by the least important key first, then by the next, each with a stable sort (exercise 5.3), which is exactly how the radix sort in chapter 6 works.

What libraries actually do

No single algorithm wins every column, so standard libraries combine them. Introsort, the usual C++ std::sort, runs quicksort, switches to heapsort if the recursion gets suspiciously deep, and finishes small pieces with insertion sort: quicksort's speed, heapsort's worst case, insertion sort's small-array efficiency. Pattern-defeating quicksort, used by Go's sort package since version 1.19 and long used for Rust's unstable sort, adds detection of sorted and repetitive input. Stable library sorts are typically Timsort or a relative of it. The practical rule follows. Use the library sort unless you have measured a reason not to, know whether it's stable, and reach for a specialised algorithm only for a specific need: a partial sort for the top k, quickselect for a median, an external merge sort for data larger than memory.

Common traps
  • Quicksort with a fixed pivot on data that's often already sorted.
  • Assuming a sort is stable: C++'s std::sort isn't; std::stable_sort is.
  • Comparators that aren't consistent, such as comparisons involving NaN, or rules that aren't transitive, which produce wrong orders or errors.
  • Sorting everything to find the top few, when a heap or selection algorithm does it in linear time.
  • Two-way partitioning on data with many duplicates, which drifts back towards quadratic.

Exercise 5.1Where n log n comes from

Show that log2(n!) grows like n log2 n without using Stirling's formula, by bounding n! from above and below.

Show solution

Upper bound: n! = 1 × 2 × ... × n ≤ nn, so log2(n!) ≤ n log2 n. Lower bound: the largest n/2 factors of n! are each at least n/2, so n! ≥ (n/2)n/2, and log2(n!) ≥ (n/2) log2(n/2), which is also proportional to n log n. Squeezed between two multiples of n log n, log2(n!) is Θ(n log n). So the decision-tree argument shows every comparison sort needs Ω(n log n) comparisons in the worst case, and merge sort, heapsort and Timsort meet that bound up to a constant.

Exercise 5.2A comparator that lies

A list of floats contains a NaN. What does sorted() do with it, and why can't any comparison sort do much better?

Show solution

Every comparison with NaN is false: nan < 3 and 3 < nan are both false, so to the sort NaN looks "equal" to everything while the numbers around it aren't equal to each other. The ordering is no longer consistent, and the sort's correctness argument, which assumes a consistent ordering, no longer holds. Python returns a list that may be only partly sorted, silently, with the NaN wherever the merges happened to leave it. Java detects some such inconsistencies and throws "Comparison method violates its general contract!". The fix is in the data, not the algorithm: remove NaNs first, or sort with a key that places them explicitly, such as key=lambda x: (math.isnan(x), x).

Exercise 5.3Two keys with stable sorts

Sort employees by department, and within each department by salary from highest to lowest, using only stable single-key sorts.

The second sort groups by department, and because it's stable, each department's employees keep the salary order the first sort gave them. Python guarantees that reverse=True keeps the sort stable, so equal salaries keep their original order too. A single sort with a tuple key, key=lambda e: (e.department, -e.salary), does the same in one pass when the secondary key can be negated. The two-pass version works for any keys, which is the general technique.

Chapter 06

Sorting without comparing

8 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

The n log n floor applies only to sorts that learn the order by comparing pairs of items. When keys are small integers, or can be cut into digits, you can put each item straight into its place and sort in linear time. The price is assumptions about the keys, extra memory, and a reliance on stability that is easy to break and hard to notice.

Counting sort

If every key is an integer between 0 and k−1, you don't need to compare anything. Count how many items have each key, turn the counts into starting positions with a running total, and place each item at its key's next free position. That's two passes over the items and one over the counts: n + k steps, linear when k isn't much larger than n. Placing items in their original order makes it stable, a property the next section depends on entirely.

Sorting 100,000 ages between 0 and 119 takes 200,120 steps, with no comparisons at all. The next line shows the limit. The count array has one slot per possible key, so sorting 64-bit ids directly would need 264 counters, 128 exbibytes of memory. Counting sort is for small key ranges: ages, grades, status codes, bytes.

Radix sort

Radix sort extends counting sort to large keys by sorting them one digit at a time, using a stable counting sort for each digit. The least significant digit (LSD) version starts from the ones digit and works up. It looks backwards, but it works, and the diagram shows why.

Take 329 and 355. They share a hundreds digit, so the hundreds pass can't tell them apart: a stable pass leaves them in the order they arrived in. They arrived in the right order because the tens pass sorted them by their tens digits, 2 before 5. So the final order of two numbers that tie on a digit is decided by the earlier passes, and it survives only if every later pass is stable. That's the whole correctness argument for LSD radix sort.

The example sorts 100,000 random 32-bit integers in four passes of one byte each, 801,024 steps against merge sort's 1,536,586 comparisons, and the gap widens with n, since radix sort is linear and merge sort isn't. The last line is the warning. Swap in a digit pass that groups by digit correctly but reverses the order of equal digits, and the output is wrong from position 1 onwards, with no error raised. The bug is silent: every pass looks correct on its own.

Bucket sort

Bucket sort assumes the keys are spread evenly over a known range. It splits the range into n buckets, drops each item into its bucket in one pass, sorts each tiny bucket, and concatenates them. With 100,000 uniformly random floats and 100,000 buckets, the fullest bucket held 9 items, so the whole sort is linear in expectation. The assumption carries all the weight. If the data clusters, say most values near zero, most items land in a few buckets, and the sort degrades to whatever sorts those buckets.

When the floor doesn't apply

None of this contradicts chapter 5. The n log n bound counts comparisons, and these sorts don't compare: they use the structure of the keys, which a comparison sort isn't allowed to see. That's also why libraries rarely use them by default. A general-purpose sort can't know that your keys are small integers or uniformly spread. Where those facts are known, they win clearly: GPU libraries sort with radix sort, databases sort fixed-width keys with it, suffix-array construction depends on it, and counting sort is the inner loop of many of them.

Common traps
  • Counting sort on a large key range, trading time for an impossible amount of memory.
  • An unstable digit pass inside a radix sort, which produces wrong output without any error.
  • Signed integers in a radix sort, where negative numbers sort after positive ones unless the sign bit is handled.
  • Bucket sort on skewed data, which piles everything into a few buckets.
  • LSD radix on variable-length strings without padding, or without switching to most-significant-digit order.

Exercise 6.2Why stable passes

Using the diagram's numbers, show what goes wrong if the hundreds pass is unstable. Then explain why, for these particular seven numbers, an unstable tens pass would have done no harm.

After the tens pass, 329 comes before 355 (tens digits 2 and 5) and 436 before 457 (3 and 5). In the hundreds pass each pair ties, on 3 and on 4. The rule is about ties: a pass must keep, among items that tie on its digit, the order the earlier passes established. With other data, such as 425 and 427 tying on both tens and hundreds, an unstable tens pass would break the result just the same.

Exercise 6.3Negative numbers

The example sorts unsigned 32-bit integers. What goes wrong with two's-complement signed integers, and what's the smallest fix?

Show solution

In two's complement, negative numbers have their top bit set, so as unsigned values they're larger than every positive number, and a plain LSD radix sort places them after all the positives (internally in the right order, since −1 is the largest of them). The fix is to flip the sign bit before sorting, or equivalently to treat the most significant byte's digit as byte ^ 0x80 in the last pass: negatives then map to 0–127 and positives to 128–255, and the order comes out right. Floating-point keys take a similar trick, flipping all the bits of negative numbers and only the sign bit of positive ones, which is described in Michael Herf's "Radix Tricks".

Part III

Beyond exact & efficient

Two chapters on what to do when the usual promises break. First the structures that give up exact answers to answer questions about billions of items in a few kilobytes; then the problems for which no efficient exact algorithm is known at all, how to recognise them, and what to build instead.

Chapter 07

Probabilistic structures: trading certainty for memory

12 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

Exact answers about a huge stream cost memory in proportion to everything you've seen. A Bloom filter, a count-min sketch and a HyperLogLog answer three common questions, "have I seen this?", "how often?" and "how many distinct?", in a small, fixed amount of memory, by accepting an error you choose in advance. Each error has a known shape: a Bloom filter only ever says yes wrongly, a count-min sketch only ever overcounts, and a HyperLogLog is off by a predictable percentage. All three can be merged across machines, which is why they're everywhere in databases, networks and analytics.

The price of exact

Answering "have I seen this user before?" exactly means storing every user. Answering "how many distinct visitors today?" exactly means remembering every visitor until midnight. At a few hundred million items that's gigabytes per question, per machine. Hashing offers a way out. A good hash function makes any item look like a random number, and random numbers have statistics you can rely on. Each structure in this chapter stores only those statistics, in an amount of memory set by the error you'll accept rather than by how much data arrives.

Bloom filters: membership with one-sided error

A Bloom filter, invented by Burton Bloom in 1970, is an array of m bits, all zero, and k hash functions. To add an item, compute its k positions and set those bits to 1. To query an item, check the same k positions. If any of them is 0, the item was definitely never added, because adding it would have set that bit. If all of them are 1, the item was probably added, or other items happened to set all k bits between them, which is a false positive. There are never false negatives.

After n insertions, the chance that a given bit is still 0 is about e−kn/m, so the false-positive rate is about (1 − e−kn/m)k. With too few hash functions each query checks too little; with too many the array fills with ones. The best choice is k = (m/n) ln 2, which leaves half the bits set. At that k, the bits you need depend only on the error rate, not on the size of the items: about 1.44 log2(1/p) bits per key, which is 9.6 bits for 1% and 14.4 bits for 0.1%. Each tenfold improvement costs another 4.8 bits per key. Implementations don't compute k independent hashes. Adam Kirsch and Michael Mitzenmacher showed that two hashes combined as h1 + i·h2 work as well, which is what the example does with one blake2b digest split in half.

The classic use is guarding an expensive lookup that usually fails. LSM-tree databases such as RocksDB and Cassandra keep a Bloom filter for each file on disk, so a read skips every file that can't contain the key. Akamai's CDN used one to cache an object only on its second request, because most objects are requested exactly once, as Bruce Maggs and Ramesh Sitaraman describe in "Algorithmic nuggets in content delivery". In both cases a false positive costs one wasted lookup, and a false negative, which never happens, would have cost a wrong answer.

Count-min sketch: frequencies that only overcount

A count-min sketch, from Graham Cormode and S. Muthukrishnan in 2005, is d rows of w counters, each row with its own hash function. Adding an item increments one counter in each row. The estimate for an item is the smallest of its d counters. Every counter the item touches holds its true count plus whatever else hashed to the same place, so each one is an overestimate, and the minimum is the least wrong. With w = ⌈e/ε⌉ and d = ⌈ln(1/δ)⌉, the estimate exceeds the true count by at most εN, where N is the total number of events, with probability at least 1 − δ.

That bound is relative to the whole stream, not to the item's own count. An error of εN is negligible for an item seen thousands of times and enormous for one seen twice. So the count-min sketch is a tool for heavy hitters: the most frequent source addresses on a network link, trending search terms, the noisiest API keys for rate limiting. Combined with a small heap of the current leaders (chapter 3), it answers "what are the top 100?" over a stream it never stores.

HyperLogLog: counting distinct items in kilobytes

Start with coins. If someone tells you the longest run of heads they saw before a tail was 10, they probably flipped about 210 times, since a run that long is a one-in-a-thousand event. Hash every item to a random-looking 64-bit number and look at its leading zero bits: a hash starting with r − 1 zeros and then a one turns up with probability 2−r, so the largest such r seen estimates log2 of the number of distinct items. Duplicates hash identically and can't raise the maximum, which is exactly why the estimate counts distinct items.

A single maximum is noisy, since one lucky hash could claim a billion items. HyperLogLog, from Philippe Flajolet and colleagues in 2007, splits the hashes among m registers using their first p bits, keeps the largest rank seen in each register, and combines the registers with a harmonic mean, which damps the occasional huge value. Its standard error is 1.04/√m: 3.25% with 1,024 registers, 0.81% with 16,384. A rank never exceeds 64, so a register needs only 6 bits, and 16,384 registers fit in 12 KB, which is what Redis's PFCOUNT uses. The example spends a whole byte per register for clarity. For small counts, when many registers are still empty, it switches to linear counting, an estimate from the number of empty registers that's more accurate in that range.

All three, measured

The example builds all three from the definitions above and measures them against exact answers: a Bloom filter at two sizes, a count-min sketch over a skewed stream in which a few items are very common, and HyperLogLog at two register counts.

The Bloom filter matches the formula closely. At 8 bits per key with k = 6 it measured 2.14% false positives against 2.16% predicted; at 12 bits per key with k = 8, 0.28% against 0.31%. Every one of the 10,000 members was found. The whole filter is 10,000 bytes, one byte per key, for keys that are 6 to 9 characters long before any hash table overhead.

The count-min sketch shows the shape of its error. The most common item, seen 20,345 times, was estimated at 20,350, off by 0.02%. Item 500, seen 38 times, was estimated at 46, off by 21%. The worst overcount among all 9,653 items was 96, inside the bound of 200, and nothing was undercounted. It does this in 13,595 counters, whatever the length of the stream.

HyperLogLog estimated 289,162 distinct values to within 1.98% using 1,024 registers and 0.05% using 16,384. The second run was luckier than its typical 0.81%, because the standard error describes a spread, not a limit. An exact count would have to store all 289,162 values, at least 2.3 MB at 8 bytes each before any table overhead, and that grows with the data. The sketch stays at 16 KB, or 12 KB packed, at a billion distinct values.

Merging matters as much as memory. Two sketches with the same size and hash functions, built on different machines or on different days, combine into exactly the sketch of the combined data. Common traps
  • Treating "probably present" as "present". Use a Bloom filter to skip work, then confirm with the real lookup when the answer matters.
  • Deleting by clearing bits, which creates false negatives for every other item that shared them.
  • Overfilling a filter: past its design size, the false-positive rate climbs steeply.
  • Merging sketches built with different sizes or hash seeds, which silently produces nonsense.
  • Reading a count-min estimate for a rare item as accurate. The bound is relative to the whole stream.
  • Using unkeyed hashes on adversarial input. Someone who knows the hash can craft items that set chosen bits or inflate chosen counters, the attack from chapter 2; use a keyed hash with a secret seed.

Exercise 7.2Taking things out

Why can't you delete an item from a Bloom filter by clearing its k bits? What can you use instead if you need deletions?

Show solution

Bits are shared. In the figure, clearing cat's bits would clear bit 7, which dog also set, and the filter would then report dog as absent: a false negative, which breaks the one guarantee a Bloom filter makes. There are three common alternatives. A counting Bloom filter replaces each bit with a small counter, typically 4 bits, incremented on add and decremented on delete, at four times the memory. A cuckoo filter stores short fingerprints in a cuckoo hash table and supports deletion, often in less space than a Bloom filter at low false-positive rates, but deleting an item that was never added can remove another item's fingerprint. Or you can avoid deletion entirely by rotating filters: keep one per hour, query the recent ones, and drop whole filters as they expire.

Exercise 7.3Unique visitors this week

You keep one HyperLogLog sketch of visitor IDs per day. How do you get the number of distinct visitors over the last seven days, and why can't you add up the seven daily counts? Can you also estimate how many visitors came on both Monday and Tuesday?

Show solution

Take the maximum of each register across the seven sketches. The result is exactly the sketch you would have built from the whole week's stream, so its estimate is the week's distinct count, with the usual error. Adding the daily counts counts a visitor who came on five days five times. For the overlap, inclusion–exclusion gives |Mon ∩ Tue| = |Mon| + |Tue| − |Mon ∪ Tue|, and every term can be estimated. But each term's error is a percentage of a large number, so the error of the difference is a percentage of the union, not of the overlap. A small overlap between two big sets can come out wildly wrong or even negative. For accurate intersections, sketches designed for them, such as MinHash or theta sketches, do much better.

Chapter 08

NP-completeness: when no fast algorithm is known

11 min read3 exercises4 flashcards

Some problems have no known algorithm much better than trying every possibility, and thousands of them are linked so tightly that a fast algorithm for any one would give a fast algorithm for all. Fifty years of searching hasn't found one. Recognising such a problem is a practical skill. It stops you looking for an algorithm that probably doesn't exist, and points you at what works instead: small inputs, special structure, approximation with a guarantee, measured heuristics, or a solver.

Easy to check, hard to find

Take subset sum: given a list of numbers and a target, is there a subset that adds up to exactly the target? If someone hands you a subset, checking it takes a few additions. Finding one is another matter. The obvious method tries all 2n subsets, and nobody knows a method that is polynomial in the size of the input for every instance.

That asymmetry defines the class. P is the set of problems that can be solved in polynomial time. NP is the set of yes-or-no problems whose yes-answers come with a certificate, like the subset, that can be checked in polynomial time. The name means "nondeterministic polynomial": a machine that could guess the right certificate would finish in polynomial time. Every problem in P is also in NP, since you can check an answer by solving the problem yourself. Whether P equals NP is the most famous open question in computer science. Stephen Cook posed it in 1971, it's one of the Clay Millennium Prize problems, and most researchers believe the answer is no.

Reductions: why one problem stands for thousands

A reduction from problem A to problem B is a polynomial-time translation of any instance of A into an instance of B with the same answer. Given one, a fast algorithm for B would give a fast algorithm for A, so B is at least as hard as A. Cook, and independently Leonid Levin, proved that Boolean satisfiability (SAT) is as hard as everything in NP: any NP problem can be translated into a formula that's satisfiable exactly when the answer is yes. In 1972 Richard Karp reduced SAT to 21 classic problems, among them vertex cover, clique, Hamiltonian cycle, graph colouring and a version of subset sum. Garey and Johnson's 1979 book catalogued hundreds more, and there are now thousands. A problem is NP-hard if everything in NP reduces to it, and NP-complete if it's also in NP itself.

Direction is the classic mistake. To show that your new problem X is hard, reduce a known NP-complete problem to X: "if I could solve X quickly, I could solve vertex cover quickly." Reducing X to SAT shows only that X is no harder than SAT. That's useful too, in a different way, because it means a SAT solver can solve X.

Feeling the wall

The example makes the wall concrete. It brute-forces subset sum on instances with no solution, which is the worst case because every subset has to be tried. It checks a certificate, solves a larger instance by dynamic programming, solves a 10-city travelling salesperson problem exactly and with two heuristics, and compares an approximation for vertex cover against the true optimum.

Each extra number doubles the brute-force work. Twenty numbers mean a million subsets, which is nothing. Forty mean a trillion, which compiled code can still grind through. Sixty mean a million times more again. Checking a claimed answer for 30 numbers took 12 additions, against a search space of more than a billion subsets. That gap between finding and checking is NP in one line of output.

What to do instead

Check the size first. Exponential doesn't automatically mean too slow. If your instances have 20 items, brute force over a million subsets is the simplest correct solution. With pruning (backtracking, branch and bound), brute force often reaches much further in practice than its worst case suggests.

Exploit small numbers. The dynamic program tracks which sums are reachable instead of which subsets exist. For 30 numbers below 1,000 it decided reachability of 6,575 in 102,145 steps. That's polynomial in the value of the target, not in its length in bits, which makes it pseudo-polynomial. With 40-bit numbers the table would need about 240 cells and the trick is gone. Problems like subset sum and knapsack are called weakly NP-complete for this reason: they're practical whenever the numbers involved are small.

Exploit structure. NP-hardness is a statement about the worst instances, and yours may avoid them. Vertex cover is easy on trees and on bipartite graphs, where by Kőnig's theorem it equals a maximum matching. 2-SAT is solvable in linear time though 3-SAT is NP-complete. Colouring an interval graph, such as assigning meetings to rooms, is solved by a greedy algorithm. Some problems are hard only in one parameter: a vertex cover of size k can be found in about 2k·n steps by branching on the two ends of an uncovered edge, which is fast for a huge graph with a small cover.

Approximate with a guarantee. The vertex-cover algorithm in the example takes both ends of any uncovered edge. It found 10 nodes where the optimum is 8, and it can never be worse than twice the optimum (exercise 8.3). For travelling salesperson with real distances, which obey the triangle inequality, Christofides' algorithm guarantees a tour within 1.5 times the optimum, an approximation ratio improved in 2020 by a famously tiny margin. Some problems resist even this: without the triangle inequality, no polynomial algorithm can approximate travelling salesperson within any constant factor unless P = NP.

Use heuristics, and measure them. Nearest neighbour built a tour 4% longer than the optimum; 2-opt, which reverses any segment that shortens the tour, brought it to 1%. Neither guarantees anything in general, but local search of this kind routinely gets within a few percent on real instances. Measure a heuristic against exact answers on small instances, or against a lower bound on large ones, before you trust it.

Hand it to a solver. SAT solvers and mixed-integer programming solvers routinely solve industrial instances with millions of variables, in hardware verification, scheduling and logistics. Their worst case is still exponential, but real instances have structure that decades of solver engineering exploit. Translating your problem into SAT or an integer program, which is itself a reduction, and running Google's OR-Tools, the open-source HiGHS or a commercial solver like Gurobi often beats a hand-written algorithm by a wide margin.

Hard problems in disguise

NP-hard problems rarely announce themselves. Choosing one version of each package so that every dependency constraint holds is NP-complete: Russ Cox showed how to encode 3-SAT in it. That's why Go modules chose minimal version selection, which sidesteps the general problem by design, while Dart's pub and Python's uv use PubGrub, a resolver built on SAT-solving techniques. Scheduling exams so no student has a clash is graph colouring, and so is register allocation in a compiler. Placing containers onto servers is bin packing. Choosing which features fit a release budget is knapsack. Planning a delivery route is travelling salesperson. When a problem asks you to choose a subset, an assignment or an ordering that satisfies constraints or optimises a total, suspect NP-hardness before you spend a week looking for a clever polynomial algorithm.

Common traps
  • Reading NP as "not polynomial". It means nondeterministic polynomial, and every problem in P is in NP.
  • Reducing in the wrong direction: to prove X hard, reduce a known hard problem to X, not X to it.
  • Calling a pseudo-polynomial algorithm polynomial because its running time is a product of two input numbers.
  • Giving up because a problem is NP-hard, when your instances are small, structured, or well within a solver's reach.
  • Presenting a heuristic's answer as optimal, or reporting it without a bound or a comparison.
  • Assuming a problem is hard because it looks hard. Shortest paths, minimum spanning trees, bipartite matching, 2-SAT and linear programming are all polynomial.
Exercises
Exercise 8.1Exams without clashes

Your university wants to know whether all its exams fit into k time slots so that no student has two exams at once. Show that this problem is NP-hard, using the fact that deciding whether a graph can be coloured with k colours is NP-complete. Which way does the reduction go?

Show solution

Reduce graph colouring, the known hard problem, to exam scheduling, the new one. Given any graph, make one exam per node and, for each edge, one student who takes the two exams at its ends. A clash-free timetable with k slots assigns each exam a slot so that the two ends of every edge get different slots, which is exactly a k-colouring, and every k-colouring gives such a timetable. The translation takes time proportional to the size of the graph. So a fast exam scheduler would colour arbitrary graphs fast, and exam scheduling is at least as hard as colouring. Going the other way, turning timetables into graphs, would prove only that scheduling is no harder than colouring. That's true, and useful for using a colouring heuristic, but it proves nothing about hardness.

Exercise 8.2A polynomial algorithm for an NP-complete problem?

The subset-sum dynamic program runs in O(n·T) time, where T is the target. Subset sum is NP-complete. Why isn't this a proof that P = NP?

Show solution

"Polynomial" means polynomial in the size of the input, measured in bits. A target T takes only about log2 T bits to write down, so with b-bit numbers T can be as large as 2b, and O(n·T) is O(n·2b): exponential in the input length. Adding one bit to every number doubles the running time while barely growing the input. The algorithm is pseudo-polynomial, fast only when the numbers are small. Some problems stay NP-hard even when all their numbers are small, such as bin packing and 3-partition; these are called strongly NP-hard, and no pseudo-polynomial algorithm for them exists unless P = NP.

Exercise 8.3Why the approximation is never more than twice optimal

The vertex-cover approximation walks the edges and, whenever an edge has neither end in the cover, adds both ends. Prove that the result is a vertex cover, and that it's at most twice the size of the smallest one.

Show solution

It's a cover because every edge is examined, and after an edge is examined at least one of its ends is in the set: either it already was, or the algorithm just added both. For the bound, look at the edges that triggered additions. No two of them share an end, because once an edge's ends are added, any later edge touching either of them is already covered. They form a matching M, and the algorithm's cover has exactly 2|M| nodes. Any vertex cover, including the optimal one, must contain at least one end of each edge in M, and since those edges share no ends, those are |M| different nodes. So the optimum is at least |M|, and the algorithm's cover is at most twice the optimum. In the example, the algorithm matched 5 edges and took 10 nodes, so the optimum had to be at least 5; it was 8.

The eight rules

One per chapter, each linked back to it. Read them before you choose a data structure for anything that will grow, and again when something that was fast in testing is slow in production.

  1. Grow by a factor, never by a constant, and shrink later than you grow; that is what makes appending amortised constant time.
  2. A hash table is only as fast as its hash and its load factor; resize before it fills, and use a keyed hash when other people choose the keys.
  3. When you only need the next item in order, use a heap, build it in one pass, and give A* an estimate that never overestimates.
  4. An unbalanced search tree is a linked list waiting for sorted input; balance it, or use a library that does.
  5. Use the library sort, know whether it's stable, and don't sort everything to find the top few.
  6. Linear-time sorts buy their speed with assumptions about the keys; check the range and the distribution before you rely on them.
  7. When exact answers cost too much memory, choose the error you can live with, size the sketch for your peak, and confirm a "probably" before acting on it.
  8. Before hunting for a fast algorithm, ask whether the problem is NP-hard; if it is, use small inputs, structure, approximation, measured heuristics or a solver.
