How to use this volume

Every chapter has the same shape. It opens with its idea in one sentence, explains it through worked examples, lists the traps people fall into, and closes with exercises and a checklist you can apply to real code the same week.

The exercises matter more than the reading. Do them in your own editor, not in your head. Each has a solution folded underneath it: try first, then open it. Where your answer differs, decide whether it is worse or merely different. In this subject it is often the second.

Examples are in Python because it reads almost like pseudocode, but every idea transfers to other languages, and the text says so where a language feature changes the advice. Some early examples use floating-point numbers for money to stay short. Chapter 11 explains why real code must not.

Tick a chapter as read in its heading and the contents will track your progress. The marks are kept in this browser only.

The one question

Nearly everything in this volume answers one question: how expensive will the next change be?

Good names make the next change cheaper to understand. Deep modules let you change one thing without touching five. Tests make change safe, and refactoring stops its cost creeping upwards. Review, writing and estimation spread understanding across a team so no change depends on one person's memory.

When two pieces of advice conflict, and several in this book do, ask which one makes the next change cheaper for the next reader. That reader is usually you, six months from now, with none of today's context. The Pragmatic Programmer compresses the whole idea into three letters: ETC, easier to change.

Part I

Code that reads well

Code is read far more often than it is written. These four chapters are about the reader: the names they see first, the functions they step through, the comments that tell them what code cannot, and the shape of modules that let them understand one part without holding the whole system in their head.

Chapter 01

Naming

6 min read2 exercises

A name is the smallest unit of design. A good one is a compressed explanation. A bad one is a small lie that every future reader has to see through.

Why names carry so much weight

When you open an unfamiliar file, names are what you read first and most. You skim identifiers to build a map before you read a single expression. A precise name lets the reader skip the body entirely. A vague one forces them into it. A misleading one sends them the wrong way with confidence.

Naming is hard because it forces you to decide what a thing is. If you can't name a function without the word "and", or you can only reach for "manager" or "helper", the difficulty is usually telling you something about the design rather than about your vocabulary. Treat naming trouble as a design signal.

Principles that hold up

  • Name for intent, not for type or mechanism. pending_orders tells the reader what they are holding. order_list tells them only the container, and data tells them nothing at all.
  • Make length proportional to scope. A loop index that lives for two lines can be i. A constant read from forty files deserves a full, unambiguous name. Short names in wide scopes and long names in tiny ones are both mistakes.
  • Use the words your domain uses. If the finance team says an invoice is voided, write invoice.void(), not invoice.set_status(3). Domain-driven design calls this a ubiquitous language: the same terms in conversation, in tickets, and in code.
  • Put units in the name when the type doesn't carry them. timeout_seconds, price_cents, distance_km. In 1999 NASA lost the Mars Climate Orbiter because one team's software reported thrust in pound-force seconds and another's read it as newton-seconds.
  • Name booleans as predicates. is_active, has_shipped and can_retry read naturally after if. Avoid negatives: if not is_disabled makes every reader perform a double negation in their head.
  • Name functions for what they return or what they do. Queries are nouns or questions: total_price(), is_eligible(). Commands are verbs: send_invoice(), cancel_order(). A get_user() that also creates a user when one is missing is lying. Call it get_or_create_user().
  • One word per concept, one concept per word. If fetch, get, load and retrieve all mean "read from the database" in one codebase, readers will assume they differ. Pick one. Equally, don't let account mean a login in one module and a billing entity in another.
  • Avoid words that say nothing. data, info, item, manager, handler, processor, helper, util. Each is a placeholder for a decision not yet made. A UserManager usually turns out to be three things: a repository, a password policy, and a notification sender.

Renaming is cheap, so do it often

Modern editors rename safely across an entire codebase in seconds. Treat every name as a draft that improves as your understanding does. The moment you realise what a thing actually is, rename it, because otherwise that understanding is lost when you close the file.

The exception is names that escape your codebase: public API fields, database columns, event names, configuration keys. Those are contracts with other people, and chapter 14 covers how to change them.

Common traps
  • Names that encode the whole implementation. is_least_relevant_multiple_of_larger_prime_factor came up in a public exchange between John Ousterhout and Robert Martin. It is precise and nearly unreadable. When a name gets that long, the idea probably wants a comment or a better abstraction.
  • Private abbreviations. cstmr_acct_bal saves eight keystrokes and costs every reader a decoding step. Widely known abbreviations such as id, url and http are fine.
  • Names that drift. A send_email that now also writes an audit record has a stale name. Stale names are worse than vague ones, because readers trust them.
  • Cute names. yeet_order() is funny once, to one person.

Chapter 02

Functions

7 min read3 exercises

A function should do one thing, at one level of abstraction, and its signature should tell a caller everything they need without opening the body.

What "one thing" means

The rule is old and usually misread as "short". A better test is whether you can describe what the function does in one sentence without the word "and". If you can, it probably does one thing, whatever its length. If the sentence needs "validates the cart and charges the card and emails a receipt", you have three functions sharing a body.

The second test is levels of abstraction. A function that reads like a sequence of business steps shouldn't suddenly drop into string slicing or HTTP details. Mixing levels forces the reader to change zoom constantly.

checkout now reads as the four steps of the process, and each step can be understood, tested and changed alone. price takes now as an argument instead of reading the clock itself, which makes it deterministic; chapter 6 explains why that matters so much. The payment and email details moved behind payments.charge and send_receipt, where they live with the rest of their kind.

How long is too long?

There is no line count. Robert Martin argues for functions of a handful of lines. John Ousterhout argues that aggressive splitting produces conjoined functions that can only be understood together, so a reader must jump between five of them to follow a single idea. They debated this in public and did not converge.

The useful synthesis is two questions. Can a caller use this function without reading its body? Can a reader understand this function without reading the functions it calls? When splitting a function makes either answer worse, don't split it.

Arguments

  • Fewer is better. Every parameter is something a caller must understand and supply. Zero to three is comfortable. More usually means some of them belong together.
  • Flag arguments are two functions in disguise. render(invoice, True) means nothing at the call site. Split it into render_draft and render_final, or at least make the flag keyword-only so the call reads render(invoice, draft=True).
  • Group parameters that travel together. If street, city, postcode, country appear in five signatures, they are an Address. Introducing that object is one of the most common refactorings in chapter 11.
  • Don't mutate your arguments unless the name says so. A caller who passes a list to total_of(items) does not expect to find it sorted afterwards.

Commands and queries

Bertrand Meyer's command-query separation says a function should either change state or return information, never both. A query can be called any number of times without consequence, so it is safe in a log line, a debugger or a test. A command's name warns you that something happens.

Functions that do both surprise people: a get_next_invoice_number() that also increments a counter, or an is_valid() that records the check. The standard library breaks the rule where it's genuinely convenient, list.pop() being the famous case, and that's fine as long as the name makes the side effect obvious.

Guard clauses over nesting

Each special case is handled and dismissed at the top, and the main path runs unindented to the end. The reader never has to track which else belongs to which if.

Functional core, imperative shell

Gary Bernhardt's name for a structure that makes most code easy to test. Put decisions in pure functions that take values and return values: no clock, no network, no database. Wrap them in a thin outer layer that gathers inputs, calls the core, and performs the effects. The core is where the bugs would be, and it needs no mocks to test. The shell is so simple it barely needs testing at all.

Common traps
  • Extracting until nothing is left. Ten three-line functions that each call the next are harder to follow than one thirty-line function. Inline them back.
  • Names that restate the body. Extracting x > 0 and y > 0 into check_x_and_y() adds a jump and no meaning. is_in_first_quadrant() adds meaning.
  • Hidden state threading. Helpers that read and write instance variables set by other helpers are conjoined. Pass values explicitly.
  • Returning None for failure. Every caller must remember to check, and the ones who forget crash later, far from the cause. Raise instead. Chapter 16 covers this.

Chapter 03

Comments

6 min read2 exercises

Comments should say what the code cannot: why a decision was made, what an interface promises, and what a reader would otherwise have to learn the hard way.

The disagreement worth understanding

Few topics split experienced engineers like comments. Robert Martin treats a comment as a small failure: a sign the code didn't express itself, and a liability that will drift out of date. John Ousterhout treats comments as essential. Code can say what happens, but it cannot say why, and it cannot describe an abstraction without the reader reading its implementation. Their public exchange ended with each holding the opposite default: Martin distrusts a comment until it proves useful, and Ousterhout assumes it helps until it proves wasteful.

Both are right, about different kinds of comment. Comments that narrate what the code does are noise, and Martin's instinct is correct for them. Comments that describe an interface or record a reason are irreplaceable, and Ousterhout's instinct is correct there. The skill is telling the kinds apart.

Comments worth writing

KindWhat it saysExample
InterfaceWhat a function promises its caller: inputs, output, errors, side effectsA docstring on a public function
WhyThe reason for a decision that looks wrong or arbitrary"Retry twice: the provider returns 502s for about a second during their deploys."
WarningA consequence the reader must know before changing the code"Not thread-safe. Callers must hold the order lock."
ReferenceWhere the full explanation livesA link to the spec, the ticket, or the paper
IntentThe idea behind code that can only execute it"Both lists are sorted, so advance whichever head is smaller."

And the comments worth deleting:

  • Narration. # loop over the orders above for order in orders:.
  • Commented-out code. Version control remembers it. Nobody will ever be sure it's safe to delete, so everybody leaves it, forever.
  • Journal comments. Change history belongs in commit messages, where it's attached to the change.
  • Banners. # ===== HELPERS ===== usually means the file wants to be two files.
  • Apologies. # sorry, hack without saying what the hack works around. Replace it with a why comment and a ticket link.

Write the interface comment first

Ousterhout suggests writing the interface comment before the implementation. It works as a design tool: if the comment is long, full of conditions and caveats, the abstraction is probably wrong, and it's cheaper to find out before the code exists. A good interface comment describes what, never how. The how belongs to the implementation, which must stay free to change.

Every narration comment is gone, and the body is no harder to read without them. The docstring now tells a caller three things the code alone could not: that failure leaves state unchanged, what the reservation's lifecycle is, and the locking rule. None of that is visible in seven lines of implementation.

The comment that protects a fence

G. K. Chesterton described a fence across a road. A reformer wants it removed because he can see no use for it. The wiser reply: go and find out why it was put there, and then you may take it down. Code is full of fences, such as an odd sleep, a check that looks redundant, or a sort that seems unnecessary. A one-line why comment is the difference between the next engineer understanding the fence and removing it in a cleanup that brings back a production bug from three years ago.

Common traps
  • Docstrings that repeat the types. sku (str): the sku adds nothing a type hint doesn't. Describe meaning, units and constraints instead: "a catalogue SKU, case-sensitive".
  • Stale comments. A comment the code has outgrown is worse than none. Review comments as carefully as the code beside them.
  • Comments excusing bad names. x = 86400 # seconds in a day should be SECONDS_PER_DAY = 86400.
  • Explaining how in an interface comment. It couples callers to the implementation you'll want to change later.

Chapter 04

Complexity and deep modules

8 min read3 exercises

Complexity is anything that makes a system hard to understand or change. The strongest defence is the deep module: a lot of functionality behind a small, simple interface.

What complexity looks like

John Ousterhout defines complexity by its effects, which makes it recognisable in practice. It shows up in three ways.

  • Change amplification. A simple change requires edits in many places. Changing the currency symbol means touching forty templates.
  • Cognitive load. How much a developer must hold in mind to make a change safely. A function that only works if you remember to call init() first and flush() afterwards has high cognitive load.
  • Unknown unknowns. It isn't obvious which code must change, or what you need to know to change it. This is the worst of the three, because you can't see the cost until something breaks.

Its two root causes are dependencies, where code can't be understood or changed in isolation, and obscurity, where important information isn't obvious. Most of the rest of this volume is about reducing one or the other.

Deep and shallow modules

Picture every module as a rectangle. The top edge is its interface: everything a caller must learn to use it. The area beneath is the functionality it provides. A deep module has a narrow top and a large area. It gives a lot and asks little. A shallow module has a wide top and a small area, and its interface costs nearly as much to learn as its implementation would to write.

Unix file I/O is the classic deep module. Five calls, open, read, write, lseek and close, hide disk layout, caching, permissions, device drivers and concurrent access. A class whose methods each forward to one method on another class is the classic shallow module: an interface to learn, and nothing behind it.

A worked example: a rate limiter

Both implement the same token bucket. In the shallow design, every endpoint repeats five lines of algorithm, and a caller who forgets refill or save_bucket gets a limiter that is subtly wrong. Switch to a sliding window and you edit every caller: change amplification. The deep design hides the algorithm, the storage, the clock and the locking behind one method. The interface got smaller, and the module got more powerful.

Information hiding, and how it leaks

David Parnas made the case in 1972: divide a system by the design decisions most likely to change, and give each module one decision to hide. The opposite is information leakage, where one decision is reflected in several modules. If both the CSV exporter and the CSV importer know the column order, changing the format means changing both, and nothing forces you to remember.

A common cause is temporal decomposition: structuring code by the order things happen. A read step, a parse step and a write step feel natural, but if reading and writing both need to know the file format, the format leaks into both. Structure by knowledge, not by time. One module owns the format and offers both directions.

Pull complexity downwards

When complexity is unavoidable, it's better for the module to absorb it than to push it onto every caller. A module has one author and many users. Sensible defaults, handling common edge cases internally, and retrying transient failures are all ways of pulling complexity down. A configuration option for something the module could work out itself does the opposite: it pushes a decision onto every user.

Define errors out of existence

Every error a module can raise is part of its interface, and every caller must deal with it. Sometimes the cheapest fix is to redefine the operation so the error can't happen. Python's standard library has good examples.

Each one takes a case that would otherwise be an exception and gives it a sensible meaning. The same move works in your own code: make unsubscribe succeed when the user is already unsubscribed, and every caller loses a try block. Use judgement, though. Some errors must stay loud, like a payment that failed.

Tactical and strategic programming

Tactical programming aims to get the current feature working as fast as possible. Each shortcut is small and seems reasonable, and complexity accumulates one reasonable decision at a time. Strategic programming treats working code as necessary but not sufficient: the goal is a good design that also works. Ousterhout suggests investing something like ten to twenty percent of development time in design improvements. It pays back within months, because every later change is cheaper.

Common traps
  • Classitis. Splitting everything into tiny classes because small feels good produces many shallow modules, each with an interface to learn and little behind it.
  • Pass-through methods. A method that only calls another method with the same signature adds interface without adding depth. Merge the layers, or give each a distinct job.
  • Configuration as an escape hatch. Every option you expose is a decision every user must now make.
  • Pretending abstractions don't leak. Joel Spolsky's law says all non-trivial abstractions leak to some degree. Design for the leaks you can't avoid, such as network failure, and document them in the interface.

Two modules share a design decision. Name it, and restructure so only one module knows it.

The leaked decision is the password storage format: the algorithm, the iteration count, the salt length and the byte layout. Both modules know all four. Raise the iteration count in one and every login breaks.

# passwords.py: the only module that knows the storage format.
ITERATIONS = 600_000
SALT_BYTES = 16


def hash_password(password: str) -> bytes:
    salt = os.urandom(SALT_BYTES)
    return salt + _derive(password, salt)


def verify_password(password: str, stored: bytes) -> bool:
    salt, digest = stored[:SALT_BYTES], stored[SALT_BYTES:]
    return hmac.compare_digest(_derive(password, salt), digest)


def _derive(password: str, salt: bytes) -> bytes:
    return hashlib.pbkdf2_hmac("sha256", password.encode(), salt, ITERATIONS)

Chapter 05

SOLID, read critically

7 min read2 exercises

SOLID is five heuristics about managing dependencies in object-oriented code. Learn the problem each one solves, because that is how you'll know when it doesn't apply.

Robert Martin assembled these principles around the turn of the millennium, and they became an interview staple. They're genuinely useful at the boundaries of a system, where change is likely. Applied everywhere, they produce an interface for every class and five files for every feature. Treat each one as the answer to a specific question.

S: single responsibility

The original wording was "a class should have only one reason to change". Martin later sharpened it: a module should be responsible to one actor, meaning one group of people who might ask for changes. The point isn't that a class does one tiny thing. It's that changes requested by different people shouldn't collide in the same code.

A tax rule change and a logo change now touch the same class, the same tests and the same review. Split along the lines of who asks for change: keep total on Invoice, move rendering to an InvoicePdf, and sending to a ReceiptSender.

O: open for extension, closed for modification

You should be able to add behaviour by adding code, rather than by editing code that already works. In practice, that means finding the axis along which variation actually happens and making it pluggable.

The caveat is the important part. Do this only where variation has actually happened. Building plug-in points for variations nobody has asked for is speculative generality, and it makes code harder to read for no benefit. YAGNI, "you aren't gonna need it", is the counterweight to this principle.

L: Liskov substitution

Barbara Liskov's principle: anywhere code uses a type, it must be able to use any subtype without noticing. A subtype must accept at least what its parent accepts, promise at least what its parent promises, and raise no new surprises. The textbook violation is a Square that inherits from Rectangle and breaks the expectation that setting the width leaves the height alone. The one you'll actually meet lives in storage backends and test fakes.

Code tested against the in-memory version never sees UserNotFound, so the error path goes untested and breaks in production. Chapter 9 shows the fix: one set of contract tests, run against both implementations.

I: interface segregation

Clients shouldn't depend on methods they don't use. A function that only reads a file shouldn't demand an object that can also delete buckets and rotate credentials. That's harder to call, harder to fake, and coupled to changes it doesn't care about. In Python, a Protocol states exactly what a function needs.

D: dependency inversion

High-level policy shouldn't depend on low-level detail. Both should depend on an abstraction, and the policy side should own it. Your order logic shouldn't import a payment provider's SDK. It should declare what it needs from a payment gateway, and a small adapter should provide that on top of the provider.

Imports now point from the Stripe adapter towards your domain, not the other way round. Switching providers, or using a fake in tests, touches one small class.

The critique

Two criticisms are worth taking seriously. First, SOLID was framed for the class-based languages of the 1990s. In a language with first-class functions, several of the principles collapse into "pass a function", and in data-oriented code some barely apply. Second, principles phrased as rules invite mechanical application: an interface for every class, including those with one implementation that will never change.

In 2022 Dan North proposed CUPID as an alternative framing: properties to move towards rather than rules to obey. Code should be composable, follow the Unix philosophy of doing one thing well, be predictable, be idiomatic for its language, and be domain-based in its names and structure. You needn't adopt it. It's a useful reminder that the goal is code that's pleasant to change, not code that passes a checklist.

  • Inheriting to reuse code. Liskov violations nearly always start here. Chapter 6 covers the alternative.

Chapter 06

Composition and dependency injection

5 min read2 exercises

Build behaviour by combining small objects instead of inheriting it, and hand objects their dependencies instead of letting them find their own. You get code you can rearrange, and seams you can test through.

What goes wrong with inheritance

Inheritance couples a subclass to its parent's implementation, not just its interface. Change the internals of a parent method, and a subclass that overrode a different method can break. This is the fragile base class problem. Hierarchies also force a single axis of classification onto things that vary along several.

Adding a push channel is now one class, not four. The Design Patterns authors gave this advice in 1994: favour object composition over class inheritance. Inheritance still has its place, in a genuine "is-a" relationship with a stable parent, or in a framework explicitly designed to be subclassed. It just shouldn't be the default.

Dependency injection, without a framework

Dependency injection sounds like a technology. It is only this: an object receives the things it depends on instead of constructing or looking them up. Pass them to the constructor.

All construction happens in one place, near the program's entry point. Everything else receives what it needs. You can read main.py to see how the application is wired, and a test can wire it differently.

Time and randomness are dependencies

Code that calls datetime.now(), random.random() or uuid4() directly is nondeterministic, and nondeterministic code produces flaky tests. Treat the clock and the random source like any other dependency.

Seams

Michael Feathers defines a seam as a place where you can change what a program does without editing it at that place. Constructor injection creates seams on purpose: ReminderJob behaves differently depending on what you pass it, and not one line inside it changes.

Python also has weaker seams. monkeypatch and unittest.mock.patch swap names at runtime. They work, and they're essential for legacy code, but they couple tests to module structure: move an import and the patch silently stops applying. Prefer seams you design over seams you patch.

The same idea at architecture scale

Alistair Cockburn's hexagonal architecture, also called ports and adapters, applies this to a whole application. The core declares ports, the interfaces it needs. Adapters implement them for real infrastructure: a Postgres adapter, a payment provider adapter, an HTTP controller. Tests plug in fakes. It's the dependency inversion from chapter 5, repeated at every boundary.

Common traps
  • A constructor with nine parameters. Injection makes dependencies visible, and sometimes what you see is a class doing too much. Split it rather than hiding the list inside a container.
  • Service locators. A global registry you ask for things, like registry.get("mailer"), hides the dependencies again. It looks like injection and delivers none of the benefits.
  • Framework first. Containers help in large applications. In small ones, plain constructor arguments are clearer. Understand the plain version before reaching for the magic.
  • Wrappers nobody can read. Logged(Retrying(Metered(Cached(Timed(client))))) is hard to debug. Build it once, in the composition root, and give it a name.

Chapter 07

Design patterns worth knowing

7 min read3 exercises

Patterns are names for solutions that keep recurring. Knowing them lets you recognise a problem's shape and discuss it in one word. The deeper skill is noticing when your language already solves the problem, or when you don't have the problem at all.

Where patterns came from

Design Patterns, published in 1994 by Gamma, Helm, Johnson and Vlissides, catalogued twenty-three recurring solutions in object-oriented code. Many of them work around limits of C++ and early Java, languages without first-class functions. In Python, JavaScript or Kotlin several shrink to a line: Strategy is passing a function, Command is a closure, Iterator is a generator, Singleton is a module.

That doesn't make the catalogue obsolete. The problems are still real, and the names are still the fastest way to describe a structure in a design review. What changes is how much ceremony the solution needs.

Adapter

Wrap something you don't control behind an interface you do. Every third-party API your domain touches should go through one. It isolates the vendor's quirks, gives you one place to handle their errors, and gives tests something to fake.

The vendor's longitude-first quirk now lives in exactly one place.

Strategy

Choose an algorithm at runtime. In languages with first-class functions, a strategy is simply a function you pass in.

Decorator

Add behaviour around an object or function without changing it. The Retrying and Logged wrappers in chapter 6 are decorators. Python's @ syntax applies the same idea to functions.

Keep decorators few and obvious. A stack of them that changes a function's signature or control flow makes it hard to see what a call actually does.

Facade

One simple entry point in front of a complicated subsystem. It's chapter 4's deep module under another name: the Mailer.send() of exercise 4.1 is a facade over connection handling, authentication, message building and retries.

Observer

Let parts of a system react to an event without the event's source knowing about them. When an order ships, send an email, record a metric and notify the warehouse, without the shipping code importing any of them.

The cost is invisible control flow. Reading publish tells you nothing about what will happen, and one failing handler can surprise the publisher. Use it where the decoupling is worth that, and keep subscriptions in one easy-to-find place.

Factory

Put complex or conditional construction in one place. A function that returns the right object for a configuration is a factory. You rarely need a class for it.

Repository

Not in the 1994 book, but everywhere since Martin Fowler's catalogue of enterprise patterns and Eric Evans' domain-driven design. A repository offers a collection-like interface over storage, such as orders.get(id), orders.add(order) and orders.overdue(today), so domain code never sees SQL. It's also what chapter 9's fakes replace.

State machine

When an entity moves through a lifecycle, make its states and legal transitions explicit instead of scattering status checks across the codebase. The classic version uses a class per state. A transition table is often clearer.

Every legal move is visible in one table. "Can a cancelled order be shipped?" is answered by reading, not by searching.

One anti-pattern: Singleton

Singleton promises one instance and a global access point. The first is occasionally needed. The second is global state under a respectable name, which brings hidden dependencies, tests that depend on order, and code that can't run with two configurations. If you need one instance, create one in the composition root and pass it to whatever needs it.

Pattern-itis

The Spring framework for Java contains a real class called AbstractSingletonProxyFactoryBean. It's often cited as the logical end point of applying patterns because they exist rather than because a problem needs them. The test for a pattern is the one this volume applies everywhere: does it make the next change cheaper? If you can't name the change, don't add the pattern.

Chapter 08

What tests are for

8 min read3 exercises

A test suite exists to let you change code with confidence. A good test fails only when behaviour you care about breaks, and when it fails, it tells you what broke.

Four jobs a test suite does

  • Catch regressions. What worked yesterday still works today.
  • Make refactoring safe. Without tests, every structural improvement is a gamble, so people stop making them, and complexity wins.
  • Document behaviour. A well-named test is an executable example of what the code promises. Unlike a comment, it can't silently drift.
  • Give design feedback. Code that's painful to test is usually painful to use: too many dependencies, hidden state, mixed responsibilities.

What makes a good test

Kent Beck lists twelve desirable properties of tests, and notes that they trade off against each other. Six matter most day to day.

  • Behavioural. It checks what the code does, not how. Change the implementation without changing behaviour, and the test still passes.
  • Specific. When it fails, the cause is obvious from its name and message.
  • Deterministic. Same code, same result, every run. No dependence on time, randomness, ordering or the network.
  • Fast. Fast enough that you run it after every small change.
  • Isolated. It doesn't need other tests to run first, and leaves nothing behind.
  • Readable. A newcomer can see what's being claimed without reading the code under test.

Anatomy of a test

Arrange, act, assert: set up a situation, perform one action, check the outcome. Behaviour-driven teams say given, when, then. Either way, one test checks one behaviour, and its name states that behaviour as a fact.

When the first version fails, its name says only "coupon", and it has reached into a private method that a refactoring may rename. When one of the second pair fails, the failure reads like a bug report.

The word "unit" is contested. Martin Fowler distinguishes solitary unit tests, which replace every collaborator with a double, from sociable ones, which let real collaborators run and replace only those that are slow or nondeterministic. Much of the argument about testing strategy is people using the same word for these two different things.

Pyramids, trophies and honeycombs

Mike Cohn's test pyramid says: many unit tests, fewer integration tests, very few end-to-end tests, because the higher you go, the slower and more brittle tests become. Kent C. Spotify's honeycomb makes a similar case for microservices, where most bugs live at the boundaries between services.

Martin Fowler's verdict on the debate is the useful one. The shapes differ mostly because people define "unit" differently, and the ratio matters far less than quality. Most teams would gain more from tests that run fast, fail reliably, and fail only for useful reasons than from any change of shape.

Test behaviour, not implementation

A test that asserts on private methods, internal data structures, or the exact sequence of internal calls fails whenever the implementation changes, even when behaviour doesn't. Those failures teach a team that refactoring breaks tests, and so the team stops refactoring. Test through the same interface your callers use.

Coverage

Coverage reports which lines ran during the tests. It's a useful negative signal: uncovered code is certainly untested. It's a weak positive signal, because a line can run without any assertion checking what it did. Setting a coverage target turns it into a goal, and Goodhart's law applies: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Chapter 10 describes mutation testing, which measures what coverage only pretends to.

Flaky tests

A flaky test passes and fails on the same code. It's worse than having no test, because it teaches everyone to ignore red. The usual causes are real time, unseeded randomness, state shared between tests, test ordering, real network calls, and asynchronous work checked before it finishes. Fix a flaky test the day you find it, or quarantine it with a ticket and an owner. Never simply rerun until it's green.

Common traps
  • Recomputing the expected value. assert total(items) == sum(i.price * i.qty for i in items) repeats the implementation's logic, so it repeats its bugs. Use literal expected values.
  • Asserting on log output. Logs are for people, and they change for good reasons. Assert on outcomes.
  • Testing the framework. A test that checks your ORM saves a field is a test of the ORM.
  • One giant test per feature. It fails for twenty reasons and names none of them.
  • Sleeping to wait. time.sleep(2) is either too long or, on a slow CI machine, too short. Wait for a condition, with a timeout.

Test doubles

6 min read3 exercises

Replace a real dependency only when it's slow, nondeterministic, or has effects you can't allow in a test. When you do, prefer the double that couples the test least to how your code works.

The vocabulary

Gerard Meszaros coined "test double", after the stunt double who stands in for an actor, and named five kinds. People use "mock" loosely for all of them, which causes real confusion in code review.

DummyFills a parameter; never actually usedA logger the tested path never callsNothing

State or behaviour?

A test can check the outcome, meaning the state of the world after the action, or it can check the interactions, meaning which calls were made along the way. Martin Fowler's essay Mocks Aren't Stubs describes two schools. Classicists use real objects where practical and verify state. Mockists replace every collaborator and verify interactions. Both schools produce working software, but the classicist default ages better, because interaction tests encode how the code works, and how is exactly what refactoring changes.

How over-mocking goes wrong

This test fails if you batch the save, rename send_receipt, or move the email into an event handler, and none of those change behaviour. Worse, it passes when the code is broken. If the real repo.save requires a field that place never sets, the mock accepts the call happily.

Don't mock what you don't own

When you mock a third-party library directly, the mock encodes your belief about how the library behaves. If the belief is wrong, or the library changes, your tests keep passing while production breaks. The fix is the adapter from chapter 7. Wrap the library in a small class you own, fake that class in most tests, and test the adapter itself against the real library, or the vendor's sandbox, in a handful of integration tests. The idea comes from Steve Freeman and Nat Pryce, who helped invent mock objects in the first place.

Keeping fakes honest

A fake is only useful if it behaves like the real thing, including its errors. The technique that guarantees it is a shared contract test: one suite, run against every implementation. It's chapter 5's Liskov substitution, enforced by the test runner.

Every test in this file runs twice. If the in-memory fake ever drifts from Postgres, a test fails before anyone relies on the difference. The database is only started for the Postgres runs, because getfixturevalue requests it lazily.

When a mock is the right tool

Checking interactions is right when the interaction is the behaviour: an outbound message with no other observable effect. Even then, a spy or a recording fake usually reads better than a strict mock, because you assert on what was sent after the fact instead of scripting expectations up front. Save strict mocks for cases where the exact protocol matters, such as asserting that a retry loop calls a failing dependency exactly three times.

Python specifics that bite

  • Patch where the name is looked up, not where it's defined. If billing.py does from payments import charge, you must patch billing.charge. Patching payments.charge replaces a name that billing no longer uses. This is the most common mock.patch bug there is.
  • Use autospec. A plain Mock accepts any method with any arguments, typos included. create_autospec(RealClass), or patch(..., autospec=True), rejects calls the real object would reject.
  • Beware mocks returning mocks. client.get().json()["items"] on a MagicMock quietly returns another mock. If a test passes through a chain like that, check it's asserting anything real.
Common traps
  • Mocking the code under test. If you patch a method on the object you're testing, you're testing your patch.
  • One mock per collaborator, by reflex. Ask first whether the real collaborator is actually slow or nondeterministic. Often it's a plain function that should simply run.
  • Tests that pass with the code deleted. If a test is all assert_called checks on mocks returning mocks, delete the body of the function under test and see whether it notices.

Chapter 10

TDD, properties, snapshots and mutants

7 min read3 exercises

Four techniques, each catching a different kind of mistake. Test-driven development shapes the design; property-based testing finds the inputs you didn't imagine; snapshots guard large outputs; mutation testing tests your tests.

Test-driven development

Kent Beck's cycle has three steps. Red: write a small test for behaviour that doesn't exist yet, and watch it fail. Green: write the simplest code that makes it pass. Refactor: clean up both, with the tests as your net. Then repeat, in cycles of a few minutes.

What it gives you: every line of production code exists because a test demanded it, so coverage comes free. Each test is written from the caller's side, so an awkward interface is felt immediately. And you're never more than a few minutes away from working code.

Here is the rhythm, building a parser for durations like 1h30m.

Notice what the tests drove. The malformed-input test forced a decision that the happy-path tests never raised: is 1h 30m, with a space, valid? Here the answer is no, and that decision is now written down where the next person will find it.

The criticism. John Ousterhout argues that TDD's tiny increments encourage tactical programming: you optimise each step and never stand back to design the whole. Robert Martin replies that the refactor step is exactly where design happens. The practical middle is to test-drive behaviours rather than methods, and to stop and think about structure every few cycles.

TDD shines on well-understood logic, and on bug fixes, where writing the failing test first proves you've actually reproduced the bug. It struggles with exploratory work where you don't yet know what you're building. For that, write a throwaway spike first, learn from it, delete it, and then test-drive the real thing.

Property-based testing

Example-based tests check the cases you thought of. A property-based test states something that must hold for every input, and lets a library generate hundreds of inputs looking for a counterexample. When it finds one, it shrinks it to the smallest input that still fails. The idea began with QuickCheck for Haskell in 2000. In Python the library is Hypothesis; in JavaScript, fast-check.

Four shapes of property cover most needs:

  • Round trip. Decoding what you encoded gives back the original: parse(format(x)) == x.
  • Invariant. Something always true of the output: a sorted list is ordered, and has the same elements as the input.
  • Oracle. A simple, slow, obviously correct implementation agrees with the fast one.
  • Idempotence. Doing it twice equals doing it once: normalize(normalize(s)) == normalize(s).

Suppose we add format_duration, the inverse of our parser, and state the round trip.

Hypothesis fails almost at once and reports the smallest counterexample: seconds=0. format_duration(0) returns an empty string, and the parser rightly rejects it. Nobody writing examples by hand thought to try zero. The fix is a decision, format zero as 0s, after which the property holds for every value in the range.

Snapshot tests

A snapshot, or golden, test saves the output of a run to a file and fails whenever later output differs. It's the cheapest way to guard something large and structured: a rendered email, an invoice's text, a command-line tool's help output, a JSON response. Approval testing, a technique popularised by Llewellyn Falco, is the same idea with a human approving each change.

Their weakness is social. When a snapshot fails, the easy move is to regenerate it without reading the diff, and a test that's updated without being read tests nothing. Keep snapshots small enough to review, commit them, and treat a snapshot diff in a pull request as code to be reviewed.

Mutation testing

Coverage says a line ran. Mutation testing asks whether any test would notice if that line were wrong. A tool makes many small changes to your code, one at a time: < becomes <=, + becomes -, a call is deleted. Each changed program is a mutant. If some test fails, the mutant is killed. If every test still passes, the mutant survived, and you've found behaviour your suite doesn't check.

Mutate >= into >, and both tests still pass. The mutant survives, because the suite never checks the boundary, which is exactly where a customer with a basket of precisely fifty dollars will find the bug. Add assert qualifies_for_free_shipping(5000) and it dies.

Tools include mutmut and Cosmic Ray for Python, Stryker for JavaScript, TypeScript, C# and Scala, PIT for Java, and cargo-mutants for Rust. Mutation testing is slow, since it runs the suite once per mutant, so point it at critical modules or at changed code rather than everything on every commit.

Common traps
  • TDD as dogma. Test-first is a technique for getting feedback, not a moral position. Use it where the feedback is worth having.
  • Weak properties. assert isinstance(result, str) holds for every input and proves almost nothing. A good property would fail for a plausible bug.
  • Rubber-stamped snapshots. If your team updates snapshots in bulk without reading them, delete the snapshots.
  • Chasing a perfect mutation score. Some mutants are equivalent: the change doesn't alter behaviour, so no test can kill them. Aim to understand the survivors, not to eliminate every one.

Chapter 11

Refactoring

9 min read3 exercises

Refactoring is changing the structure of code without changing what it does, in steps small enough that you're never more than a minute or two from passing tests.

What refactoring is, and isn't

Martin Fowler's definition is strict on purpose. A refactoring preserves behaviour. Fixing a bug while restructuring isn't refactoring, and neither is a rewrite. Kent Beck describes two hats: when you add function, you don't restructure, and when you restructure, you don't add function. You can swap hats every few minutes, but you wear one at a time, and ideally each gets its own commit.

The discipline is what makes it safe. Each step is small and mechanical, the tests run after every one, and if anything goes red you undo the last step rather than debugging it.

When to refactor

  • Preparatory. Before adding a feature, reshape the code so the feature is easy to add. Beck's version: first make the change easy, then make the easy change.
  • Comprehension. When you've worked out what confusing code does, encode that understanding in names and structure before you forget it.
  • Litter-picking. Leave code a little better than you found it. Small, continuous, and part of normal work rather than a separate project.
  • The rule of three. The first time, just write it. The second time, wince at the duplication. The third time, refactor.

When not to: code that works, is rarely read, and never changes. Refactoring pays off through future changes. If there won't be any, there's no payoff.

Smells, and the refactorings that fix them

Fowler and Beck called the symptoms of design problems code smells. They aren't bugs. They're signs that the next change will cost more than it should. The second edition of Refactoring lists twenty-four. These twelve account for most of what you'll meet.

Replace primitive with object: money

Primitive obsession deserves its own example, because its most common form is also a real bug. Binary floating point can't represent most decimal fractions exactly.

Represent money as integer minor units or as Decimal, and wrap it in a type so the rules live in one place.

Adding dollars to euros is now an error instead of a silent nonsense number. Rounding happens in one place, by one rule. Every signature that takes Money says what it means. And the factory takes a string on purpose, because Decimal(0.1) would faithfully preserve the float's error.

Habits that keep it safe

  • Let the editor do it. Rename, extract, inline and move are automated in every serious editor, and automated refactorings preserve behaviour by construction.
  • Commit after every green step. Small commits make it trivial to undo the step that went wrong.
  • Keep refactoring commits apart from behaviour changes. A reviewer can verify a pure refactoring quickly when they know nothing is meant to change.
  • Refactor in the main line. A refactoring branch that lives for weeks conflicts with everything. Small pieces, merged alongside feature work, actually land.
Common traps
  • Refactoring without tests. Without a net, a refactoring is a rewrite with extra confidence. Get tests first; chapter 12 shows how when there are none.
  • Big-bang restructures. A two-week refactoring branch usually dies. Small steps merge.
  • "Refactoring" as a euphemism. If behaviour changes, call it a change. Stakeholders and reviewers both deserve an accurate word.
  • Abstracting too early. Two similar pieces of code aren't yet a pattern. Sandi Metz's advice: duplication is far cheaper than the wrong abstraction.

Chapter 12

Legacy code

9 min read3 exercises

Michael Feathers defines legacy code as code without tests. The craft is getting a safety net around it before you change it, one small seam at a time, without stopping the business while you do.

Why legacy code is hard

The problem isn't age or style. It's that you can't change the code safely, because you can't tell whether a change broke something. And you can't easily add tests, because the code wasn't written to be tested: it reads the clock, calls the database in the middle of a calculation, and constructs its own collaborators. Feathers calls this the legacy code dilemma. To change code safely you need tests, and to add tests you often need to change code.

The legacy change algorithm

  1. Identify change points. Where exactly does the change need to go?
  2. Find test points. Where can you observe the effect of that code?
  3. Break dependencies. Make just enough careful, minimal edits to get the code into a test harness.
  4. Write tests. Pin down the current behaviour around the change points.
  5. Make the change, and refactor. Now, with a net.

Step 3 is the dangerous one, because you're changing code without tests. Keep those edits tiny and mechanical, lean on your editor's automated refactorings, and review them twice.

Characterisation tests

A characterisation test records what the code actually does, not what it should do. The recipe: call the code with some input, assert something you know is wrong, run the test, and let the failure tell you the real output. Paste that in as the expected value. Repeat with inputs that exercise different branches.

Some characterised values will look wrong. Don't fix them now. Note them, keep the test as it is, and fix the behaviour later as a separate, deliberate change, agreed with whoever owns it. Someone may be depending on the bug. When the output is large, such as a generated report, combine this with the snapshot tests of chapter 10. Approval-testing tools are built for exactly this job.

Getting code into a test harness

Most legacy code resists tests because of one dependency it reaches for directly. Feathers catalogues two dozen dependency-breaking techniques. Three of them cover most cases in Python.

Parameterise the dependency. Add a parameter whose default preserves the old behaviour. Existing callers don't change, and tests can pass something else.

Extract and override. Move the awkward call into its own method, then override that method in a test subclass. It's a crude seam, but it's safe to create and needs no changes to any caller.

Wrap the global. Code that reaches for a module-level singleton, such as settings.FEATURE_X or cache.client, can first be pointed at a small function. That function can then be replaced in tests with monkeypatch. It's a weaker seam than injection, but on the first day it's often the only safe one.

Adding a feature without making it worse

When the code around a change is too tangled to test yet, don't add your new logic to the tangle. Two techniques keep new code clean and tested from its first line.

Sprout. Write the new behaviour as a new, fully tested function, and call it from one line inside the old code.

Wrap. When new behaviour must happen before or after every call to old code, rename the old function and put a new one, with the old name, in front of it.

Scratch refactoring

To understand a baffling piece of code, refactor it aggressively with no intention of keeping the result. Rename everything, extract functions, delete whatever looks dead. You'll learn how it works. Then throw the changes away with git restore . and do it properly, with tests.

Replacing a whole system: the strangler fig

In 2004 Martin Fowler named this pattern after the strangler figs he saw in Queensland, which grow around a host tree until they can stand on their own. Put a routing layer in front of the old system. Build new functionality, or rebuild old functionality, as a new system behind the same router. Move routes across one at a time. Eventually the old system handles nothing and can be switched off.

Inside a single codebase, the same move is called branch by abstraction. Introduce an interface in front of the old component, make every caller use it, build the new implementation behind it, switch over, and delete the old one. Both approaches let you migrate in production, in small, reversible steps.

Large changes: the Mikado method

For a change too big to make in one step, try it naively and note what breaks. Each breakage is a prerequisite. Revert, then attempt each prerequisite the same way, building a graph of what depends on what. When you reach changes that succeed on their own, make them, commit, and work back up the graph. Ola Ellnestam and Daniel Brolund named it after the pick-up-sticks game, where you remove sticks from the top of the pile without disturbing the rest.

The rewrite temptation

Faced with a large legacy system, most engineers want to start again. Joel Spolsky's 2000 essay Things You Should Never Do made the case against, using Netscape's decision to rewrite its browser from scratch: years passed with nothing shipped while competitors moved on. Old code is full of fixes for bugs nobody remembers, and a rewrite rediscovers them one production incident at a time. Rewrites do occasionally succeed, usually when the scope is small or the platform underneath is truly dead. The default should be incremental replacement.

Common traps
  • Fixing bugs while characterising. You will find odd behaviour. Record it; don't fix it mid-harness. Change one thing at a time.
  • Large untested edits. Every dependency-breaking edit happens without tests, by definition. Keep them tiny.
  • Strangling without a finish line. A migration that stalls at sixty percent leaves you maintaining two systems forever. Plan the last route, and the deletion, from the start.
  • Removing fences. Code that looks pointless in a legacy system is often a fix for something you haven't met yet. Find out why before you delete it.

Chapter 13

Code review

9 min read3 exercises

Review's biggest payoff is shared understanding, not bug-catching. Review to keep the codebase healthy and to teach, keep changes small enough to review properly, and remember that there's a person on the other side of every comment.

What review actually does

Ask engineers why they review code and most will say: to find bugs. In 2013 Alberto Bacchelli and Christian Bird studied review at Microsoft, observing and interviewing developers and classifying hundreds of real review comments. Finding defects was the most common stated motivation, yet reviews turned up fewer defects than people expected. Their main outcomes were knowledge transfer, team awareness, and alternative solutions to problems.

That changes how you should review. A review that only hunts for bugs misses its most valuable output: two people who now understand this code instead of one, and a reviewer who learned a technique or a corner of the system they didn't know.

The standard

Google's published engineering practices set the bar clearly: approve a change once it definitely improves the overall health of the codebase, even if it isn't perfect. There's no perfect code, only better code. A reviewer who blocks for polish slows the whole team down. One who approves anything lets the codebase decay a change at a time. The balance is to hold the line on design and correctness, and to let personal preferences go.

What to look for, most important first

  1. Design. Do the pieces make sense together? Does this belong here, in a library, or nowhere?
  2. Functionality. Does it do what the author intended, and is that good for users? Think about edge cases and concurrency, which tests often miss.
  3. Complexity. Could it be simpler? Is anything built for a future that may never arrive?
  4. Tests. Are there tests, do they test behaviour, and would they fail if the code broke?
  5. Naming. Do the names say what things are?
  6. Comments. Do they explain why, and are they needed at all?
  7. Style and consistency. Mostly the formatter's job. Anything a tool didn't catch is a nit.
  8. Documentation. Did the user-facing or developer docs change along with the behaviour?
  9. Context. Look at the whole file, not just the diff. Is this change making the file worse?
  10. Good things. Say so when you see them. Review is also how people learn what good looks like.

This list is adapted from Google's. Its unwritten rule matters as much as any item: read every line you were asked to review, and if you can't understand something, say so. Code the reviewer can't follow is code the next maintainer can't follow either.

Size is the variable that matters most

A study of code review at Cisco, covering 2,500 reviews, found that reviewers' ability to find defects fell sharply beyond about 200 to 400 lines at a time, and after 60 to 90 minutes of continuous reviewing. The 2025 DORA report found the other half of the problem: AI assistance is making pull requests larger, working against the small-batch discipline its own research links to better delivery.

Big changes don't get reviewed. They get skimmed and approved. If you want real review, send small changes. Split refactoring from behaviour. Land the database migration separately. Put new code behind a flag and merge it in slices.

As the author

  • Review your own diff first, in the review tool, the way the reviewer will see it. You'll catch the debug print, the stray file and the confusing name before anyone else has to.
  • Keep it small and single-purpose. One logical change per pull request.
  • Explain the why. The diff shows what changed. The description must say why, how you tested it, and where reviewers should look hardest.
  • Separate refactors from behaviour changes. Reviewers can skim a pure rename. They must study a logic change. Don't make them do both at once.
  • Respond to every comment. Change it, or explain why not. "Done" is a perfectly good reply.
  • Your code is not you. A comment about the code isn't a verdict on your competence.

As the reviewer

  • Respond quickly. Slow review is a major cause of large pull requests: if review takes three days, people batch their work to avoid waiting. Google's guidance is to respond within one business day at most.
  • Big picture first. If the design is wrong, line comments are wasted effort. Say so early, and ideally in conversation.
  • Ask, don't command. "What happens if the list is empty?" invites the author to find the bug. "This crashes on an empty list" is fine when you're sure. "Fix this" teaches nothing.
  • Label severity. Make clear what blocks approval and what's optional. The Conventional Comments convention gives you labels such as issue, suggestion, question, nit and praise.
  • Explain why, and offer a way forward. A reason turns a preference into a lesson.
  • Automate the arguments. Formatting, import order and lint rules belong to tools. Human attention is too expensive to spend on them.

Disagreeing well

Most review disagreements are preference dressed up as principle. Before pushing back, ask whether you have data, a documented standard, or a concrete failure scenario. If you do, state it. If you only have a preference, label it as one, or let it go.

When a thread reaches its third round, stop typing and talk. A five-minute call resolves what twenty comments won't. If you still disagree, agree on who decides, usually the code owner or a tech lead, rather than letting the change stall, and record the outcome in the thread so the next reader sees why.

Reviewing code an assistant wrote

The standard doesn't change; the failure modes do. Generated code is fluent, which makes its errors harder to spot: calls to APIs that don't exist, edge-case handling that looks right and isn't, security checks that are almost correct, and tests that assert whatever the implementation happens to do. Two rules help. The author must understand every line they submit, whoever wrote the first draft. And tests generated alongside code prove little on their own, so check that they encode the intended behaviour, not merely the actual behaviour.

Common traps
  • Rubber-stamping. "LGTM" after two minutes on 800 lines isn't a review. If you can't review properly, say so and suggest someone who can.
  • Nitpick avalanches. Twenty style comments bury the one design concern. Let tooling handle style, and prioritise.
  • Gatekeeping. Using review to enforce personal taste, or to show off, teaches people to avoid sending you reviews.
  • Last-minute redesigns. Proposing a different architecture the day before release, having not been involved earlier. Raise design concerns in design discussions, not at the final gate.

Version control and releasing

7 min read3 exercises

Integrate small changes continuously, make every commit tell the reader why, and separate deploying code from releasing features.

A commit is a message to the future

Months from now, someone will run git blame on a confusing line and read your commit message. It's the only record of why that line is the way it is. The diff already says what changed. The message must say why.

  • One logical change per commit. It builds, it passes the tests, and it can be reverted on its own.
  • A short summary line, around fifty characters, in the imperative mood: "Fix double charge on payment retry", as if completing the sentence "this commit will…".
  • A body that explains why, wrapped at about seventy-two characters. What was wrong, why this approach, and what the alternatives were.

Conventional commits

A lightweight convention that makes history machine-readable: prefix the summary with a type. fix: corresponds to a patch release, feat: to a minor release, and a ! after the type, or a BREAKING CHANGE: footer, to a major one. Release tooling can then generate changelogs and version numbers for you.

Curate history before you share it

Your local history can be messy while you work. Before merging, make it tell a clean story. Git can fold small fixes into the commits they fix:

The rule that keeps this safe: never rewrite history that other people have already pulled. Rebase your own branch; never rebase main.

Deploying is not releasing

Trunk-based development raises an obvious question: how do you merge unfinished features every day? You merge them switched off. A feature flag lets code reach production dormant, which separates deploying, putting code on servers, from releasing, exposing behaviour to users. Pete Hodgson's taxonomy names four kinds of flag, and the distinction matters because their lifespans differ.

Flags are debt, because each one doubles the paths through the code it guards. On 1 August 2012, Knight Capital deployed new trading code that reused an old flag. One of its eight servers hadn't received the new code, and on that server the flag reactivated logic that had been dead for years. In about forty-five minutes the firm lost roughly $440 million. Remove release flags as part of finishing a feature, and never reuse a flag's name.

Versioning what others depend on

If other people depend on your code, as a library, an API or an event schema, version it. Semantic Versioning's rules are short: in MAJOR.MINOR.PATCH, increment PATCH for backward-compatible bug fixes, MINOR for backward-compatible features, and MAJOR for anything that breaks existing users. Versions below 1.0.0 promise nothing.

The hard part is knowing what counts as breaking. Hyrum Wright, a Google engineer, observed that with enough users, every observable behaviour of a system will be depended on by somebody, whatever the documentation promises: field order, the wording of error messages, timing, exactly how rounding works. So "backward compatible" really means compatible with what people actually do, and you often only find out by changing it. Deprecate before removing: announce, warn in logs or responses, provide a migration path and a date, and then remove in the next major version.

Changelogs

A changelog is for people, not machines. It lists what changed that a user would care about, newest first, grouped under Added, Changed, Deprecated, Removed, Fixed and Security, as in the widely used Keep a Changelog convention. A changelog generated from conventional commits is a good start, and deserves a human edit before release.

Common traps
  • "Merge branch main into feature-x", fifteen times over. Rebase your branch onto main instead, or squash on merge.
  • Force-pushing shared branches. Use --force-with-lease if you must, and never on main.
  • Flag graveyards. Give every release flag a removal ticket on the day it's created.
  • Breaking changes in minor versions. It's the quickest way to teach users to pin every dependency forever.

When things go wrong

Every system fails. These three chapters cover finding the cause when it does, deciding how failure is handled before it happens, and the discipline of measuring before you optimise.

Chapter 15

Systematic debugging

7 min read3 exercises

Debugging is the scientific method applied to a program: observe, hypothesise, predict, test, narrow. Changing code at random until the symptom disappears isn't debugging. It's moving the bug somewhere you haven't looked yet.

Where bugs live

Every bug lives in the gap between what you believe the code does and what it actually does. So the first move is always to distrust a belief. "That function can't return None." "The config is definitely loaded." "This branch is never reached." The belief you're most sure of is usually the one you haven't checked.

The loop

  1. Reproduce it reliably, ideally as a failing automated test. If you can't reproduce it, you can't know you've fixed it.
  2. Read the evidence. The whole error message, the whole stack trace, the logs around it. The answer is in the error message more often than people expect.
  3. Form a hypothesis, specific and falsifiable: "the discount is applied twice because the handler runs in both the web and the worker process".
  4. Predict. If the hypothesis is true, what else must be true? What would you see if you looked?
  5. Test the prediction with the cheapest experiment available: a log line, a breakpoint, a one-line change.
  6. Narrow, and repeat. Every experiment should cut the search space.
  7. Fix the cause, not the symptom.
  8. Keep the reproduction as a regression test.
  9. Look for siblings. The same mistake is often made in more than one place.

Binary search, everywhere

When you can't reason your way to the bug, halve the search space. It works along three axes.

Across history. If it worked last month, the bug is in a commit since then, and git bisect will find which one. Given a script, it runs unattended.

Across a thousand commits, that takes about ten test runs.

Across input. A 40,000-line CSV crashes the importer. Delete the second half. Still crashing? Keep halving. In a few minutes you have the one line that matters. This is delta debugging, the same shrinking that Hypothesis does automatically.

Across code. Add a checkpoint halfway along the suspect path: is the data already wrong at this point? The first place it's wrong is where to look.

Use a debugger

Print debugging works, and in production, logs may be all you have. Locally, though, a debugger shows the whole state at once, and lets you ask questions you didn't think of in advance.

When something crashes, inspect the moment it died:

Inside pdb, p expr prints a value, n steps over a line, s steps into a call, u and d move up and down the stack, and w shows where you are. A conditional breakpoint, such as b invoices.py:88, total < 0, stops only when the interesting case happens.

Check the boring things first

A surprising share of debugging time is spent on a program that isn't the one you think is running.

  • Is the code you're editing the code that's running? Stale builds, cached bytecode, the wrong virtual environment, a container image that wasn't rebuilt.
  • Is it the environment you think? Staging or production, which config file, which feature flags.
  • What changed? Deploys, dependency updates, configuration, data, traffic. Diff the working environment against the broken one.
  • Is the input what you think? Print it exactly, with repr(), so you can see the trailing space and the invisible character.

Explain it out loud

The Pragmatic Programmer popularised rubber-duck debugging: explain the problem, line by line, to a rubber duck. It works because explaining forces you to state every assumption, and one of them is wrong. A colleague works even better, and often doesn't need to say a word.

The minimal reproduction

Shrink the failing case until removing anything makes the bug disappear. The bug often becomes obvious along the way. If it doesn't, you now have the perfect bug report: something another person can run in thirty seconds. An issue with a minimal reproduction is far more likely to be fixed quickly than one without.

Symptom or cause?

A null check that stops a crash is often a fix for the symptom. The value shouldn't have been null, and whatever produced it upstream is still wrong. Keep asking why until you reach a cause you can fix at its source. The "five whys" of Toyota's production system is a structured version of this habit. Don't stop at the first answer that makes the error go away.

Common traps
  • Shotgun debugging. Changing several things at once until it works. You won't know which change fixed it, and the others may have broken something new.
  • Fixing without reproducing. "I think this should fix it" ships a guess.
  • Stopping at the symptom. The crash is gone; the corrupt data upstream is not.
  • Heroic all-nighters. After an hour stuck, take a break or ask someone. Fresh eyes are the cheapest debugging tool there is.
  • Heisenbugs. Bugs that vanish when observed, usually from timing or concurrency. Adding a print changes the timing. Use timestamped logging, or tools built for races, rather than breakpoints.

Chapter 16

Error handling

8 min read3 exercises

Decide deliberately where each failure is detected, where it's handled, and what the user and the operator each get to see. Most error-handling bugs come from never having made those decisions.

Three kinds of failure

KindExampleRight responseWho needs to know
Programmer errorNone where a value must exist; an impossible stateFail fast and loudly. Don't catch it; fix the codeDevelopers, through the crash and its stack trace
Operational errorTimeout, connection refused, disk full, rate limitedExpected. Retry if transient and safe; degrade; reportOperators, through logs and metrics
User errorInvalid email, expired card, unknown couponValidate at the boundary; return a specific, helpful messageThe user, in words they understand

Handling all three the same way, usually with one broad except, is the root of most error-handling mistakes.

Fail fast on bugs

When a program is in a state its author said was impossible, the safest thing is to stop. Carrying on means corrupting data, or acting on it in ways nobody intended. Checks at a function's entry catch bugs close to their cause, where they're cheap to diagnose. The most dangerous lines in Python look like this:

Someone will discover, weeks later, that inventory hasn't synced since March.

Handle errors where you have context

Catch an exception only where you can do something meaningful about it: retry, fall back, translate it into a message for the user, or add context and re-raise. Everywhere else, let it propagate. Low-level code usually can't handle anything; the request handler at the top usually can.

When an exception crosses a layer boundary, translate it into the vocabulary of the layer above, and chain the original so nothing is lost.

Callers catch CardDeclined to show the customer a message, PaymentProviderUnavailable to retry or queue, or PaymentError for both. Nobody outside this module knows which SDK sits underneath, and from e keeps the original traceback attached for whoever debugs it.

Parse, don't validate

Alexis King's phrase, from a 2019 essay, for a pattern that removes a whole category of bug. A validator checks input and returns a boolean, and the rest of the program has to trust that someone called it. A parser checks input and returns a new type that can only exist if the input was valid. Downstream code takes that type, so it can't receive unchecked data.

Parse at the edge of the system, as early as possible, and let the types carry the proof inwards. The EmailAddress of chapter 11, and Money, are the same pattern.

Retries, done safely

Retrying is the right response to transient failures and the wrong response to everything else. Four rules:

  • Retry only what's transient. Timeouts, 503 responses, connection resets. Never a validation error or a declined card, which will fail the same way every time.
  • Retry only what's safe to repeat. Reads are safe. A payment is not, unless the provider supports an idempotency key and you send the same key on every attempt.
  • Back off exponentially, with jitter. Retrying immediately hammers a service that's already struggling, and if thousands of clients back off on the same schedule, they come back in synchronised waves. Marc Brooker's analysis for AWS showed that randomising the delay, "full jitter", spreads them out.
  • Retry at one layer, with a cap. If the client, the service and the database driver each retry three times, one user request can become twenty-seven attempts on the database. This is retry amplification.

Every network call needs a timeout

A call without a timeout can wait forever, and a worker waiting forever holds its resources forever. Several popular HTTP clients default to no timeout at all, including the widely used requests. Set one explicitly on every call, sized to how long the caller can genuinely afford to wait.

Logging errors

  • Log once, where you handle it. Logging and re-raising at every layer produces five copies of one error, and nobody can tell whether it was one incident or five.
  • Log structured context. An order id, a user id and a request id turn a log line into something you can search. log.error("charge failed", extra={"order_id": order.id}) beats string formatting.
  • Never log secrets or personal data. Card numbers, passwords, tokens, and often email addresses. Logs are copied to more places, with weaker controls, than your database.
  • Make levels mean something. Error: a person should look at this. Warning: unexpected but handled, worth watching in aggregate. Info: business events. Debug: detail for diagnosis, normally off in production.

What the user sees

Users need to know what happened, in their terms, and what they can do next. They don't need a stack trace, which is also an information leak to anyone probing your system. Give them a reference they can quote to support, and log the same reference with full detail, so a support ticket leads straight to the log entry.

Design errors away, again

Chapter 4's advice applies here with extra force. Every error case you design away is one nobody has to handle: idempotent deletes, defaults for missing optional values, operations that clamp instead of raising. Keep exceptions for the situations a caller genuinely needs to know about.

Common traps
  • Catching Exception mid-stack. It catches your bugs too. Catch the specific exceptions you can actually handle.
  • Returning None or -1 for failure. Callers forget to check, and the failure surfaces later, somewhere unrelated.
  • Retrying non-idempotent operations. Double charges, duplicate emails, repeated shipments.
  • Silent background jobs. A job that fails quietly is worse than one that crashes, because a crash at least gets noticed.
  • Messages that blame. "Invalid input" helps nobody. "Postcode must be 5 digits; you entered 4" tells the user how to fix it.

Chapter 17

Performance: measure first

6 min read3 exercises

Most code is fast enough. When it isn't, find the slow part by measuring, fix that part, and measure again. Intuition about where time goes is wrong often enough that it isn't worth consulting.

The whole quotation

"Premature optimization is the root of all evil" is one of the most quoted lines in programming, nearly always without its context. Donald Knuth wrote it in 1974, and the same passage goes on to say that we shouldn't pass up our opportunities in the critical three percent. His point wasn't that performance doesn't matter. It was that performance matters in a small part of a program, and you can't guess which part.

Decide what "fast enough" means

Before optimising, set a target in terms users experience: "search returns in under 300 ms for 95% of requests", not "make search faster". Without a target, you don't know when to stop.

Measure percentiles, not averages. If 99 requests take 50 ms and one takes 5 seconds, the average is about 100 ms, which describes nobody's experience. The 99th percentile of 5 seconds describes the user who gave up. At scale the slow tail is hit constantly, because a page that makes twenty backend calls is slowed down by whichever one hits its tail.

Profile before you touch anything

A profiler tells you where time actually goes. In Python:

cProfile ships with Python and counts every call, which adds overhead but gives exact numbers. py-spy samples a running program from the outside with little overhead, so it's safe to point at production. In a flame graph, width is time: look for the widest bars in code you own.

For a small piece of code, benchmark with timeit, which repeats the measurement to average out noise.

Amdahl's law

Speeding up part of a program only helps in proportion to that part's share of the total. If a request spends 90% of its time waiting on the database, making your Python ten times faster saves at most 9%. That's why profiling comes first: it tells you which part is worth speeding up at all.

Where the big wins usually are

Roughly in order of how often they matter in application code:

  1. Round trips. The N+1 query: fetch a list, then query once for each item. Each query is fast; a thousand of them aren't. Batch them.
  2. Algorithmic complexity. A list lookup inside a loop is quadratic. A set or a dict makes it linear.
  3. Doing less work. Paginate, filter in the database rather than in Python, compute lazily, and skip what the caller doesn't use.
  4. Caching. Powerful, and it brings invalidation, staleness and memory problems with it. Cache only what you've measured to be slow and repeated.
  5. Concurrency for waiting. If time goes on waiting for the network, overlapping the waits helps. If it goes on computation, threads won't help in standard CPython; you need processes or native code.
  6. Micro-optimisations. Caching attribute lookups in hot loops and the like. Real, small, and almost never the answer.

SQLAlchemy's equivalents are joinedload and selectinload. Turn on query logging in development and count the queries each request makes. The N+1 is one of the most common performance bugs in web applications, and one of the easiest to fix once seen.

With 10,000 customers and 100,000 orders, the first version performs billions of operations. The second performs about 110,000.

Keep optimised code readable

Optimised code is often less obvious. Leave a why comment with the measurement that justified it, so the next reader doesn't "simplify" it back. Ideally, keep a benchmark in the repository, so a regression shows up in CI rather than in production.

Common traps
  • Optimising without a profile. You'll speed up the part that wasn't slow.
  • Benchmarking badly. One run, a cold cache, a laptop on battery, a debug build. Repeat, warm up, and compare against a baseline on the same machine.
  • Trusting averages. Look at the 95th and 99th percentiles.
  • Caching first. A cache hides a slow path without fixing it, and adds a consistency problem.
  • Optimising code that runs once. A migration that takes four minutes instead of two doesn't matter.

Chapter 18

Technical writing

8 min read3 exercises

Writing is how an engineering decision reaches people who weren't in the room, including your future self. Write for a specific reader who has less context than you, and put the conclusion first.

Principles

  • Know your reader, and what they'll do next. A design doc for a sceptical senior engineer and a README for someone on their first day need different things. Name the reader before you start.
  • Lead with the conclusion. Put the decision, recommendation or answer in the first paragraph. Busy readers stop early, so make sure they stop knowing the important part.
  • One idea per paragraph, carried by its first sentence. Someone skimming only first sentences should still get the argument.
  • Concrete beats abstract. "Cuts p99 checkout latency from 1.8 s to 400 ms" beats "improves performance".
  • Show, with examples. One example of an API call explains more than a paragraph describing it.
  • Cut. Most first drafts improve by losing a third. Delete the throat-clearing, the hedges and the repetition.

Four kinds of documentation

Daniele Procida's Diátaxis framework observes that documentation serves four different needs, and that most bad documentation mixes them. A tutorial that pauses to recount design history loses its learner. A reference page that tells stories is useless for looking things up.

The README

A README's job is to take a stranger from nothing to productive. The test: can a new engineer get from cloning the repository to a running, tested build using only the README, in under fifteen minutes?

Design documents

Write a design doc when getting something wrong would be expensive, or when several people need to agree. Its value lies less in the document than in the thinking it forces, and in the review it invites while changing course is still cheap. Google's practice, as described by Malte Ubl, suggests a structure like this:

  • Context and scope. What exists today, and what this document covers.
  • Goals and non-goals. Non-goals are things that could reasonably be goals but that you're choosing not to pursue. "Supporting multiple currencies" is a useful non-goal. "Not crashing" is not.
  • The design. An overview first, then the details that matter: interfaces, the data model, the flows that are hard.
  • Alternatives considered. The section that earns the document its keep. Each alternative, including doing nothing, and the trade-off that ruled it out.
  • Cross-cutting concerns. Security, privacy, observability, migration, cost.
  • Open questions. What you haven't decided yet, stated plainly.

Keep it as short as the decision allows. A design doc nobody finishes reading has failed at its only job.

Architecture decision records

An ADR records one decision, briefly, in the repository, where the code's future readers will find it. Michael Nygard proposed the format in 2011: a title, a status, the context that forced a choice, the decision itself, and its consequences, good and bad. ADRs are never edited after acceptance. When a decision changes, a new ADR supersedes the old one, so the history of thinking survives.

Runbooks and postmortems

A runbook is a how-to guide for someone under stress at three in the morning. Make every step a command they can copy, say what they should see, and say what to do if they don't see it.

A postmortem is written after an incident: a timeline, the impact, the contributing causes, what went well, and action items with owners. Write it blamelessly. The useful question is what about the system let a reasonable person make that mistake, because that's the only question whose answer prevents the next one.

Writing, and assistants

An assistant can produce a fluent design doc in seconds, and it will read well. What it can't do is make the decisions the document exists to record, or know your system's real constraints. Much of the value of writing is that it exposes gaps in your own thinking, and a generated draft skips that step unless you interrogate every claim in it. Use tools to tighten prose you've reasoned through, not to replace the reasoning.

Common traps
  • Burying the lede. Three paragraphs of background before the recommendation.
  • Writing for yourself. Undefined acronyms, skipped steps, and the word "obviously".
  • Docs that rot. Documentation away from the code, unreviewed and unowned, decays. Keep docs in the repository and update them in the same pull request as the behaviour.
  • Design docs as theatre. A doc written after the code, to satisfy a process, is a description, not a design.

Chapter 19

Estimation, decomposition and scope

7 min read3 exercises

An estimate is a forecast under uncertainty. Make it honest by breaking the work into small pieces, stating your assumptions, giving a range, and cutting scope rather than stretching time when reality disagrees.

Why estimates go wrong

  • The planning fallacy. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky found that people estimate by imagining the task going well. Nothing in that imagined version gets interrupted, blocked, or rewritten.
  • Hofstadter's law. It always takes longer than you expect, even when you take Hofstadter's law into account. Correcting for optimism once isn't enough.
  • Forgotten work. The estimate covers writing the code. It leaves out tests, review, deployment, documentation, the migration, and two days of back-and-forth about what was actually wanted.
  • Unknown unknowns. The integration that turns out to need an API the vendor doesn't have.

The cone of uncertainty

At the start of a project, estimates are genuinely uncertain, often by a factor of four in either direction, according to Barry Boehm's data as popularised by Steve McConnell. The range narrows as requirements, design and implementation turn unknowns into knowns. So an early estimate should be a wide range, revised as you learn. A single number at the start is false precision.

Techniques that help

  • Decompose until the pieces are small. A task estimated at more than a couple of days is hiding unknowns. Break it down until each piece is about a day. The decomposition is often worth more than the estimate.
  • Turn unknowns into spikes. "Find out whether the payment provider supports partial refunds" is a time-boxed task whose deliverable is an answer. Estimate the rest once you have it.
  • Give ranges. Three-point estimation asks for optimistic, likely and pessimistic values. The PERT formula weights them as (O + 4M + P) ÷ 6, but the most useful part is having said the pessimistic number out loud.
  • Take the outside view. Kahneman's remedy for the planning fallacy is reference class forecasting: instead of imagining this task, ask how long similar tasks actually took. Record your estimates and actuals; your own history is the best reference class you'll ever have.
  • Separate estimates, targets and commitments. An estimate is a prediction, a target is a business wish, and a commitment is a promise. McConnell's point is that they're different things, and much misery comes from a target being heard as an estimate, or an estimate as a commitment.

Slice vertically

Split work into vertical slices, thin pieces of end-to-end functionality a user could see, rather than horizontal layers like "all the database work, then all the API, then all the interface". Layers deliver nothing until the last one lands, and integration problems appear at the end, when there's no time left. Slices deliver something at every step, and surface integration problems in the very first one. The Pragmatic Programmer calls that first slice a tracer bullet: a skeleton that goes all the way through, so you can see whether you're on target.

Take a request to let customers export their order history as a CSV file.

SliceWhat shipsEstimate
1. Tracer bulletA button that downloads a CSV of order ids and dates for the logged-in customer. End to end, and ugly.0.5–1 day
2. All the columnsTotals, items and status, with money formatted properly.0.5 day
3. Date filterExport a chosen date range. Needs a date picker in the interface.1–2 days
4. Large historiesCustomers with 10,000+ orders time out, so generate the file in a background job and email a link.2–4 days
5. PolishAccessible controls and localised column headers.1 day

After slice 1 there's something to show. After slice 2 most customers are served. Slice 4 has the widest range because it's the least known. If time runs out, slices 3 to 5 become a conversation about scope rather than a missed deadline.

Negotiate scope, not quality

When the estimate exceeds the time available, there are four levers: time, scope, people and quality. Fred Brooks observed that adding people to a late software project makes it later. Cutting quality, by skipping tests or review, borrows against the future at a punishing rate. That leaves time and scope, so offer options rather than a yes or no.

Report progress honestly

  • Report in finished slices, not percentages. "Two of five slices are in production" can be checked. "About 80% done" can't, and Tom Cargill's ninety-ninety rule applies: the first 90% of the code takes the first 90% of the time, and the remaining 10% takes the other 90%.
  • Raise risks early. The day you learn an estimate is wrong is the day to say so. A slip announced three weeks ahead is a planning input. Announced the day before, it's a crisis.
  • Re-estimate as you learn. A forecast that never changes isn't a forecast.

A note on #NoEstimates

Some teams stop estimating individual items altogether. If work is sliced into small pieces of similar size, you can forecast from throughput: we finish about eight slices a week, this feature is about twenty-four slices, so roughly three weeks. It works well for teams with steady flow, and it rests on the same underlying skill as good estimation: decomposing well.

Common traps
  • Giving a number on the spot. "Let me break it down and come back to you this afternoon" is a professional answer.
  • Padding silently. Hidden buffers erode trust when they're discovered. Express uncertainty openly, as a range.
  • Estimating only the coding. Include testing, review, deployment, migration and documentation, or say explicitly that you haven't.
  • Horizontal slicing. It feels efficient and delivers nothing until the very end.
  • Accepting a deadline as an estimate. If someone hands you a date and asks you to confirm it, you've been asked for a commitment. Say which one you're giving.

You estimate a data migration at optimistic 2 days, most likely 4, and pessimistic 12, because nobody has ever fully inspected the legacy data. What's the PERT estimate, and what do you actually tell your manager?

(2 + 4 × 4 + 12) ÷ 6 = 30 ÷ 6 = 5 days. But the useful message is about the skew:

Probably four or five days, but it could take up to two and a half weeks,

The spike is the real recommendation. The formula only tells you that the range is lopsided.

The nineteen rules

If you forget everything else, keep these: one per chapter, each linked back to it. They make a decent checklist for your own pull requests.

  1. Name things for what they mean, in the domain's own words, with units.
  2. One thing per function, at one level of abstraction, with effects pushed to the edges.
  3. Comment the why and the interface. Delete the narration.
  4. Hide a lot behind a little, and design errors out of existence.
  5. Apply a principle only when you can name the change it makes cheaper.
  6. Compose behaviour and inject dependencies, including time and randomness.
  7. Use a pattern when you have its problem, in its lightest form.
  8. Test behaviour through public interfaces, at the boundaries, deterministically.
  9. Fake what's slow or effectful, and keep fakes honest with contract tests.
  10. Test first for feedback, state properties for the cases you can't imagine, and test your tests.
  11. Refactor in small green steps, wearing one hat at a time.
  12. Characterise before changing. Sprout and wrap. Strangle, don't rewrite.
  13. Keep changes small, review design first, and label what blocks.
  14. Integrate daily, explain why in every commit, and deploy dark.
  15. Reproduce, hypothesise, bisect, and keep the reproduction as a test.
  16. Fail fast on bugs, retry only the transient and idempotent, and time out everything.
  17. Set a percentile target, profile first, and fix the widest bar.
  18. Lead with the conclusion, for a named reader, and record decisions beside the code.
  19. Estimate in ranges, slice vertically, and trade scope, never quality.
